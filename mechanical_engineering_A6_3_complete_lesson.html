Section 01

The engineering problem: controlling movement

A precision packaging machine has a guided platform connected to a long metal tie rod. The rod safely carries the load according to an average-stress check, yet the platform shifts farther than its alignment tolerance allows. Nothing has broken, but the machine no longer operates accurately. This is a deformation problem: a component may be strong enough to avoid failure while still being too flexible for its job.

Mechanical engineers evaluate both strength and stiffness. Strength concerns whether a material reaches a failure criterion such as yielding. Stiffness concerns how much a part elastically changes shape under load. The two requirements must be checked separately. In this lesson, you will calculate axial strain, interpret elastic modulus, predict length changes, and compare practical design options for a machine component.

Section 02

What is engineering strain?

When an axial load stretches a straight rod from its original length L₀ to a new length L, the change in length is ΔL = L − L₀. Engineering strain is the change in length divided by the original length: ε = ΔL/L₀. Strain is dimensionless because both lengths use the same unit. It is often written as a decimal or percentage. For example, 0.001 strain is 0.1% strain.

For a rod originally 1,000 mm long that extends by 1.5 mm, ε = 1.5/1,000 = 0.0015, or 0.15%. Under the usual sign convention, extension is positive and shortening is negative. Engineers may compare the magnitude when evaluating a specified deformation limit.

Strain tells an engineer the relative deformation, not the absolute movement. A 1 mm extension in a 100 mm part represents a much greater strain than the same 1 mm extension in a 2,000 mm part.

Section 03

Elastic versus permanent deformation

Elastic deformation is reversible: when a load is removed, the material returns approximately to its original shape. Plastic deformation is permanent: after unloading, some change in shape remains. A machine component that yields may no longer fit its assembly even if it has not fractured.

For many common engineering metals under small loads, stress and strain are approximately proportional in the linear elastic region. This relationship is a useful first approximation for sizing rods and supports. It is not universal: rubberlike polymers may have strongly nonlinear behavior, and temperature, time, and loading history can change material response.

When a machine needs repeatable precision, engineers normally design to stay within the appropriate elastic range and within the machine's movement tolerance. The maximum permitted displacement can be more restrictive than the material's strength limit.

Section 04

Young's modulus and Hooke's law

Young's modulus, also called the elastic modulus E, describes the slope of the initial linear elastic portion of an axial stress–strain curve. In that region, σ = Eε, or E = σ/ε. Because strain is dimensionless, E has units of stress, usually MPa or GPa. A larger E means less elastic strain for the same axial stress, assuming the same loading direction and conditions.

Typical approximate reference values for classroom calculations are steel E ≈ 200 GPa, aluminum alloy E ≈ 70 GPa, and some engineering polymers E ≈ 2–4 GPa. These are illustrative values; actual grades and temperatures matter. Remember that 1 GPa = 1,000 MPa.

Worked example: A metal specimen experiences 100 MPa stress and 0.0005 elastic strain. E = 100/0.0005 = 200,000 MPa = 200 GPa. The number represents elastic stiffness, not yield strength or the load the part can safely carry.

Section 05

Reading a stress–strain data table

Engineers often receive measurements from a tensile test as a table before creating a graph. To estimate E from data in a linear elastic region, select two points and calculate the slope: E ≈ (σ₂ − σ₁)/(ε₂ − ε₁). Use two points from the initial straight-line portion, not points after yielding.

00Unloaded reference
0.0002550Linear elastic

Using the second and fourth rows, E = (150 − 50)/(0.00075 − 0.00025) = 100/0.00050 = 200,000 MPa = 200 GPa. A common mistake is to divide by the percentage strain written as 0.05 instead of its decimal form 0.0005. Convert percentages to decimals before calculating.

For an actual material, test data may become nonlinear. Do not force a straight-line modulus calculation across a curved or post-yield portion of a curve.

Section 06

From stress to rod elongation

Combine the stress equation from A6.2, σ = F/A, with Hooke's law, ε = σ/E, and the definition ε = ΔL/L₀. Rearranging gives the axial elongation equation ΔL = FL₀/(AE). Here F is axial force, L₀ is original length, A is cross-sectional area, and E is Young's modulus.

Use consistent units. If F is in N, L₀ is in mm, A is in mm², and E is in N/mm² (MPa), then ΔL is in mm. This relationship assumes a straight, uniform member, a centered axial load, and linear elastic behavior. It does not capture bending, connection slip, buckling, or local stress concentrations.

Engineering interpretation: Double the load and elongation doubles. Double the length and elongation doubles. Double the area or elastic modulus and elongation halves, provided the simplified assumptions remain valid.
Section 07

Worked example 1 · A precision lifting rod

A lifting rod carries F = 12,000 N. Its original length is 1,500 mm, its area is 300 mm², and its material has E = 200,000 MPa. The engineering team specifies that elastic extension should be no more than 0.40 mm.

Step 1: Calculate average stress. σ = F/A = 12,000/300 = 40 MPa.

Step 2: Calculate strain. ε = σ/E = 40/200,000 = 0.00020.

Step 3: Calculate elongation. ΔL = εL₀ = 0.00020 × 1,500 = 0.30 mm.

Step 4: Evaluate the requirement. The predicted 0.30 mm extension is below the 0.40 mm tolerance, so the rod passes this simplified deformation check. A separate strength and safety assessment is still required.

Section 08

Worked example 2 · Compare steel and aluminum

A designer is comparing equal-length, equal-area rods subjected to the same axial force. For an illustrative comparison, steel has E = 200 GPa and aluminum has E = 70 GPa. The stress in both rods is the same because F/A is the same. Their elastic strains, however, differ.

At σ = 70 MPa, steel strain is 70/200,000 = 0.00035. Aluminum strain is 70/70,000 = 0.00100. Under these conditions, the aluminum rod stretches about 2.86 times as much as the steel rod. That does not mean aluminum is unsuitable: its lower density may be advantageous, and the designer can increase its area or adjust its geometry.

Mechanical engineers choose between materials by checking both deformation and mass constraints rather than selecting the highest modulus automatically.

Section 09

Worked example 3 · Size for a movement limit

A machine tie rod carries 10,000 N over a length of 1,000 mm. Its elastic modulus is 200,000 MPa. The machine allows no more than 0.25 mm axial elongation. Rearrange ΔL = FL/(AE) to obtain A ≥ FL/(EΔLmax).

A ≥ (10,000 × 1,000)/(200,000 × 0.25) = 200 mm². This is the theoretical minimum area for the elastic-deformation criterion. If the allowable average stress were separately specified as 40 MPa, the stress-based area would be A ≥ 10,000/40 = 250 mm². The engineer must satisfy both checks, so the governing minimum would be 250 mm² before accounting for other design requirements.

Section 10

A design decision: stiffness versus weight

Consider a moving actuator that must remain lightweight but position a tool accurately. A lighter aluminum component may reduce inertia and motor demand, yet its lower modulus can cause more elastic movement than an otherwise identical steel component. The engineer can respond by changing the cross-sectional geometry, shortening the load path, adding bracing, or selecting a different material. Sometimes geometry provides a more efficient solution than simply choosing a heavier material.

When comparing candidate components, document the applied load, relevant area, original length, material modulus, predicted stress, predicted extension, and each design limit. A useful engineering decision is not just “which stretches least?” but “which candidate meets all relevant constraints with reasonable cost and mass?”

Section 12

Independent engineering practice

Complete each problem before opening the worked solution. Show equations, substitutions, units, and an engineering conclusion.

1. A 1,200 mm rod extends 0.6 mm. Find engineering strain.

ε = 0.6/1,200 = 0.0005, or 0.05%.

2. A specimen experiences 120 MPa stress and 0.0006 elastic strain. Find E.

E = 120/0.0006 = 200,000 MPa = 200 GPa.

3. A 2,000 mm rod experiences strain 0.0004. Find extension.

ΔL = εL₀ = 0.0004 × 2,000 = 0.8 mm.

4. A 15,000 N force acts on 500 mm². What is average axial stress?

σ = 15,000/500 = 30 MPa.

5. A steel rod has F = 8,000 N, L = 1,000 mm, A = 200 mm², E = 200,000 MPa. Find extension.

ΔL = 8,000 × 1,000/(200 × 200,000) = 0.20 mm.

6. For problem 5, what happens to extension if area doubles?

Extension halves to 0.10 mm because ΔL is inversely proportional to area.

7. A stress–strain table shows 40 MPa at 0.0002 strain and 120 MPa at 0.0006. Estimate E.

E = (120 − 40)/(0.0006 − 0.0002) = 80/0.0004 = 200,000 MPa = 200 GPa.

8. A component passes a stress limit but exceeds its extension tolerance. Is the design acceptable?

No. The deformation requirement is independent of the stress requirement. Increase area, shorten the member, change geometry, or choose a stiffer material, then verify all other constraints.

Vocabulary glossary

Select a term to expand its definition.

Engineering strain

Change in length divided by original length: ε = ΔL/L₀.

Elongation

Increase in length under loading.

Elastic deformation

Reversible change in shape or length after unloading.

Plastic deformation

Permanent change in shape or length after unloading.

Young's modulus

Slope of the initial linear elastic stress–strain relationship, E = σ/ε.

Hooke's law

In the linear elastic region, stress is proportional to strain: σ = Eε.

Stress–strain curve

Graph of engineering stress versus engineering strain measured during loading.

Linear elastic region

Portion of a stress–strain relationship where stress is approximately proportional to strain.

Stiffness

Resistance of a material or component to elastic deformation.

Displacement tolerance

Maximum permitted movement of a component during operation.

Axial elongation

For a uniform linear elastic member, ΔL = FL/(AE).

Governing constraint

The requirement that demands the more restrictive design choice.

Practice: calculate modulus from a tensile-test data table

Mechanical engineers often receive measured stress and strain values rather than a perfectly labeled graph. To estimate Young's modulus, choose two points within the initial straight-line elastic region and calculate the slope E = Δσ/Δε. Do not use points from the plastic region.

00 MPaInitial elastic
0.000480 MPaInitial elastic
0.0008160 MPaInitial elastic
0.0012210 MPaDeparting from linearity
0.0040250 MPaPlastic deformation

Worked calculation: E = (160 − 80) MPa/(0.0008 − 0.0004) = 80/0.0004 = 200,000 MPa = 200 GPa. Using the last two rows would not give the initial Young's modulus because they are not in the same linear elastic region.

Practice 1 · Estimate E using the first and third rows.

E = (160 − 0)/(0.0008 − 0) = 200,000 MPa = 200 GPa.

Practice 2 · Why not use the 0.0040 strain row?

It is in the plastic region; its secant slope from the origin is not the initial elastic modulus.

Practice 3 · A rod experiences 100 MPa elastic stress with E = 200 GPa. Find its strain.

ε = σ/E = 100/200,000 = 0.0005.

Section 15

Lesson summary

Mechanical engineers must evaluate both stress and deformation. Engineering strain measures relative change in length, ε = ΔL/L₀. In the initial linear elastic region, Young's modulus relates stress and strain through σ = Eε. For a uniform, axially loaded member, predicted elastic extension is ΔL = FL/(AE). These equations let engineers estimate movement, compare materials, and size components for a displacement tolerance.

A part can pass a strength-related stress check but fail a precision-related displacement check. An engineering recommendation should therefore report both, along with the model's limitations. In A6.4, you will investigate yielding, fracture, and other ways materials fail when loading exceeds appropriate limits.
