SECTION 01

Why Unit Conversion Matters

Mechanical engineers routinely receive measurements in different units. A fabrication drawing may show a plate length in millimeters while a formula expects meters. A cart may move at a speed reported in kilometers per hour, while a later force calculation requires meters per second. If the units are not handled correctly, the arithmetic can look reasonable while the result is wrong.

In Lesson A3.1, you learned that a measurement consists of a physical quantity, a numerical value, and a unit. Here, we will learn how to change the unit without changing the physical quantity. For example, a 1.2-meter beam is exactly 1,200 millimeters long. The number changes, but the actual beam does not. The mathematical method for making and checking these changes is called dimensional analysis.

This skill supports nearly every later unit. A force equation may combine kilograms, meters, and seconds. A drawing may require a conversion from centimeters to millimeters. A graph may show speed in kilometers per hour when an experiment requires meters per second. Keeping units attached to each step is an engineering quality check.

SECTION 02

Conversion Factors Are Ratios Equal to One

A conversion factor is a fraction built from two equivalent measurements. Because 1 meter equals 1,000 millimeters, both 1,000 mm / 1 m and 1 m / 1,000 mm are equal to one. Multiplying a measurement by one does not change the physical amount; it only changes how the amount is written.

The direction of the fraction matters. If the starting measurement is in meters and the target is millimeters, put meters in the denominator so they cancel. If the starting measurement is in millimeters and the target is meters, put millimeters in the denominator. The unit you want to remove must appear once above and once below the fraction bar.

1 m = 1,000 mm → conversion factors: (1,000 mm / 1 m) and (1 m / 1,000 mm)

Other useful relationships include 1 km = 1,000 m, 1 cm = 10 mm, 1 kg = 1,000 g, 1 min = 60 s, and 1 h = 3,600 s. These equalities are the starting points for our calculations. A conversion factor must express a true equality; a guessed ratio can invalidate an entire design calculation.

SECTION 03

Convert a Length in Three Connected Steps

Suppose a technical drawing gives a bracket length of 250 mm, but a calculation requires meters. Rather than moving a decimal point without explanation, use three connected steps.

Step 1 — Identify the starting unit and target unit

Starting measurement: 250 mm. Target unit: m. We know 1 m = 1,000 mm.

Step 2 — Set up a conversion factor that cancels the old unit

250 mm × (1 m / 1,000 mm)

The unit mm appears in the numerator of the measurement and denominator of the conversion factor. It cancels, leaving meters.

Step 3 — Calculate and check the result

250 ÷ 1,000 = 0.250 m

The result is sensible: a quarter of a meter is 250 millimeters. To reverse the calculation, multiply 0.250 m by (1,000 mm / 1 m) and recover 250 mm. Reversing the conversion is a useful check.

SECTION 04

Convert Through More Than One Unit

Some conversions require more than one factor. A test cart travels at 18 km/h. To use its speed in an SI calculation, we need meters per second (m/s). Both the distance unit and the time unit must change.

Step 1 — Start with the measurement and identify both changes

We begin with 18 km/h. Kilometers must become meters, and hours must become seconds. Recall that 1 km = 1,000 m and 1 h = 3,600 s.

Step 2 — Arrange two conversion factors so unwanted units cancel

18 km/h × (1,000 m / 1 km) × (1 h / 3,600 s)

The km cancels with km, and h cancels with h. Notice that hours are placed in the numerator of the second factor because the original hours were in the denominator of km/h.

Step 3 — Calculate and verify the final unit

18 × 1,000 ÷ 3,600 = 5 m/s

The cart's speed is 5 m/s. This is not a different speed; it is the same speed written using different units. The remaining unit is m/s, exactly what we intended. A quick sense check helps: 18 km is 18,000 m, and one hour is 3,600 s, so 18,000 ÷ 3,600 = 5 meters per second.

SECTION 05

Converting Area and Volume: Why Powers Matter

Length, area, and volume are different physical quantities. If 1 m = 100 cm, then 1 m² is not 100 cm². A square meter is a square 100 cm long and 100 cm wide, so its area is 100 × 100 = 10,000 cm². The conversion factor must be squared because both dimensions change.

Step 1 — Recognize the power on the unit

For a surface area of 0.25 m², the unit is squared. Therefore the entire length conversion must be squared.

Step 2 — Square the conversion factor

0.25 m² × (100 cm / 1 m)²

Squaring the fraction gives 10,000 cm² per 1 m², and the square-meter units cancel.

Step 3 — Multiply and check

0.25 × 10,000 = 2,500 cm²

For volume, cube the length conversion: 1 m³ = (100 cm)³ = 1,000,000 cm³. Forgetting to square or cube a conversion factor is a common source of large engineering errors.

SECTION 06

Check Equations with Dimensional Consistency

Units are not only useful for conversion. They can also help test whether an equation is structurally reasonable. An equation is dimensionally consistent when the quantities on both sides have the same dimensions. You cannot add a length to a time and obtain a meaningful physical measurement, just as you cannot add 5 meters to 3 seconds.

Consider the basic relationship distance = speed × time for constant speed. The units on the right are (m/s) × s. Seconds cancel, leaving meters, which matches the distance on the left. The equation passes this unit check.

distance = speed × time → m = (m/s) × s = m

Now imagine someone writes distance = speed + time. The right side would add m/s and s. Those are unlike dimensions, so the expression fails the unit check. Dimensional consistency can reveal an incorrect equation, but it cannot prove an equation is physically correct. Two formulas can have matching units while only one correctly describes the system.

SECTION 07

Recognize Common Unit Errors

A frequent mistake is to multiply by a conversion factor upside down. For example, converting 250 mm to meters by multiplying by 1,000 m/mm produces an enormous result and does not represent a valid equality. The correct factor is 1 m / 1,000 mm. Always write the equality first, then orient the fraction so the original unit cancels.

Another mistake is to ignore compound units. Converting km/h to m/s requires changing both distance and time; changing only kilometers to meters leaves m/h, not m/s. A third mistake is using a length factor for area or volume without squaring or cubing it. A fourth is reporting a number without the final unit. The result “5” is incomplete when the calculation asks for speed in m/s.

When checking an answer, ask: Do the units cancel as intended? Does the final unit match the requested quantity? Is the numerical size reasonable? A sound engineering solution should pass all three checks.

SECTION 09

A Complete Engineering Example

A workshop cart moves along a level floor at a constant speed of 3.6 km/h for 25 seconds. How far does it travel in meters? This problem combines a unit conversion with an equation check.

Step 1 — Convert speed to meters per second

3.6 km/h × (1,000 m / 1 km) × (1 h / 3,600 s) = 1 m/s

Step 2 — Choose the equation and check units

For constant speed, distance = speed × time. The units are (m/s) × s = m, which is the required distance unit.

Step 3 — Substitute and calculate

distance = 1 m/s × 25 s = 25 m

The cart travels 25 meters. This answer is plausible: traveling one meter each second for 25 seconds covers 25 meters. Notice that the calculation uses the converted speed, not the original numerical value of 3.6, because that value was in km/h rather than m/s.

UNIT TEST + FINAL EXAM SKILLS

Converting Speed Between km/h and m/s

Mechanical engineers frequently compare speeds reported in kilometers per hour (km/h) with calculations using meters per second (m/s). The units must agree before substituting into formulas. Since 1 km = 1,000 m and 1 hour = 3,600 seconds, converting km/h to m/s means multiplying by 1,000 and dividing by 3,600. Equivalently, divide by 3.6. To go from m/s to km/h, multiply by 3.6.

Worked example — moving conveyor:
  1. Given: Conveyor speed = 54 km/h.
  2. Write the conversion: 54 km/h × (1,000 m / 1 km) × (1 h / 3,600 s).
  3. Cancel units: km and h cancel, leaving m/s.
  4. Calculate: 54 × 1,000 ÷ 3,600 = 15 m/s.
  5. Check: The number becomes smaller because one meter per second corresponds to 3.6 kilometers per hour.
Reverse conversion:
  1. Given: Vehicle speed = 8 m/s.
  2. Multiply: 8 × 3.6 = 28.8 km/h.
  3. Check: 28.8 ÷ 3.6 = 8 m/s.

Independent practice: Convert 72 km/h to m/s and 12.5 m/s to km/h. Then determine how far a machine moves in 6 seconds at 72 km/h.

Show worked solutions

72 ÷ 3.6 = 20 m/s. 12.5 × 3.6 = 45 km/h. Distance = 20 m/s × 6 s = 120 m.

APPLY WHAT YOU LEARNED

Additional Practice: Units as an Equation Check

In engineering, units can help reveal an incorrect operation before a calculator is used. If distance equals speed multiplied by time, the units must cancel to leave a unit of length. For example, meters per second multiplied by seconds gives meters: (m/s) × s = m. Dividing speed by time instead would give m/s², which is acceleration, not distance.

Worked example — conveyor travel:
  1. Write the model: d = vt.
  2. Identify compatible inputs: v = 2.5 m/s and t = 12 s.
  3. Substitute: d = (2.5 m/s)(12 s).
  4. Cancel seconds: (m/s) × s = m.
  5. Calculate: d = 30 m.

Be careful with 3 km/h multiplied by 20 s: the hour and second units do not cancel. Convert km/h to m/s or seconds to hours before multiplying. For example, 3 km/h = 3,000 m / 3,600 s ≈ 0.833 m/s, so in 20 s the distance is about 16.7 m.

Try it: A belt travels 4.5 m in 6 s at steady speed. How far does it move in 24 s?

Show reasoning

Speed = 4.5 ÷ 6 = 0.75 m/s. Distance = 0.75 × 24 = 18 m.

Lesson summary

Dimensional analysis converts measurements by multiplying by conversion factors equal to one, so unwanted units cancel. Area and volume conversions require squared or cubed conversion factors. Units can also be used to check equations: both sides must have compatible dimensions. Before accepting an engineering calculation, verify the conversion direction, resulting units, and whether the numerical answer makes sense.
