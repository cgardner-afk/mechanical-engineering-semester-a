Section 01

Why engineers care about energy of motion

Imagine two test carts rolling down a factory aisle. Both carry the same equipment, but one travels slowly and the other travels much faster. If either cart reaches an obstacle, it must be brought to a stop. The faster cart may require much more energy to be removed during stopping. Engineers need a way to measure that energy before choosing a motor, brake, or protective bumper.

In Unit 7, you studied motion, speed, velocity, and momentum. Momentum depends on mass multiplied by velocity. In this lesson, we will study kinetic energy, the energy an object has because it is moving. Kinetic energy also depends on mass and motion, but its relationship with speed is different. Understanding that difference is especially important when machines move faster.

Words to know before we begin

A test cart is a wheeled platform used to study movement. A payload is the load it carries. A brake is a device that slows a moving machine. Energy is the capacity to cause change or perform work. In mechanical engineering, energy is often transferred from one object or form to another.

Section 02

Connect work and energy

In A8.1, you learned that a force acting through a displacement can transfer energy by doing work. For example, a motor that pushes a cart forward while the cart moves can increase its motion. The motor's work can increase the cart's kinetic energy. A brake or friction force acting opposite the motion does negative work and can reduce kinetic energy.

Work and kinetic energy are both measured in joules (J). This is not an accident: work is one way energy moves into or out of a system. A moving cart possesses kinetic energy even when nobody is currently pushing it. Work describes energy transfer by a force during displacement; kinetic energy describes energy the cart has because it is moving.

Think about the difference

A cart moving at a steady speed still has kinetic energy. If its speed does not change, its kinetic energy does not change. It is possible for several forces to act while their positive and negative work cancel, leaving the kinetic energy unchanged.

Section 03

Mass, speed, and the kinetic-energy equation

Mass describes how much matter an object contains, and engineers measure it in kilograms (kg). Speed describes how fast the object moves, and engineers measure it in meters per second (m/s). For a moving object treated as a single body, kinetic energy is calculated with the equation below.

Kinetic energy = ½ × mass × speed²   →   KE = ½mv²

Here, KE means kinetic energy in joules, m is mass in kilograms, and v is the magnitude of velocity (speed) in meters per second. The small raised 2 means square the speed: multiply the speed by itself. For example, 5² = 5 × 5 = 25. The symbol ½ means divide by two, or multiply by 0.5.

Because speed is squared, reversing the direction of travel does not change kinetic energy. A cart moving east at 4 m/s and the same cart moving west at 4 m/s have the same kinetic energy. Their velocities and momenta have different signs, but their kinetic energies are equal and never negative.

Order of operations matters

For KE = ½mv², square the speed first, multiply by the mass, then divide by two. Do not square the mass or square the entire product. A calculator can evaluate 0.5 × mass × speed × speed.

Section 04

Worked example: calculate kinetic energy step by step

A 240 kg automated cart moves at 5 m/s. How much kinetic energy does it have? We will solve this using the same four-step method used throughout the course: identify the values, select the equation, calculate carefully, and interpret the answer.

Worked example 1 — Cart kinetic energy

Step 1: Write the known values: m = 240 kg and v = 5 m/s.

Step 2: Select KE = ½mv².

Step 3: Square the speed: 5² = 25. Then substitute: KE = 0.5 × 240 × 25 = 3,000 J.

Step 4: Interpret: the moving cart has 3,000 joules of kinetic energy. A stopping system would need to account for the transfer of that energy as the cart comes to rest.

Check the units. Mass is in kilograms and speed is in meters per second, so the calculation produces kg·m²/s². That combination is equivalent to a joule. If a problem gives speed in kilometers per hour instead of meters per second, it must first be converted; the assessment problems in this lesson use meters per second.

Worked example 2 — A heavier vehicle

A 600 kg test vehicle travels at 4 m/s. First square 4: 4² = 16. Next multiply: KE = 0.5 × 600 × 16 = 4,800 J. Notice that the larger mass can produce greater energy even at a lower speed.

Step-by-step worked method · Calculate kinetic energy

  1. Step 1: Read mass m = 240 kg and speed v = 5 m/s.
  2. Step 2: Choose KE = ½mv².
  3. Step 3: Square speed first: 5² = 25.
  4. Step 4: Calculate KE = 0.5 × 240 × 25 = 3,000 J.
  5. Step 5: Interpret as the cart's energy of motion.
Section 05

Why speed has a squared effect

The most important pattern in the kinetic-energy equation is the speed squared. Imagine two identical carts. Cart A moves at 3 m/s, while Cart B moves at 6 m/s. Cart B moves twice as fast. Because 6² = 36 and 3² = 9, Cart B has four times as much kinetic energy, not twice as much.

You can compare speeds without knowing the cart's mass, provided the mass stays the same. Divide the new speed by the original speed to find the speed factor, then square that factor. A speed factor of 2 gives an energy factor of 2² = 4. A speed factor of 3 gives 3² = 9. A speed factor of 4 gives 4² = 16.

Worked example — Tripled speed

A 100 kg cart travels at 2 m/s. Its KE is 0.5 × 100 × 2² = 200 J. The same cart at 6 m/s has KE = 0.5 × 100 × 6² = 1,800 J. Divide 1,800 by 200 to check: 9 times as much energy. Tripling speed multiplies kinetic energy by nine.

This pattern is one reason speed limits matter in industrial facilities. A moderate increase in speed can produce a much larger increase in energy that brakes or protective systems must manage.

Step-by-step worked method · Compare speeds and energy

  1. Step 1: Hold mass constant.
  2. Step 2: Calculate speed ratio: 6/3 = 2.
  3. Step 3: Square the ratio: 2² = 4.
  4. Step 4: Conclude the faster cart has four times the kinetic energy; speed is squared.
Section 06

Why mass has a different effect

Mass appears only once in the kinetic-energy equation; it is not squared. If speed stays constant and mass doubles, kinetic energy also doubles. If mass triples at the same speed, kinetic energy triples. This is a linear relationship between mass and kinetic energy when speed is held constant.

Worked example — Double the payload

A cart with mass 150 kg moves at 4 m/s. KE = 0.5 × 150 × 16 = 1,200 J. Now increase its total mass to 300 kg without changing speed. KE = 0.5 × 300 × 16 = 2,400 J. Doubling mass doubles the kinetic energy.

When both mass and speed change, evaluate both effects. For example, if mass doubles and speed doubles, kinetic energy increases by 2 × 2² = 8 times. This is useful when engineers evaluate loaded and unloaded vehicles operating at different speeds.

Step-by-step worked method · Compare mass and energy

  1. Step 1: Hold speed fixed at 4 m/s.
  2. Step 2: For 150 kg: KE = 0.5 × 150 × 4² = 1,200 J.
  3. Step 3: For 300 kg: KE = 0.5 × 300 × 4² = 2,400 J.
  4. Step 4: Conclude doubling mass doubles KE; mass is not squared.
Section 07

Read a kinetic-energy graph

Engineers often plot calculated or measured values on a graph. A kinetic-energy versus speed graph places speed in m/s on the horizontal axis and kinetic energy in joules on the vertical axis. Each plotted point shows the energy corresponding to a particular speed for the stated mass. Because energy depends on speed squared, the graph curves upward rather than forming a straight line.

To answer a question about the highest plotted speed, first find the rightmost plotted point. Then trace across to the vertical axis to read the kinetic energy. Do not read the speed value as though it were energy, and do not assume the largest number printed on the axis is a plotted measurement.

Values calculated from KE = ½(100)v²: (0, 0), (2, 200), (4, 800), (6, 1,800), (8, 3,200). The curve becomes steeper as speed increases.

Worked graph-reading example

The highest plotted speed is 8 m/s. At that speed, the plotted energy is 3,200 J. We can verify with the equation: 0.5 × 100 × 8² = 3,200 J. At 4 m/s, the energy is 800 J. Doubling speed from 4 to 8 m/s quadruples the energy from 800 to 3,200 J.

Section 09

Common mistakes and a decision checklist

The most common kinetic-energy mistake is forgetting to square speed. A second mistake is squaring the mass instead of speed. A third is assuming that negative velocity creates negative kinetic energy. Because velocity is squared, kinetic energy cannot be negative for an ordinary moving object. A fourth mistake is mixing up the horizontal and vertical axes when reading an energy graph.

Before choosing an answer, ask:

1. Am I calculating energy in joules or comparing two energies? 2. If calculating, do I have mass in kg and speed in m/s? 3. Did I square speed before multiplying? 4. If comparing, did mass stay constant or did speed stay constant? 5. If reading a graph, did I identify the plotted point and read its vertical energy value?

Remember that momentum and kinetic energy are different quantities. Momentum uses p = mv and includes direction; kinetic energy uses KE = ½mv² and does not have a direction. Both are useful to engineers, but they answer different questions.

Assessment-style graph reading: kinetic energy versus speed

Read the horizontal axis first: it gives speed in meters per second. Read the vertical axis next: it gives kinetic energy in joules. For a 4 kg test cart, kinetic energy is KE = ½(4)v² = 2v². The graph curves upward because speed is squared.

Use the formula to read exact values: at 2 m/s, KE = 2(2²) = 8 J; at 4 m/s, KE = 2(4²) = 32 J; at 6 m/s, KE = 2(6²) = 72 J. When speed doubles from 2 to 4 m/s, energy becomes four times as large. A graph question may ask for the value, the shape, or the ratio.

Section 10

Independent engineering practice

Attempt each problem before opening the explanation. Write the equation, substitute the values, calculate, and include the correct unit. For comparisons, explain which variable changes and which stays constant.

1. A 180 kg cart moves at 5 m/s. Find its kinetic energy.

KE = 0.5 × 180 × 5² = 0.5 × 180 × 25 = 2,250 J.

2. A 350 kg cart moves at 4 m/s. Find its kinetic energy.

KE = 0.5 × 350 × 16 = 2,800 J.

3. A cart moves at 3 m/s and then 9 m/s without changing mass. By what factor does kinetic energy change?

Speed factor = 9/3 = 3. Energy factor = 3² = 9 times.

4. Two equal-mass carts travel at 2 m/s and 8 m/s. How many times more KE does the faster cart have?

Speed factor = 8/2 = 4. Energy factor = 4² = 16 times.

5. A 250 kg cart doubles its mass at the same speed. What happens to kinetic energy?

It doubles, because KE is directly proportional to mass when speed stays constant.

6. A cart has 1,800 J of KE. Its speed stays the same while its mass is tripled. Find the new KE.

New KE = 3 × 1,800 = 5,400 J.

7. A cart's speed changes from +6 m/s to −6 m/s. Does its KE change?

No. Both velocities have the same squared value: (+6)² = (−6)² = 36. Direction changes, but kinetic energy does not.

8. For a 100 kg cart, calculate KE at 2 m/s and 6 m/s.

At 2 m/s: 0.5 × 100 × 4 = 200 J. At 6 m/s: 0.5 × 100 × 36 = 1,800 J.

9. On the lesson's graph, what is the KE at the highest plotted speed?

The rightmost plotted point is at 8 m/s. Read its vertical coordinate: 3,200 J.

10. A 300 kg cart moves at 10 m/s. Find its kinetic energy.

KE = 0.5 × 300 × 100 = 15,000 J.

11. If both mass and speed double, by what factor does KE increase?

Mass factor = 2. Speed-squared factor = 2² = 4. Total factor = 2 × 4 = 8 times.

12. Explain why a faster factory cart may require more attention to braking design.

Because KE increases with speed squared, doubling speed gives four times the kinetic energy at the same mass. The braking system must account for the transfer of this greater energy during stopping. The KE equation alone does not specify braking force or stopping distance.

Section 11

Glossary: kinetic-energy vocabulary

Click each vocabulary term to reveal its definition. Use this glossary whenever you encounter unfamiliar language in the lesson.

Energy

The capacity to cause change or perform work; measured in joules (J).

Kinetic energy (KE)

Energy an object has because it is moving. KE = ½mv²; measured in joules.

Mass

The amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms (kg).

Speed

How fast an object moves, without specifying direction; measured in meters per second (m/s).

Velocity

Speed together with direction. Its sign may indicate direction, but kinetic energy uses its squared magnitude.

Joule (J)

The unit of energy and work. One joule equals one newton-meter.

Square (²)

Multiply a number by itself. For example, 4² = 4 × 4 = 16.

Speed factor

The ratio of a new speed to an original speed. Squaring this factor gives the kinetic-energy factor when mass is unchanged.

Linear relationship

A relationship in which doubling one quantity doubles the other, when all other relevant variables are constant.

Quadratic relationship

A relationship involving the square of a quantity, such as kinetic energy and speed when mass is constant.

Work

Energy transferred by a force acting through displacement; measured in joules.

Braking system

Parts of a machine that slow or stop motion by transferring kinetic energy to other forms or locations.

Test cart

A wheeled platform used in engineering investigations of motion and energy.

Payload

The load carried by a vehicle or machine.

Graph axis

A labeled horizontal or vertical scale used to show measurements; in a kinetic-energy graph, speed is often horizontal and energy vertical.

Section 12

Lesson summary and next steps

Kinetic energy is energy of motion. Calculate it with KE = ½mv², using mass in kilograms and speed in meters per second. The answer is measured in joules. When speed stays the same, doubling mass doubles kinetic energy. When mass stays the same, doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy, tripling speed multiplies it by nine, and quadrupling speed multiplies it by sixteen.

Engineers use these relationships to evaluate moving equipment, compare test vehicles, and anticipate energy that must be transferred during stopping. In the next lesson, A8.3, you will learn about gravitational potential energy—energy stored when a load is raised to a height—and connect that idea to lifting systems and ramps.
