SECTION 01

Why engineers calculate moments

When a mechanic loosens a stubborn bolt, a longer wrench often makes the job easier. The mechanic may apply the same force, but moving that force farther from the bolt creates a larger turning effect. Mechanical engineers call this turning effect a moment, also known as torque in many applications. Moments appear in wrenches, door hinges, levers, pedals, robotic arms, and the beams and bridges that we will study later in Unit A5.

A force can make an object accelerate in a straight line, but it can also tend to rotate an object around a point or axis. The pivot is the point about which that rotation is considered. To predict the turning effect, engineers need three pieces of information: the force magnitude, the location where the force acts, and the direction of the force relative to the pivot.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to calculate a moment, rearrange the moment equation, identify clockwise or counterclockwise rotation from a diagram, and use the perpendicular component of an angled force. These are foundational skills for the lever, equilibrium, and structural analysis lessons that follow.

SECTION 02

The moment of a perpendicular force

For a force applied perpendicular to a straight lever, moment magnitude is the force multiplied by the distance from the pivot to the point where the force acts. The force is measured in newtons, the distance in meters, and the resulting moment in newton-meters (N·m). A newton-meter of moment is a turning effect, not a measurement of energy in this context.

M = F × d⊥

Here, M is the moment magnitude, F is force, and d⊥ is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line of action. When the force is perpendicular to the lever, this distance is simply the lever length from the pivot to the applied force. When the force is angled, the perpendicular distance is not necessarily the full lever length.

Worked example 1 · A wrench

A technician applies a downward force of 45 N at the end of a horizontal wrench, 0.30 m from the bolt. The force is perpendicular to the wrench.

  1. Identify the pivot: the bolt at the wrench's left end.
  2. Identify the perpendicular force: F = 45 N.
  3. Identify the lever arm: d⊥ = 0.30 m.
  4. Calculate: M = 45 × 0.30 = 13.5 N·m.

The moment magnitude is 13.5 N·m. Because the wrench extends to the right and the force points downward, the turning tendency is clockwise.

SECTION 03

Read the pivot, force, and distance from a diagram

Engineering drawings often show forces as arrows. The arrow points in the direction of the force, and its label gives the force magnitude. The pivot is marked separately. Before multiplying any numbers, identify the pivot and trace the line of action of the force. A distance printed elsewhere on the drawing may not be the correct moment arm.

Notice that the distance is measured from the pivot to the force's application point along the wrench. Because the force is perpendicular, that length is also the moment arm. In a different drawing, the force may be angled or applied closer to the pivot. The turning effect must then be recalculated using the correct geometry.

SECTION 04

Rearrange the formula to solve for force or distance

Sometimes an engineer knows the moment a mechanism must produce and needs to select a wrench length or determine the required effort. The same equation can be rearranged. For a perpendicular force, F = M/d and d = M/F. Keep the distance in meters before calculating so that the units remain consistent.

F = M ÷ d    |    d = M ÷ F

Worked example 2 · Find the required lever arm

A handle must produce an 18 N·m moment. A worker can comfortably apply a perpendicular force of 60 N. How far from the pivot should the force act?

  1. Start with M = Fd.
  2. Divide by F: d = M/F.
  3. Substitute: d = 18/60 = 0.30 m.
  4. Check: 60 N × 0.30 m = 18 N·m.

Worked example 3 · Find the required force

A technician needs 24 N·m of moment using a 0.40 m wrench. If the force is perpendicular, F = 24/0.40 = 60 N. If the wrench length were doubled to 0.80 m, the same moment would require only 30 N.

SECTION 05

Clockwise and counterclockwise moments

A moment has both magnitude and a sense of rotation. For a horizontal bar pivoting at the left, a downward force on the right produces a clockwise tendency, while an upward force on the right produces a counterclockwise tendency. If the pivot is at the right end instead, a downward force applied on the left tends to rotate the bar counterclockwise. The same downward arrow can therefore cause a different rotation depending on its position relative to the pivot.

In many two-dimensional calculations, engineers choose counterclockwise as positive and clockwise as negative. This is a sign convention, not a law of nature. You may choose the opposite convention if you use it consistently. In this lesson, we will report a moment's magnitude as a positive value and state its rotation direction separately; in Lesson A5.3 we will add signed moments to test rotational equilibrium.

Diagram reasoning

Imagine a bar extending left from a pivot at its right end. A downward 20 N force acts 0.50 m to the left of the pivot. Its moment magnitude is 20 × 0.50 = 10 N·m, and its turning tendency is counterclockwise. Changing the pivot to the left end while keeping the force on the right would reverse the rotation direction.

SECTION 06

Angled forces and the perpendicular component

A force does not have to be perpendicular to create a moment. Suppose a worker pulls a lever at an angle. Only the component of the force perpendicular to the lever produces the turning effect about the pivot. The component parallel to the lever does not create a moment about that pivot, because its line of action passes through the pivot.

When the angle θ is measured between the lever and the force direction, the perpendicular component is F sin θ. The moment magnitude is therefore:

M = F × r × |sin θ|

Here r is the distance along the lever from the pivot to the point of application. The absolute value indicates that the equation gives a magnitude; determine clockwise or counterclockwise direction from the diagram. If θ = 90°, sin 90° = 1, so the entire force contributes. If θ = 0°, sin 0° = 0, so the force produces no turning effect about the pivot.

Worked example 4 · Pulling at 30°

A 50 N force acts at 30° above a horizontal lever, 0.40 m from its pivot. Use sin 30° = 0.5.

  1. Find the perpendicular force: F⊥ = 50 × 0.5 = 25 N.
  2. Multiply by lever length: M = 25 × 0.40 = 10 N·m.
  3. Because the force pulls upward on the right side of a left-hand pivot, the turning tendency is counterclockwise.

A common mistake is to multiply 50 × 0.40 and ignore the angle, giving 20 N·m. That would be correct only if the force were perpendicular.

SECTION 07

Line of action and the shortest distance

The most general way to think about a moment is force times the shortest perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line of action. The line of action is an imaginary straight line extending through the force arrow. This method is equivalent to multiplying the full lever length by the perpendicular component of force.

For example, a 100 N force acts on a lever at a point 0.60 m from the pivot, but at an angle of 30° to the lever. The perpendicular distance is 0.60 sin 30° = 0.30 m. The moment is 100 × 0.30 = 30 N·m. The component method gives the same result: (100 sin 30°) × 0.60 = 50 × 0.60 = 30 N·m.

If a force's line of action passes directly through the pivot, its perpendicular distance is zero and it produces no moment about that pivot. This is true even if the force itself is very large. The location and direction of the force matter as much as its magnitude.

SECTION 08

Predict how changes affect torque

Moments follow simple proportional patterns. With a perpendicular force held constant, doubling the lever arm doubles the moment. With the lever arm held constant, doubling the force doubles the moment. With both doubled, the moment becomes four times as large. But the effect of changing angle is not linear because the perpendicular component depends on sine.

Worked example 5 · Compare designs

Design A uses a 40 N perpendicular force at 0.25 m, producing 10 N·m. Design B uses the same 40 N force at 0.50 m, producing 20 N·m. Design C keeps the 0.50 m lever but applies the 40 N force at 30°, producing 40 × 0.50 × 0.5 = 10 N·m. The longer lever in Design C loses some of its advantage because the force is not perpendicular.

This is why engineers specify both where an operator applies a force and the direction in which it should be applied. A longer handle may reduce the effort needed, but available space, tool strength, and safe operation also affect the design.

SECTION 10

Engineering applications and design judgment

Torque calculations guide tool selection, robotic arm design, hinges, and hand-operated mechanisms. If a bolt requires a specified moment, a longer wrench can reduce the needed hand force. However, engineers must consider whether the wrench or fastener can safely withstand the resulting loads. More moment is not always better: applying too much torque can damage threads or deform parts.

In a robotic arm, a motor near a joint must resist the moment created by the arm and any payload. A heavy payload held far from the joint produces a larger turning effect than the same payload held close to it. The moment concept also prepares us to analyze levers and structural beams. In those systems, several moments may act at once, and the engineer must determine whether they balance.

A moment calculation should always include a clearly identified pivot, a force direction, a correct perpendicular distance, a numerical answer with N·m units, and an explanation of clockwise or counterclockwise tendency when the diagram makes that direction meaningful.

SECTION 11

Common mistakes and how to check your work

One frequent error is using centimeters without converting to meters. A 40 cm lever arm is 0.40 m, not 40 m. Another is multiplying the entire force by the lever length when the force is angled; use F sin θ or the perpendicular moment arm. A third is choosing rotation direction from the arrow alone without locating the pivot. Finally, do not confuse moment magnitude with a signed net moment: magnitude is nonnegative, while a signed moment depends on the chosen clockwise/counterclockwise convention.

Use three quick checks. First, is the force actually perpendicular? Second, is the distance measured from the pivot to the correct line of action? Third, does the answer have units of N·m and a physically reasonable size? If a 50 N force acts 0.20 m from a pivot at 90°, a result of 250 N·m should immediately seem suspicious; the correct result is 10 N·m.

INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Independent practice with worked solutions

Try these problems without using the simulator first. Then open the solution to check the setup, arithmetic, units, and rotation direction.

A · Perpendicular wrench force

A 65 N downward force acts 0.20 m to the right of a pivot on a horizontal wrench. Find the moment magnitude and direction.

Show worked solution

M = 65 × 0.20 = 13 N·m. Downward on the right of the pivot produces a clockwise moment.

B · Find the distance

A 72 N perpendicular force creates a moment of 36 N·m. Find the lever arm.

Show worked solution

d = M/F = 36/72 = 0.50 m.

C · Find the force

A mechanism needs 30 N·m of moment with a perpendicular lever arm of 0.60 m. Find the force.

Show worked solution

F = M/d = 30/0.60 = 50 N.

D · Angled force

A 90 N force acts at 30° to a 0.50 m lever. Use sin 30° = 0.5. Find moment magnitude.

Show worked solution

M = Fr sin θ = 90 × 0.50 × 0.5 = 22.5 N·m.

E · Read a diagram in words

A horizontal lever extends to the left of a pivot at its right end. A downward 35 N force acts 0.40 m from the pivot. Find magnitude and direction.

Show worked solution

M = 35 × 0.40 = 14 N·m counterclockwise. A downward force to the left of the pivot tends to lift the lever's right side.

F · Compare two designs

Design 1 uses a 40 N perpendicular force 0.30 m from a pivot. Design 2 uses the same force at 60° to a 0.40 m lever. Use sin 60° ≈ 0.866. Which creates more moment?

Show worked solution

Design 1: M = 40 × 0.30 = 12 N·m. Design 2: M = 40 × 0.40 × 0.866 ≈ 13.86 N·m. Design 2 creates about 1.86 N·m more moment.

G · No turning effect

A 150 N force acts along a straight lever toward the pivot, 0.75 m from the pivot. What is its moment about the pivot?

Show worked solution

The angle between the lever and the force is 180°, so sin 180° = 0. The line of action passes through the pivot. Moment magnitude is 0 N·m.

VOCABULARY & GLOSSARY

Vocabulary and key concepts

Moment (torque)

The turning effect of a force about a pivot or axis, measured in N·m.

Pivot

The reference point or axis about which a moment is calculated.

Moment arm

The shortest perpendicular distance from the pivot to a force's line of action.

Line of action

An imaginary line extending through the direction of an applied force.

Perpendicular component

The part of a force acting at 90° to the lever, equal to F sin θ when θ is the angle between the force and lever.

Clockwise and counterclockwise

The two possible senses of rotation around a pivot in a two-dimensional diagram.

Sign convention

A consistent choice of positive and negative directions used to calculate combined moments.

TEST PREPARATION · FOLLOW THE METHOD

Step-by-step problem-solving guide: Moments, directions, and angled forces

These are new examples practicing the same skills found on the Unit 5 assessment and Semester A final. Work through the numbered steps in order. For a diagram question, read the printed labels; never measure the drawing with a ruler unless instructed.

Perpendicular moment

Practice-style example: A 65 N force acts 0.40 m from a pivot, perpendicular to a wrench. Find its moment.

  1. Step 1. Read F = 65 N and perpendicular arm d = 0.40 m.
  2. Step 2. We need moment magnitude M in N·m.
  3. Step 3. Choose M = F × d because the force is perpendicular.
  4. Step 4. Substitute M = 65 × 0.40.
  5. Step 5. Multiply: M = 26 N·m.
  6. Step 6. Check: N × m = N·m; a longer arm would increase the moment.
Final answer: 26 N·m

Find the unknown distance

Practice-style example: A 32 N·m moment comes from an 80 N perpendicular force. Find the arm length.

  1. Step 1. Known: M = 32 N·m, F = 80 N.
  2. Step 2. Unknown: distance d in meters.
  3. Step 3. Start M = Fd; divide both sides by F to obtain d = M/F.
  4. Step 4. Substitute d = 32/80.
  5. Step 5. Calculate d = 0.40 m.
  6. Step 6. Check: 80 × 0.40 = 32 N·m.
Final answer: 0.40 m

Angled force and direction

Practice-style example: A 90 N force acts at 30° to a 0.60 m lever. sin 30° = 0.5. Find moment magnitude.

  1. Step 1. Read F = 90 N, d = 0.60 m, angle θ = 30° measured between force and lever.
  2. Step 2. Unknown: moment magnitude.
  3. Step 3. Use M = Fd sin θ; only the perpendicular force component turns the lever.
  4. Step 4. Substitute M = 90 × 0.60 × 0.5.
  5. Step 5. Calculate M = 27 N·m.
  6. Step 6. Check the rotation separately: a downward force at the right of a pivot turns clockwise; a downward force at the left turns counterclockwise.
Final answer: 27 N·m
Try it yourself — reveal the full solution

Question: A 110 N force acts perpendicularly 0.25 m from a pivot. Find the moment.

Solution: M = Fd = 110 × 0.25 = 27.5 N·m.

Lesson summary

A moment is the turning effect of a force about a pivot. For a perpendicular force, moment magnitude equals force multiplied by the perpendicular distance to its line of action, M = Fd⊥. For a force applied at an angle θ to a lever of length r, M = Fr|sin θ|. Moments are measured in N·m, and their rotation tendency can be clockwise or counterclockwise depending on the force direction and its location relative to the pivot. Accurate diagrams, unit conversions, and careful identification of the perpendicular moment arm are essential for analyzing levers and, later, structures in equilibrium.
