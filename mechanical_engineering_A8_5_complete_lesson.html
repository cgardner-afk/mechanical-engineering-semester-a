Section 01

Why engineers measure power and efficiency

Imagine two warehouse hoists that can lift the same heavy crate to the same storage platform. One takes 10 seconds and the other takes 25 seconds. Both give the crate the same increase in gravitational potential energy, but the first hoist performs that useful task more quickly. Engineers describe how quickly work is done or energy is transferred using power.

Now imagine that the two hoists use different amounts of electrical energy to accomplish the lift. One wastes more energy as heat and sound in its motor and moving parts. Engineers describe the proportion of supplied energy that becomes useful output using efficiency. A machine can be powerful but inefficient, efficient but slow, or both powerful and efficient. These ideas help engineers choose motors, estimate operating costs, and evaluate lifting systems.

Words to know before we begin

A hoist is a machine that lifts a load. Input energy is energy supplied to a machine. Useful output energy is the part transferred into the intended result, such as raising a load. Power is energy transferred per unit of time. Efficiency compares useful output with input.

Section 02

Review work and gravitational potential energy

In A8.1, you learned that work is energy transferred when a force acts through a displacement. For a constant force parallel to movement, W = Fd, where W is work in joules (J), F is force in newtons (N), and d is displacement in meters (m). In A8.3, you learned that lifting a load increases gravitational potential energy by GPE = mgh, where m is mass in kilograms, g = 9.8 N/kg near Earth's surface, and h is the vertical height gained in meters.

For an ideal steady lift, the useful work done to raise the load equals its increase in GPE. A 100 kg crate raised 4 m gains 100 × 9.8 × 4 = 3,920 J. The crate gains the same energy whether the hoist takes 5 seconds or 20 seconds. The time changes the power, not the useful energy required to reach that height.

Units reminder

Energy and work are measured in joules (J). Time is measured in seconds (s). Power is measured in watts (W). The letter W can mean work when used as an equation variable, or watt when written as a unit after a number. Use the context to tell them apart.

Section 03

Power: how quickly work is done

Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or work is done. To calculate average power over a time interval, divide the work by the elapsed time. The result is measured in watts. One watt means one joule of energy transferred each second: 1 W = 1 J/s.

Average power = work ÷ time   →   P = W/t

Here P means average power in watts, W is work in joules, and t is elapsed time in seconds. A machine transferring 1,200 J in 4 seconds has average power of 1,200 ÷ 4 = 300 W. If the same energy transfer takes 8 seconds, average power is only 150 W.

Worked example 1 — Hoist power

A hoist does 6,000 J of useful work in 15 seconds. Step 1: Identify W = 6,000 J and t = 15 s. Step 2: Use P = W/t. Step 3: Calculate 6,000 ÷ 15 = 400 W. Interpretation: The hoist transfers an average of 400 joules of useful energy each second.

If you know power and time instead, rearrange the equation to find work: W = Pt. If you know work and power, rearrange to find time: t = W/P. Rearranging means performing the same multiplication or division on both sides of the equation to isolate the unknown quantity.

Step-by-step worked method · Calculate average power

  1. Step 1: Read useful work = 6,000 J and time = 15 s.
  2. Step 2: Use P = W/t.
  3. Step 3: Substitute 6,000/15 = 400 W.
  4. Step 4: Check units: J/s = W; distinguish useful output from input power.
Section 04

Useful power when lifting a load

For a load lifted steadily through height h in time t, useful output work is mgh. Substitute this into the power equation to obtain Puseful = mgh/t. This gives the rate at which the load gains gravitational potential energy. It is not automatically the electrical input power, because a real hoist also has losses.

Worked example 2 — Find useful output power

A 150 kg load rises 6 m in 18 s. First calculate useful energy: mgh = 150 × 9.8 × 6 = 8,820 J. Then divide by time: Puseful = 8,820 ÷ 18 = 490 W. The load gains an average of 490 joules of gravitational potential energy each second.

Suppose the same load rises the same 6 m in 9 seconds. Its energy gain is still 8,820 J, but its useful output power becomes 8,820 ÷ 9 = 980 W. Halving the lifting time doubles the average useful power needed for the same job.

Step-by-step worked method · Calculate lifting power

  1. Step 1: Read mass 150 kg, height 6 m, time 18 s.
  2. Step 2: Useful energy = mgh = 150 × 9.8 × 6 = 8,820 J.
  3. Step 3: Useful power = 8,820/18 = 490 W.
  4. Step 4: For the same lift in 9 s: power = 8,820/9 = 980 W.
  5. Step 5: Explain that faster completion requires greater average useful power.
Section 05

Input energy, useful output, and losses

Real machines do not convert all supplied energy into the intended useful result. Some electrical energy becomes heat in motor windings; some becomes sound or thermal energy through friction. The energy is not destroyed. It is transferred into forms that are not useful for the task.

Input energy = useful output energy + other energy transfers

For example, if a motor receives 5,000 J of electrical energy and raises a load by an amount corresponding to 3,500 J of GPE, the remaining 1,500 J is transferred into other forms. We call this the unaccounted-for or loss energy when evaluating useful output. Do not subtract the energy from existence; it remains in the total energy accounting.

Worked example 3 — Energy accounting

Input = 8,000 J. Useful output = 6,200 J. Other transfers = input − useful output = 8,000 − 6,200 = 1,800 J. Check: 6,200 + 1,800 = 8,000 J.

Step-by-step worked method · Find energy transferred to other forms

  1. Step 1: Read input = 8,000 J and useful output = 6,200 J.
  2. Step 2: Use E_other = E_input − E_useful.
  3. Step 3: Calculate 8,000 − 6,200 = 1,800 J.
  4. Step 4: Check 6,200 + 1,800 = 8,000 J.
Section 06

Efficiency: the useful fraction of input energy

Efficiency tells us what fraction of the supplied energy becomes useful output. It is a ratio, meaning one quantity divided by another. Divide useful output energy by input energy, then multiply by 100 to express the answer as a percentage.

Efficiency (%) = (useful output energy ÷ input energy) × 100

A percentage means a number out of 100. An efficiency of 80% means that 80 out of every 100 joules supplied become the intended useful output, while 20 joules are transferred into other forms. For an ordinary machine with no additional energy source, useful output energy cannot exceed input energy, so efficiency cannot exceed 100%.

Worked example 4 — Calculate efficiency

A lift receives 10,000 J of input energy and transfers 7,500 J into the load's gravitational potential energy. Divide useful output by input: 7,500 ÷ 10,000 = 0.75. Multiply by 100: 75% efficiency. The other 2,500 J (25%) is transferred into other forms.

When the time interval is the same for input and output, you can also calculate efficiency using power: efficiency (%) = (useful output power ÷ input power) × 100. Energy ratios and power ratios give the same efficiency when they describe the same interval.

Step-by-step worked method · Calculate efficiency

  1. Step 1: Read useful output 7,500 J and input 10,000 J.
  2. Step 2: Use efficiency = E_useful/E_input.
  3. Step 3: Divide: 7,500/10,000 = 0.75.
  4. Step 4: Multiply by 100 to obtain 75%.
  5. Step 5: Check efficiency is between 0% and 100% for this ordinary machine.
Section 07

Rearrange efficiency to find input energy

Engineers often know how much useful energy a machine must deliver and its expected efficiency. They need to estimate how much energy to supply. Convert the percentage to a decimal before rearranging. For example, 80% becomes 0.80 and 65% becomes 0.65.

Algebra steps — Find input energy

Start with efficiency as a decimal: η = Euseful / Einput. Multiply both sides by Einput: η × Einput = Euseful. Divide both sides by η: Einput = Euseful / η. The Greek letter η (eta) is a common symbol for efficiency.

Worked example 5 — Estimate electrical energy

A hoist must deliver 4,800 J of useful lifting energy at 80% efficiency. Convert 80% to 0.80. Input energy = 4,800 ÷ 0.80 = 6,000 J. Other transfers = 6,000 − 4,800 = 1,200 J.

Check your answer: the required input should be greater than the useful output when efficiency is below 100%. Dividing by 0.80 gives a larger number, which makes physical sense.

Step-by-step worked method · Find required input energy

  1. Step 1: Read useful output 4,800 J and efficiency 80%.
  2. Step 2: Convert 80% to decimal 0.80.
  3. Step 3: Rearrange E_input = E_useful/η.
  4. Step 4: Calculate 4,800/0.80 = 6,000 J.
  5. Step 5: Check other transfers = 6,000 − 4,800 = 1,200 J.
Section 08

Find electrical input power for a lifting system

A common engineering design question combines several ideas from this unit. A load has a known mass, must rise through a known height, must be lifted in a specified time, and the hoist has a known efficiency. We want the average electrical input power, not just the useful power delivered to the load.

Electrical input power = mgh ÷ (time × efficiency as a decimal)
Worked example 6 — Complete hoist calculation

A 200 kg load is raised 5 m in 20 seconds by a hoist operating at 70% efficiency. Step 1: Useful energy = mgh = 200 × 9.8 × 5 = 9,800 J.

Step 2: Useful power = 9,800 ÷ 20 = 490 W.

Step 3: Convert efficiency: 70% = 0.70.

Step 4: Electrical input power = 490 ÷ 0.70 = 700 W.

Check: Input energy = 700 × 20 = 14,000 J. Useful output = 9,800 J. The useful fraction is 9,800/14,000 = 0.70 = 70%. The difference, 4,200 J, is transferred into other forms.

Be careful about which power a question requests. Useful output power is the rate at which the load gains GPE. Input power is the rate at which electrical energy is supplied. For a real hoist with efficiency below 100%, input power is larger than useful output power.

Step-by-step worked method · Find electrical input power

  1. Step 1: Read m = 200 kg, h = 5 m, t = 20 s, efficiency 70%.
  2. Step 2: Useful energy = 200 × 9.8 × 5 = 9,800 J.
  3. Step 3: Useful output power = 9,800/20 = 490 W.
  4. Step 4: Convert efficiency 70% to 0.70.
  5. Step 5: Electrical input power = 490/0.70 = 700 W.
  6. Step 6: Check input energy = 700 × 20 = 14,000 J and efficiency = 9,800/14,000 = 70%.
Section 09

Compare two machines fairly

To compare machines, first check that they perform the same useful task. Two hoists that lift the same load through the same height deliver the same useful energy. If one uses less input energy, it is more efficient. If one completes the lift in less time, it delivers greater useful output power. Neither observation by itself tells you everything about the other property.

HoistUseful outputInput energyTimeEfficiencyUseful power
A6,000 J8,000 J12 s75%500 W
B6,000 J7,500 J20 s80%300 W

Hoist B is more efficient because 6,000/7,500 = 80%, compared with 75% for A. Hoist A has greater useful output power because it does the same useful work in less time. A design decision might favor A when speed matters most, or B when reducing energy use matters most. In A8.6, you will make these trade-offs under design constraints.

Section 11

Common mistakes and equation decision guide

Do not confuse joules with watts: joules measure energy and watts measure energy per second. Do not use the numerical percentage 80 in place of 0.80 when dividing by efficiency. Do not divide input energy by useful output energy when calculating efficiency; the correct order is useful ÷ input. Do not mistake useful output power for electrical input power. Finally, remember that energy transferred to heat or sound is not destroyed.

Which equation should I use?

Given work and time? P = W/t. Given mass, height, and time? Puseful = mgh/t. Given useful and input energy? Efficiency = (useful/input) × 100%. Given input and useful energy, asked about losses? Loss = input − useful. Given useful power and efficiency, asked for electrical input power? Divide useful power by efficiency as a decimal.

Engineering extension: efficiency can change with load

A motor's efficiency is not always the same at every operating load. Compare two trials for the same hoist, rather than assuming one percentage applies in all situations. For a light load, the machine receives 5,000 J and delivers 3,500 J of useful lifting energy. Its efficiency is 3,500 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = 70%. For a heavier load, the machine receives 12,000 J and delivers 10,200 J of useful lifting energy. Its efficiency is 10,200 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = 85%.

What changed? The efficiency increased from 70% to 85% at the higher load in this example. This does not mean that heavier loads always improve efficiency; real machines have different operating curves. The important engineering skill is to calculate efficiency separately from the input and useful-output values given for each condition.

Try it: At a third operating condition, the hoist receives 20,000 J and delivers 16,000 J. Efficiency = 16,000 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = 80%. Which of the three operating conditions is most efficient? The second, at 85%.

Section 12

Independent practice with guided solutions

Work each problem before opening its explanation. Include units, show the calculation, and check whether the answer is an energy, power, or percentage.

1. A motor performs 3,600 J of work in 12 s. Find average power.

P = 3,600/12 = 300 W.

2. A hoist transfers 8,000 J of useful energy in 20 s. Find useful power.

P = 8,000/20 = 400 W.

3. A machine receives 10,000 J and delivers 8,500 J of useful energy. Find efficiency.

Efficiency = 8,500/10,000 × 100 = 85%.

4. A hoist receives 12,000 J and delivers 9,000 J of useful energy. How much energy goes to other forms?

12,000 − 9,000 = 3,000 J.

5. A 100 kg load rises 4 m in 8 s. Find useful output power.

GPE = 100 × 9.8 × 4 = 3,920 J. P = 3,920/8 = 490 W.

6. A hoist delivers 500 W of useful power at 80% efficiency. Find electrical input power.

Convert 80% to 0.80. Input power = 500/0.80 = 625 W.

7. A hoist must deliver 6,000 J at 75% efficiency. Find input energy.

Input energy = 6,000/0.75 = 8,000 J.

8. A 250 kg load rises 4 m in 10 s at 70% efficiency. Find input power.

Useful energy = 250 × 9.8 × 4 = 9,800 J. Useful power = 980 W. Input power = 980/0.70 = 1,400 W.

9. A motor supplies 900 W and delivers 720 W of useful output. Find efficiency.

Efficiency = 720/900 × 100 = 80%.

10. Two hoists perform 5,000 J of useful work in 10 s and 25 s. Which has greater useful power?

First: 5,000/10 = 500 W. Second: 5,000/25 = 200 W. The first is more powerful; efficiency cannot be determined from this information alone.

11. A motor uses 2,000 J and delivers 1,500 J of useful energy. Find loss energy and percentage.

Loss energy = 500 J. Loss fraction = 500/2,000 × 100 = 25%.

12. A hoist lifts 150 kg by 6 m in 15 s at 60% efficiency. Find input power.

Useful energy = 150 × 9.8 × 6 = 8,820 J. Useful power = 8,820/15 = 588 W. Input power = 588/0.60 = 980 W.

Section 13

Glossary: power and efficiency vocabulary

Click each term to reveal its definition. The expandable vocabulary format matches the earlier lessons.

Power

The rate at which work is done or energy is transferred; measured in watts.

Watt (W)

The unit of power. One watt equals one joule per second (1 J/s).

Joule (J)

The unit of energy and work.

Work

Energy transferred by a force acting through displacement; for aligned constant force, W = Fd.

Average power

Total work or energy transferred divided by elapsed time.

Efficiency

Useful output energy divided by input energy, usually expressed as a percentage.

Input energy

Energy supplied to a machine, such as electrical energy supplied to a motor.

Useful output energy

Energy transferred into the intended task, such as increasing a load's GPE.

Input power

The rate at which energy is supplied to a machine.

Useful output power

The rate at which a machine delivers useful energy.

Energy losses

Energy transferred into forms not counted as useful output, such as thermal energy and sound; it is not destroyed.

Gravitational potential energy

Energy associated with height; near Earth's surface, GPE = mgh.

Hoist

A machine used to raise or lower a load.

Load

The object or material a machine lifts or carries.

Percentage

A fraction expressed out of 100; 75% = 0.75.

Efficiency symbol η (eta)

A Greek letter commonly used to represent efficiency as a decimal or percentage.

Section 14

Lesson summary and next steps

Power measures how quickly work is done or energy is transferred. Use P = W/t for average power and Puseful = mgh/t for a load lifted steadily through a known height. Efficiency compares useful output with input: efficiency (%) = useful/input × 100. A real machine transfers some input energy into heat, sound, and other forms, so electrical input power is typically greater than useful output power.

To estimate electrical input power for a hoist, first calculate useful energy from mgh, divide by time, and then divide by efficiency expressed as a decimal. In A8.6, you will combine these skills to select the best lifting system under real design constraints involving lift time, energy use, and machine performance.
