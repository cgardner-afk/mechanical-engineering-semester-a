SECTION 01

Engineering investigation: design a safer bridge

Imagine that a town needs a short pedestrian bridge across a drainage channel. The bridge must carry people and equipment safely, but the budget is limited. Engineers cannot simply choose the strongest-looking design. They need to predict how loads move through the structure, check the forces carried by its supports, compare how much different layouts deflect, and decide which designs satisfy every requirement.

This culminating Unit A5 investigation combines moments, rotational equilibrium, beam reactions, and truss force paths. You will first analyze a movable point load on a simply supported beam, then compare hypothetical bridge designs using a consistent virtual test. The goal is to make a justified engineering recommendation based on evidence and constraints, rather than on appearance alone.

Learning target: Compare structural arrangements and optimize load support by calculating reactions, interpreting simulated deflection, and applying support-capacity, deflection, and cost limits.

SECTION 02

Build a mathematical model before experimenting

For a simply supported beam of span L, place a downward point load P at distance x from the left support A. The right support B is L meters from A. To keep the beam stationary, the sum of upward reactions must equal the downward load, and the moments about either support must cancel.

RBL = Px   ⇒   RB = Px/L
RA = P − RB = P(L − x)/L

As a load moves toward the right support, the right reaction rises and the left reaction falls. Both reactions remain upward for a single downward load placed between the supports. For a centered load, each reaction equals half the load, regardless of span in this ideal weightless-beam model. These equations are exact for the stated idealization; real structures also need checks for material strength, bending, deflection, and connections.

SECTION 03

Worked example: compare two load locations

A service platform spans 8 m and carries a 720 N point load. At first the load is 2 m from the left support. Later it moves to a position 6 m from the left. Find both reactions at each location and calculate the change in the right reaction.

Worked example 1 · Moving-load comparison

  1. At x = 2 m, RB = 720(2)/8 = 180 N; RA = 720 − 180 = 540 N.
  2. At x = 6 m, RB = 720(6)/8 = 540 N; RA = 720 − 540 = 180 N.
  3. The right reaction increases by 540 − 180 = 360 N. The left reaction decreases by the same amount.
  4. Check: the two reactions total 720 N in both trials.

This is why a bridge designer must test more than one load position. The maximum reaction at a support may occur when a heavy load is close to that support rather than at the center of the bridge.

SECTION 04

Design a fair structural comparison

When comparing two bridge layouts, change the structural arrangement while holding other test conditions constant. Use the same span, applied load, load position, and method of measuring deflection. If one design is tested with a heavier load, its greater deflection may reflect the different test rather than a weaker arrangement.

In a real bridge, deflection depends on stiffness, geometry, member sizes, materials, and connection behavior. This lesson's virtual design comparison uses illustrative performance data for candidate designs. Those values are not predictions from a complete finite-element model. They are provided to practice controlled comparisons, constraint checks, and engineering decisions. The reaction calculations, in contrast, come directly from the stated static-equilibrium equations.

Worked example 2 · Controlled test

Two hypothetical truss layouts are each tested under a 500 N center load over the same span. Layout X deflects 4.2 mm and Layout Y deflects 6.1 mm. Under these test conditions, X has less deflection by 1.9 mm. It would not be valid to conclude that X is always safer for every possible load or that its members cannot buckle; additional checks are necessary.

SECTION 05

Evaluate all design constraints together

Engineering specifications frequently include several limits. A bridge may have to keep deflection below a maximum, stay under a cost ceiling, and avoid exceeding the rated capacity of either support. A design that passes two of the three requirements but fails the third is not an acceptable solution. For a support check, compare each reaction with its own capacity; do not compare only the total load with a single support's rating.

Worked example 3 · Support capacity

A 9 m bridge carries a 630 N point load at x = 3 m. The left support is rated for 450 N and the right for 300 N. RB = 630(3)/9 = 210 N. RA = 630 − 210 = 420 N. Both reactions are within their respective ratings, so the support-capacity requirement is met for this loading case. This does not by itself verify beam stress, deflection, or other load cases.

Worked example 4 · Multiple constraints

A client requires a maximum deflection of 5 mm and a maximum estimated cost of $180. Candidate A costs $155 and deflects 5.8 mm; Candidate B costs $175 and deflects 4.4 mm; Candidate C costs $205 and deflects 3.7 mm. Only Candidate B satisfies both requirements. A fails deflection, while C exceeds the budget. Choosing the lowest deflection alone would produce an unacceptable design.

SECTION 06

How truss layouts change load paths

A truss carries loads through axial tension and compression in its connected members. A triangular layout creates a geometrically stable framework under ideal pin-jointed assumptions. Adding or moving diagonal members changes which joints and members carry the forces and may affect stiffness, mass, and cost. The supports must still balance the same external loads, so changing internal bracing does not automatically change the external reactions of an otherwise identical simply supported structure.

For the same span, load magnitude, and load position, the equilibrium reactions are determined by external force and moment balance. The internal forces and deflections can differ between truss layouts because the load travels through different members. This distinction is important: support reactions describe external equilibrium; member forces and deflection describe internal structural response.

Worked example 5 · Same reactions, different stiffness

Two idealized bridge layouts both carry a 480 N centered point load between the same supports. Both have RA = RB = 240 N upward. If one layout deflects 3 mm and the other 7 mm in a controlled virtual test, their external reactions remain equal even though their structural responses differ.

SECTION 08

Interpret results and recommend a design

A good recommendation states the load case, names the requirements, reports the relevant numerical results, and explains why the selected design is feasible. If multiple candidates satisfy all constraints, an engineer may choose the lowest-cost feasible option, or may prefer a design with greater stiffness if the client values additional margin. If no candidate passes, the correct conclusion is that the current options do not meet the specifications; the engineer should revise the design or negotiate a justified requirement change rather than declaring a failing option safe.

Worked example 6 · A decision from evidence

Suppose three virtual bridge candidates are tested under identical conditions. The budget is $190 and the maximum deflection is 5.0 mm. Candidate 1 costs $145 and deflects 6.0 mm. Candidate 2 costs $185 and deflects 4.5 mm. Candidate 3 costs $230 and deflects 3.0 mm. Assume all three pass the separate support-reaction checks. Candidate 2 is the only feasible choice: Candidate 1 fails deflection, and Candidate 3 fails cost. The recommendation should cite those values rather than simply calling Candidate 2 “best.”

SECTION 09

Write an engineering investigation report

After recording at least four trials, prepare a short report that could be understood by a client who has not used the simulator. Begin by stating the bridge span, applied load, and position. Show the moment equation and both support reactions for one trial, including units. Explain how the reactions changed when the load moved and why a centered load divides evenly between supports.

Next, compare the candidate layouts under the same loading conditions. Report each candidate's estimated deflection, cost, and whether it meets the two support capacities. Identify the designs that satisfy every requirement, and make a recommendation supported by the recorded evidence. Finish with one limitation of the virtual model, such as its omission of material stress, buckling, connection behavior, or real distributed loads.

Success criteria: Your report uses correct equilibrium equations, includes numerical evidence from controlled trials, checks all constraints, and clearly distinguishes simulated estimates from proven real-world structural safety.

INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Independent practice with worked solutions

Use the equations and design criteria to solve these problems before opening the worked solutions.

A · Change in right reaction

A 750 N load moves from x = 2 m to x = 6 m on a beam spanning 10 m. By how much does RB increase?

Show worked solution

Initially RB = 750(2)/10 = 150 N. Finally RB = 750(6)/10 = 450 N. Increase = 300 N.

B · Centered load

A 900 N point load is centered on a 12 m simply supported beam. Find both reactions. Would a 6 m span change those reactions if the same load remained centered?

Show worked solution

Each reaction is 450 N upward. In this ideal weightless-beam model, changing the span does not change the reactions for a centered load.

C · Support limits

A 540 N point load acts 5 m from the left support on a 9 m beam. Each support is rated for 320 N. Does this load case meet both ratings?

Show worked solution

RB = 540(5)/9 = 300 N; RA = 240 N. Both are below 320 N, so the support ratings are met for this load case.

D · Identify the failed constraint

Maximum deflection is 5 mm and maximum cost is $210. Design A: 4.7 mm, $225. Design B: 5.3 mm, $190. Design C: 4.9 mm, $205. Which designs meet both?

Show worked solution

Only Design C passes. A exceeds the budget, and B exceeds the deflection limit.

E · Controlled comparison

Two layouts are tested with different load magnitudes and show different deflections. Can the difference be attributed to layout alone?

Show worked solution

No. Load magnitude must be controlled to isolate the effect of layout.

F · External versus internal response

Two truss layouts carry the same 600 N centered point load between the same supports. Must they have the same support reactions? Must they have the same deflection?

Show worked solution

In the ideal simply supported model, each has 300 N upward at each support. Their deflections need not match because their stiffness and member arrangements can differ.

G · No feasible option

Three candidates all exceed at least one required design limit. What should the engineer report?

Show worked solution

Report that none is feasible under the stated requirements, identify the failed constraints, and recommend design changes or further analysis.

H · Moving-load interpretation

In an experiment with constant P and L, RB increases linearly with x. What relationship does this support?

Show worked solution

RB = Px/L. For fixed P and L, the reaction is directly proportional to x.

VOCABULARY & GLOSSARY

Vocabulary and key concepts

Structural arrangement

The configuration of a structure's members, supports, and connections.

Support reaction

The force supplied by a support to balance an applied load.

Deflection

Displacement of a structural component under loading.

Design constraint

A requirement a proposed design must satisfy, such as a load rating, deflection limit, or budget.

Controlled comparison

A test that holds other relevant conditions constant while changing the design feature being studied.

Feasible design

A design that satisfies all stated constraints for the tested conditions.

Load path

The route by which applied forces travel through members and joints to the supports.

Model limitation

A physical effect or condition that an engineering model does not represent.

TEST PREPARATION · FOLLOW THE METHOD

Step-by-step problem-solving guide: Integrated bridge testing and evidence

These are new examples practicing the same skills found on the Unit 5 assessment and Semester A final. Work through the numbered steps in order. For a diagram question, read the printed labels; never measure the drawing with a ruler unless instructed.

Predict how a moving load changes reactions

Practice-style example: A 500 N point load moves from x = 1 m to x = 4 m along a 5 m simply supported beam.

  1. Step 1. Identify span L = 5 m, load P = 500 N, positions x₁ = 1 m and x₂ = 4 m.
  2. Step 2. Unknown: change in right reaction.
  3. Step 3. Use Rᵣ = Px/L, taking moments about the left support.
  4. Step 4. At x₁: Rᵣ = 500 × 1/5 = 100 N; at x₂: Rᵣ = 500 × 4/5 = 400 N.
  5. Step 5. Subtract 400 − 100 = 300 N.
  6. Step 6. Check: moving the load toward the right support increases the right reaction.
Final answer: Right reaction increases 300 N

Read a reaction pattern from test data

Practice-style example: A 600 N load on a 4 m beam produces left reactions 450 N at x = 1 m, 300 N at x = 2 m, and 150 N at x = 3 m. Predict x = 0.5 m.

  1. Step 1. Observe left reaction falls by 150 N per 1 m moved right.
  2. Step 2. Unknown: left reaction at x = 0.5 m.
  3. Step 3. Use Rₗ = P(L − x)/L, or extrapolate the consistent linear pattern.
  4. Step 4. Substitute Rₗ = 600(4 − 0.5)/4.
  5. Step 5. Calculate Rₗ = 525 N.
  6. Step 6. Check: the load is closer to the left support than at x = 1 m, so left reaction should exceed 450 N.
Final answer: 525 N left reaction

Make a controlled design comparison

Practice-style example: Two bridge layouts must be compared for deflection under a 400 N midspan load.

  1. Step 1. Identify the question: which layout deflects less?
  2. Step 2. Choose layout as the independent variable and deflection as the measured response.
  3. Step 3. Keep load magnitude and position, span, support conditions, and material consistent.
  4. Step 4. Test both layouts and record deflection under identical conditions.
  5. Step 5. Compare each result with the allowable deflection and any mass/cost limits.
  6. Step 6. Recommend only a design that passes every mandatory requirement; document the conditions and limitations.
Final answer: Controlled comparison with documented measurements
Try it yourself — reveal the full solution

Question: A 750 N point load moves from 2 m to 6 m along an 8 m beam. By how much does the right reaction increase?

Solution: ΔRᵣ = 750(6 − 2)/8 = 375 N.

Lesson summary

Bridge design requires more than calculating a single force. For a simply supported beam with a point load P at distance x from the left support over span L, the reactions are RB = Px/L and RA = P(L−x)/L. Moving a load changes how much force each support carries, even though the total downward load stays constant. Different structural arrangements can have the same external reactions but different internal member forces and deflections. Engineers compare designs under controlled conditions, check each support's rated capacity, apply deflection and cost limits, and select only candidates that satisfy every requirement. A responsible recommendation cites calculations and test data and recognizes the limitations of simplified virtual models.
