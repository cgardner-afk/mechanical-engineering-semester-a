Section 01

Why stopping is an engineering problem

Imagine a motorized inspection cart rolling along a factory floor. The cart has a heavy load, and its controller suddenly detects an obstruction. The brakes must bring it to rest. The cart's speed matters, but it is not the only consideration: the engineer must also ask how quickly the cart stops. A very sudden stop can produce a large force on the cart and its contents. A more gradual stop can produce a smaller average force even when the cart begins with exactly the same mass and velocity.

Mechanical engineers encounter this problem in robotic workcells, industrial carts, packaging machinery, vehicle safety equipment, and protective barriers. A shipping container may include cushioning to slow a fragile part over a slightly longer interval. A robot controller may use a planned deceleration instead of commanding an abrupt halt. In both cases, the engineering objective is to control how the object's motion changes.

This lesson uses impulse to connect changes in momentum with the forces that act during a stop. We will calculate the impulse required to stop a moving object, determine its average stopping force, and explain why the duration of the stop is an important design variable.

Section 02

Review momentum and signed velocity

Momentum combines mass and velocity. The symbol p represents momentum, m represents mass, and v represents signed velocity. Mass is measured in kilograms (kg), velocity in meters per second (m/s), and momentum in kilogram-meters per second (kg·m/s).

p = mv

Choose a positive direction before calculating. In our examples, east is positive and west is negative. A 300 kg cart traveling east at 4 m/s has momentum (300)(+4) = +1,200 kg·m/s. A 300 kg cart traveling west at 4 m/s has momentum −1,200 kg·m/s. The negative sign identifies direction; it does not mean the cart has less mass.

A stopped cart has velocity zero, so its momentum is zero. Bringing a moving cart to rest therefore changes its momentum from its initial value to zero. That change is what impulse measures.

Section 03

Impulse means change in momentum

In ordinary conversation, an impulse can mean a sudden urge. In engineering mechanics, impulse has a precise meaning: the change in an object's momentum. The symbol J represents impulse, and the Greek letter Δ (delta) means “change in.” The subscripts i and f mean initial and final.

J = Δp = pf − pi = m(vf − vi)

This expression assumes the object's mass remains constant. To calculate impulse, find its momentum after the event and subtract its momentum before the event. For a cart moving east that stops, final momentum is zero and initial momentum is positive. Its impulse is therefore negative, pointing west. The force that stops the cart acts opposite its initial motion.

Impulse has units of N·s (newton-seconds), which are equivalent to kg·m/s. One newton is one kg·m/s², so multiplying a newton by a second gives kg·m/s. Both units describe the same physical quantity.

Important sign rule: A negative impulse is not an error. With east positive, a negative impulse means the change in momentum points west.
Section 04

Calculate impulse step by step

Before using an equation, identify the moving object, its mass, its initial velocity, and its final velocity. Keep the direction signs with the velocities. The arithmetic is straightforward once the physical situation is represented correctly.

Worked example 1 · A cart stops

A 250 kg factory cart travels east at 6 m/s and comes to rest. East is positive. Find the impulse on the cart.

Step 1: m = 250 kg; vi = +6 m/s; vf = 0 m/s.

Step 2: J = m(vf − vi).

Step 3: J = 250(0 − 6) = −1,500 N·s.

Step 4: Interpret the sign: the stopping impulse is westward, opposite the cart's initial motion.

Worked example 2 · A westbound cart stops

A 400 kg cart travels west at 3 m/s. East is positive. When it stops, J = 400[0 − (−3)] = +1,200 N·s. The positive impulse points east, opposite the cart's westward movement.

Worked example 3 · A cart slows without stopping

A 100 kg cart changes from +8 m/s to +3 m/s. J = 100(3 − 8) = −500 N·s. The cart still moves east afterward, but its momentum decreased. Impulse describes a change in momentum; it does not require the object to come to rest.

Section 05

Connect force and stopping time

How can two stopping systems produce the same impulse but different forces? The answer is time. When a force acts on an object, it changes the object's momentum. For a constant force, impulse equals force multiplied by the duration. For a changing force, we use the average net force over that interval.

J = FavgΔt

The symbol Favg means average net force, measured in newtons (N). The symbol Δt means elapsed time in seconds (s). If a net force of −500 N acts for 3 s, its impulse is (−500)(3) = −1,500 N·s. A net force of −1,500 N acting for 1 s would produce the same impulse. Both can stop the same moving cart, but the shorter stop requires a larger average force magnitude.

Impulse depends on the total change in momentum. For a given starting and ending velocity and constant mass, that change is fixed. Stopping time determines how large the average force must be to produce the change.

Section 06

Calculate average stopping force

Rearrange the impulse equation to solve for average force. Divide the impulse by the time interval. Use the entire stopping time, not a guessed portion of it.

Favg = J / Δt = m(vf − vi) / Δt

Worked example 4 · Average braking force

The 250 kg cart from Worked Example 1 stops from +6 m/s in 3.0 s. Find the average net stopping force.

Step 1: Its impulse is −1,500 N·s.

Step 2: Divide by the stopping time: Favg = −1,500 / 3.0.

Step 3: Favg = −500 N, or 500 N westward.

Step 4: Check the units: N·s / s = N.

Worked example 5 · A shorter stopping interval

The same cart stops from the same speed in only 0.50 s. Its impulse is still −1,500 N·s, but Favg = −1,500 / 0.50 = −3,000 N. The stop is six times faster, and the average force magnitude is six times greater.

Magnitude versus signed force: A problem asking for the magnitude of stopping force expects a nonnegative number (such as 3,000 N). A problem asking for signed net force requires a direction (such as −3,000 N when east is positive).
Section 07

Why a longer stop lowers force

Think of a fragile package moving on a conveyor. If the package strikes a rigid wall, its momentum can drop to zero in a very short time. If it instead contacts a suitable cushioning material, it may take longer to stop. The same change in momentum is spread over more time, so the average net stopping force is smaller.

This is an inverse relationship: for the same impulse magnitude, doubling stopping time halves average force magnitude; tripling stopping time reduces it to one-third. The relationship works only when the compared cases have the same change in momentum. If mass or incoming speed changes, calculate the impulse again before comparing forces.

Worked example 6 · Compare two designs

A 600 kg cart traveling at +5 m/s stops. Its impulse is 600(0 − 5) = −3,000 N·s. Design A stops it in 0.25 s, producing −12,000 N on average. Design B stops it in 0.75 s, producing −4,000 N on average. Design B produces one-third the average stopping-force magnitude because it triples the stopping time.

Longer stopping time is often desirable, but it is not free. A cushioning device may require more space to compress, a larger machine travel envelope, or additional cost. Engineers must also consider the peak force: an average force calculation alone does not reveal the highest instantaneous force during impact. Those tradeoffs are the focus of A7.6.

Section 08

Read force–time diagrams

Engineers often plot force vertically and time horizontally. On a force–time graph, the signed area between the force curve and the time axis represents impulse. A constant-force graph forms a rectangle, so its area is force × time. For a variable force, the same idea applies to the total signed area, although the shape may not be rectangular.

The diagram compares magnitudes for clarity. In the signed east-positive convention, a westward braking force would appear below the time axis. If the force is not constant, average force is the constant value that would produce the same total impulse over the same interval.

Worked example 7 · Area under a force–time graph

A constant westward net force of −800 N acts for 0.50 s. The signed area is (−800)(0.50) = −400 N·s. The cart's momentum changes by −400 kg·m/s. The rectangle's area represents impulse, not distance or kinetic energy.

Section 09

Impact protection and design limits

Engineers use the impulse–momentum relationship when designing protective bumpers, compliant stops, packaging inserts, and controlled braking systems. A bumper that deforms can sometimes increase the time over which a moving object slows down. A controller can reduce abrupt changes in motion by commanding a longer deceleration. Both approaches can reduce average stopping-force magnitude for the same initial and final momentum.

However, the simple model used here does not prove that a real design is safe. Real collisions can have rapidly changing forces, material failure, rebound, friction, and loads distributed through multiple components. A design may reduce average force while still creating a damaging peak force. A cushion can also run out of compression space, causing a sudden hard stop. Engineering decisions require material tests, deformation limits, available space, cost, and appropriate safety factors.

In this lesson we treat each cart as a single object with constant mass and use the average net stopping force. In A7.6 you will use this model to compare candidate designs while checking practical constraints.

Section 11

Analyze your engineering trials

Suppose the mass is 400 kg and the initial velocity is +6 m/s. Initial momentum is +2,400 kg·m/s, and the impulse to stop is −2,400 N·s. At 0.3 s, the average net force is −8,000 N. At 0.6 s, it is −4,000 N. At 1.2 s, it is −2,000 N. The impulse stays the same because the initial and final momentum are unchanged; the force magnitude falls as stopping time increases.

Now double the mass to 800 kg while keeping the initial velocity and stopping time unchanged. The initial momentum and stopping impulse both double in magnitude. Because the time is unchanged, the average stopping force magnitude doubles too. Finally, reverse the cart's direction. The signs of momentum, impulse, and force reverse, but their magnitudes remain the same when mass, speed magnitude, and stopping time are unchanged.

Engineering conclusion: For a specified mass and change in velocity, stopping time is a useful design variable. Increasing it lowers the average net force magnitude, but the model alone cannot determine whether a real barrier meets all safety requirements.

Independent engineering practice

Attempt each problem without opening its solution. State the equation, substitute the signed values, show units, and explain the direction of any signed result.

Practice 1 · Impulse on a stopping cart

A 180 kg cart moves east at 5 m/s and stops. East is positive. Solution: J = 180(0 − 5) = −900 N·s, westward.

Practice 2 · Force from stopping time

A 500 kg cart moving east at 4 m/s stops in 0.5 s. Solution: J = 500(0 − 4) = −2,000 N·s; Favg = −2,000/0.5 = −4,000 N. Magnitude = 4,000 N.

Practice 3 · Compare stopping systems

A cart's momentum changes by −1,800 kg·m/s. System A stops it in 0.3 s and B in 0.9 s. Solution: FA = −1,800/0.3 = −6,000 N; FB = −1,800/0.9 = −2,000 N. B has one-third the average force magnitude.

Practice 4 · Reverse the direction

A 250 kg cart moves west at 8 m/s and stops in 2 s; east is positive. Solution: J = 250[0 − (−8)] = +2,000 N·s; Favg = +2,000/2 = +1,000 N, eastward.

Practice 5 · Slow without stopping

A 120 kg cart changes velocity from +9 to +4 m/s in 1.5 s. Solution: J = 120(4 − 9) = −600 N·s; Favg = −600/1.5 = −400 N.

Practice 6 · Force–time area

A constant net force of −700 N acts for 0.4 s. Solution: J = (−700)(0.4) = −280 N·s, the signed change in momentum.

Section 13

Vocabulary glossary

Momentum: Mass multiplied by signed velocity (p = mv). Impulse: Change in momentum (J = Δp). Net force: Combined effect of all forces acting on an object. Average net force: Constant equivalent force that produces the same impulse over an interval. Stopping time: Elapsed time between the beginning and end of a stop. Force magnitude: Nonnegative size of a force, without its direction. Signed quantity: A value whose plus or minus sign indicates direction along a chosen axis. Newton (N): SI unit of force. Newton-second (N·s): SI unit of impulse. Force–time graph: Plot whose signed area represents impulse. Peak force: Largest instantaneous force during an event, which need not equal the average force. Deformation: Change in shape of a component during loading.

Section 15

Lesson summary and next engineering question

Impulse measures a change in momentum. With constant mass, calculate it by multiplying mass by the change in signed velocity: J = m(vf − vi). Average net force equals impulse divided by elapsed time: Favg = J/Δt. For a fixed momentum change, a longer stopping time reduces the average force magnitude. A force–time graph represents impulse through signed area, and the force direction generally opposes a cart's initial motion when it is brought to rest.

These calculations are powerful, but they do not automatically select a safe design.
