SECTION 01

Geometry Is an Engineering Tool

Mechanical engineers design objects that must fit, move, support loads, and connect to other parts. A dimension that is slightly wrong can prevent a shaft from fitting inside a housing, cause a bracket to interfere with a moving part, or make a ramp too steep. Geometry provides a language for describing the size, shape, and spatial relationships of these parts. It also helps engineers estimate material use and check whether a proposed design satisfies a requirement.

Consider a rectangular sheet-metal cover. Its length and width determine how much flat material is needed. Its thickness affects how much material volume it contains. If the cover includes a diagonal reinforcing member, right-triangle geometry determines the member's length. If a ramp must rise to a particular height, trigonometry connects the ramp's angle, horizontal run, and sloped length. Each calculation answers a different design question, so the first engineering task is to identify the quantity being sought.

Geometry is a model of a real object

Engineers simplify real objects into idealized shapes: rectangles for panels, circles for holes, cylinders for rods, and triangles for braces. The simplification is useful when the shape matches the part closely enough for the purpose of the calculation. A rounded corner, cutout, or irregular surface may require a more detailed model. Draw and label the simplified shape before choosing a formula.

Throughout this lesson, distinguish length (one-dimensional, m), area (two-dimensional, m²), and volume (three-dimensional, m³). A numerical answer with the wrong unit often signals that the wrong formula was used.

SECTION 02

Perimeter, Area, and Units

Perimeter is the distance around a two-dimensional boundary. A rectangular guard with length 1.8 m and width 0.7 m has perimeter P = 2L + 2w = 2(1.8) + 2(0.7) = 5.0 m. Perimeter can help estimate the length of edge trim or sealing material. Area measures the amount of surface inside the boundary. The same guard has area A = Lw = 1.8 × 0.7 = 1.26 m², useful when estimating sheet material.

Perimeter and area are not interchangeable. Two shapes can have the same perimeter but different areas. A rectangle 3 m by 1 m has perimeter 8 m and area 3 m²; a square 2 m by 2 m also has perimeter 8 m but area 4 m². An engineer specifying both material coverage and edge treatment must calculate both quantities separately.

Three-step rectangle example

A protective plate must cover 0.96 m² and has a fixed width of 0.80 m.

  1. Step 1 — Identify: A = 0.96 m², w = 0.80 m, unknown L.
  2. Step 2 — Isolate: A = Lw, so divide both sides by w to obtain L = A/w.
  3. Step 3 — Substitute and verify: L = 0.96/0.80 = 1.20 m. Check: 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96 m². The units m²/m simplify to m.

For a triangular panel, A = ½bh, where b is the base and h is the perpendicular height. A triangular plate with base 1.4 m and perpendicular height 0.6 m has area ½ × 1.4 × 0.6 = 0.42 m². The height is not automatically the length of a sloping side.

YOU ARE THE ENGINEER

Designing a protective machine guard

A manufacturing company asks you to design a removable rectangular cover for a machine opening measuring 1.8 m by 0.7 m. The purchasing department needs to know how much sheet metal to order, while the fabrication team needs the length of protective edging. Area answers the sheet-metal question: 1.8 × 0.7 = 1.26 m². Perimeter answers the edging question: 2(1.8 + 0.7) = 5.0 m. An engineer must choose the measurement that matches the actual manufacturing requirement. In practice, material allowances, overlap, and waste would also be considered.

SECTION 03

Circles: Radius, Diameter, and Material

Circular holes, wheels, pulleys, and pipes appear throughout mechanical engineering. The radius r is the distance from the center to the edge. The diameter D crosses the circle through its center, so D = 2r. The circumference is the distance around the circle, C = 2πr = πD. The area inside the circle is A = πr². The constant π is approximately 3.14159; keep the π symbol until the final calculation when possible.

Suppose a circular inspection opening has diameter 0.20 m. Its radius is 0.10 m. The opening's circumference is π × 0.20 ≈ 0.628 m, which could help estimate the length of a gasket. Its area is π × (0.10)² ≈ 0.0314 m², which describes the opening's surface area. Using diameter in place of radius in πr² would make the computed area four times too large.

Three-step inverse circle example

A circular plate must have area 0.1257 m². Find the radius.

  1. Step 1 — Identify: Known A = 0.1257 m²; unknown r.
  2. Step 2 — Isolate: From A = πr², divide by π to get r² = A/π, then take the positive square root: r = √(A/π).
  3. Step 3 — Substitute and verify: r = √(0.1257/π) ≈ 0.200 m. Check: π(0.200)² ≈ 0.1257 m². Use the positive root because radius represents a physical length.

Remember that squaring applies to the entire radius: (0.10 m)² = 0.0100 m². When radius doubles, circle area increases by a factor of four because area depends on r². That nonlinear relationship matters when choosing hole sizes or material diameters.

YOU ARE THE ENGINEER

Sizing a circular inspection port

Your team needs a round access opening in a machine housing so technicians can inspect internal components. The design specifies a diameter of 0.20 m. The gasket supplier asks for the length of sealing material around the opening, and the fabrication team asks for the area to remove. Use circumference for the seal: C = πD ≈ 0.628 m. Use circle area for the cutout: A = π(0.10)² ≈ 0.0314 m². The diameter is given, but the area equation requires radius; recognizing that distinction prevents an expensive cutting error.

SECTION 04

Volume: From Flat Shapes to Solid Parts

Volume describes the amount of three-dimensional space occupied by a solid. A rectangular block has volume V = Lwh. For example, a 0.40 m by 0.25 m by 0.10 m block has volume 0.40 × 0.25 × 0.10 = 0.010 m³. A cylinder has a circular cross-section, so its volume is V = πr²h. A cylinder with radius 0.05 m and height 0.30 m has volume π(0.05)²(0.30) ≈ 0.00236 m³.

Volume can help estimate the mass of a uniform solid if density is known. For example, if a block occupies 0.010 m³ and has density 2,700 kg/m³, its mass is m = ρV = 2,700 × 0.010 = 27 kg. This assumes the block is solid and uniform; hollow spaces or mixed materials change the calculation.

Three-step cylinder-height example

A cylindrical container must hold 0.0157 m³ and has inside radius 0.10 m.

  1. Step 1 — Identify: Known V and r; unknown height h.
  2. Step 2 — Isolate: Rearrange V = πr²h by dividing both sides by πr²: h = V/(πr²).
  3. Step 3 — Substitute and verify: h = 0.0157/[π(0.10)²] ≈ 0.500 m. Check by multiplying the circular area by the height. The result is an internal height estimate for the idealized cylindrical space, not a complete design that accounts for wall thickness or a lid.

Unit conversions are especially important for volume: 1 m = 100 cm, but 1 m³ = 1,000,000 cm³. Converting each dimension before multiplying is usually the safest procedure.

YOU ARE THE ENGINEER

Estimating the mass of a machine component

You are selecting a lifting fixture for a solid rectangular metal component. Its dimensions are 0.40 m × 0.25 m × 0.10 m, and its material density is 2,700 kg/m³. First calculate volume: V = 0.40 × 0.25 × 0.10 = 0.010 m³. Then estimate mass: m = ρV = 2,700 × 0.010 = 27 kg. This estimate helps engineers assess handling requirements. A real lifting plan must also consider the fixture, safety factors, and applicable safety procedures.

SECTION 05

Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Theorem

A right triangle has one angle of exactly 90°. The two sides forming that angle are called the legs. The longest side, opposite the right angle, is the hypotenuse. The Pythagorean theorem states that for a right triangle, a² + b² = c², where a and b are the legs and c is the hypotenuse. This relationship applies to right triangles, not arbitrary triangles.

Three-step brace example

A diagonal brace connects points separated by 3.0 m horizontally and 4.0 m vertically.

  1. Step 1 — Identify: Known legs a = 3.0 m and b = 4.0 m; unknown c.
  2. Step 2 — Isolate: Use a² + b² = c² and take the positive square root: c = √(a² + b²).
  3. Step 3 — Substitute and verify: c = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.0 m. Check: 3² + 4² = 5². This is the familiar 3–4–5 right triangle.

Finding a missing leg

Suppose a support is 5.0 m long and spans 4.0 m horizontally. The vertical rise is unknown.

  1. Step 1 — Identify: c = 5.0 m, a = 4.0 m, unknown b.
  2. Step 2 — Isolate: Rearrange a² + b² = c² to b² = c² − a², then b = √(c² − a²).
  3. Step 3 — Substitute and verify: b = √(25 − 16) = 3.0 m. A negative value inside the square root would signal incompatible measurements because the hypotenuse cannot be shorter than a leg.

YOU ARE THE ENGINEER

Determining the length of a diagonal support

A machine frame needs a diagonal brace between two attachment points. The points are 0.90 m apart horizontally and 1.20 m apart vertically. How long must the brace span? The attachment points and frame edges form a right triangle. Apply the Pythagorean theorem: c = √(0.90² + 1.20²) = √2.25 = 1.50 m. This gives the straight-line distance between the attachment points. The actual manufactured part may need additional length for end connections, bolts, or weld joints.

SECTION 06

Angles and the Meaning of Slope

An angle measures how far one line turns relative to another. A full turn is 360°, a straight angle is 180°, and a right angle is 90°. In mechanical engineering, angles describe the orientation of ramps, brackets, supports, cutting tools, and moving mechanisms. This lesson uses degrees, so set your calculator to degree mode before evaluating trigonometric functions.

For a ramp, rise is the vertical height gained, run is the horizontal distance, and ramp length is the sloped distance along its surface. These three measurements form a right triangle when the floor is horizontal and the rise is vertical. The ramp length is the hypotenuse. Confusing run with ramp length leads to errors in slope and material calculations.

The ratio rise/run is sometimes called the grade or slope ratio. For example, a rise of 0.50 m over a horizontal run of 5.0 m has rise/run = 0.10, or 10% grade. This percentage is not the same as an angle of 10°. The angle is arctan(0.10) ≈ 5.71°. Distinguishing percent grade from degrees is essential in interpreting design requirements.

Before using trigonometry, sketch the triangle and label the reference angle. Then identify the vertical, horizontal, and sloping dimensions. The location of the angle determines which leg is opposite and which is adjacent.

YOU ARE THE ENGINEER

Checking whether a loading ramp is too steep

A design calls for a platform 0.45 m above the floor, with 4.5 m of available horizontal space. Before selecting materials, you need to describe the ramp's steepness. Its grade is rise/run = 0.45/4.5 = 0.10, or 10%. Its angle is arctan(0.10) ≈ 5.71°. These are two different descriptions of the same slope. Engineers compare the appropriate value with the project's requirements; neither the percentage nor the angle alone establishes that a ramp is safe or code-compliant.

SECTION 07

Sine, Cosine, and Tangent: SOH–CAH–TOA

Right-triangle trigonometry connects an acute angle to ratios of side lengths. Relative to the chosen angle θ, the opposite side lies across from the angle, the adjacent side touches the angle but is not the hypotenuse, and the hypotenuse lies across from the 90° corner. The ratios are sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse, cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse, and tan θ = opposite/adjacent. The memory aid SOH–CAH–TOA summarizes those relationships.

Choosing the right ratio

If you know an angle and the ramp length and want the vertical rise, use sine because it relates opposite and hypotenuse. If you know the angle and ramp length and want the horizontal run, use cosine because it relates adjacent and hypotenuse. If you know the angle and horizontal run and want the vertical rise, use tangent because it relates opposite and adjacent. Choosing the ratio is a reasoning step, not a guessing game.

These ratios do not have units because each divides one length by another length. When solving for a side, the resulting side still has units of length. For example, multiplying a 4 m ramp length by sin θ produces a height in meters.

YOU ARE THE ENGINEER

Positioning an angled machine support

A support arm is 2.0 m long and must be installed at 30° above the horizontal. Your installation drawing needs the vertical height reached and the horizontal distance occupied. Because the arm is the hypotenuse, use sine for rise: 2.0 sin(30°) = 1.0 m. Use cosine for run: 2.0 cos(30°) ≈ 1.73 m. If you instead knew the horizontal run and angle, tangent would give the rise. Choosing the ratio depends on which dimensions are known, not on which formula seems familiar.

SECTION 08

Worked Trigonometry: Find Rise and Run

Example A — Find the rise using sine

A straight ramp is 4.0 m long and makes an angle of 15° with the horizontal floor.

  1. Step 1 — Identify: Hypotenuse = 4.0 m, θ = 15°, unknown opposite (rise).
  2. Step 2 — Isolate: sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse; multiply both sides by hypotenuse to obtain rise = length × sin θ.
  3. Step 3 — Substitute and verify: rise = 4.0 × sin(15°) ≈ 4.0 × 0.258819 = 1.035 m. Check that 1.035/4.0 ≈ 0.2588. Because the ramp slopes upward at a relatively small angle, the rise should be much less than the ramp length.

Example B — Find the horizontal run using cosine

For the same 4.0 m ramp at 15°,

  1. Step 1 — Identify: Unknown adjacent (run).
  2. Step 2 — Isolate: cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse, so run = length × cos θ.
  3. Step 3 — Substitute and verify: run = 4.0 × cos(15°) ≈ 3.864 m. Check using the Pythagorean theorem: 1.035² + 3.864² ≈ 4.0², allowing for rounding. The run is slightly shorter than the sloped length, as expected.

Example C — Find rise from run using tangent

A support must rise from a floor at 12° over a horizontal run of 2.5 m.

  1. Step 1 — Identify: Adjacent = 2.5 m, θ = 12°, unknown opposite.
  2. Step 2 — Isolate: tan θ = opposite/adjacent, so rise = run × tan θ.
  3. Step 3 — Substitute and verify: rise = 2.5 × tan(12°) ≈ 0.531 m. Check that rise/run ≈ 0.2126 = tan(12°). This problem uses run rather than ramp length; substituting 2.5 into the sine formula would answer a different question.

Reasonableness: For an acute angle, both rise and run are shorter than the hypotenuse. If your answer for the rise exceeds the ramp length, recheck the selected ratio, calculator mode, and input values.

YOU ARE THE ENGINEER

Checking clearance beneath an inclined conveyor

An inclined conveyor has a straight 5.0 m section at 12° above horizontal. You must determine how high its end will be above the starting elevation so it does not collide with overhead equipment. Step 1: Identify the conveyor length as the hypotenuse and the vertical rise as the unknown. Step 2: Use rise = length × sin(angle). Step 3: Rise = 5.0 sin(12°) ≈ 1.04 m. That is the elevation change, not necessarily the conveyor's total height above the floor. Actual clearance checks must include the equipment's thickness and mounting height.

SECTION 09

Inverse Trigonometry: Find an Angle

Sometimes the lengths are known but the angle is not. In that case, use an inverse trigonometric function. The inverse tangent, written tan⁻¹ or arctan, converts the opposite/adjacent ratio into an angle. It does not mean 1/tan θ. Most scientific calculators provide inverse trigonometric functions through a shift or second-function key.

Three-step angle example

A ramp rises 0.60 m over a horizontal run of 4.0 m. Find its angle above the floor.

  1. Step 1 — Identify: Opposite = 0.60 m, adjacent = 4.0 m, unknown θ.
  2. Step 2 — Isolate: tan θ = rise/run; apply arctan to both sides to obtain θ = arctan(rise/run).
  3. Step 3 — Substitute and verify: θ = arctan(0.60/4.0) = arctan(0.15) ≈ 8.53°. Check: tan(8.53°) ≈ 0.15. The ratio has no units, while the answer is in degrees.

You can also use inverse sine when opposite and hypotenuse are known, or inverse cosine when adjacent and hypotenuse are known. For example, if a brace rises 1.0 m over a sloped length of 2.0 m, sin θ = 1/2 and θ = arcsin(0.5) = 30°. Select the inverse function that corresponds to the two measured sides.

Angle and slope are different specifications

Suppose a design calls for a slope ratio of 1:12. That means 1 unit of rise for every 12 units of horizontal run. Its angle is arctan(1/12) ≈ 4.76°, not 12° and not 1°. In real projects, applicable accessibility and building requirements must be checked separately; this calculation only converts between geometry descriptions.

YOU ARE THE ENGINEER

Determining a bracket's installation angle

A bracket must connect two points separated by 0.60 m vertically and 2.0 m horizontally. The fabrication drawing needs the angle of the bracket above horizontal. You know the opposite and adjacent sides, so use inverse tangent: θ = arctan(0.60/2.0) = arctan(0.30) ≈ 16.7°. That angle tells the installation team how to orient the bracket. If the same drawing needs the bracket's straight-line span, use the Pythagorean theorem: √(0.60² + 2.0²) ≈ 2.09 m.

SECTION 11

Engineering Checks and Common Mistakes

Engineering calculations must satisfy both mathematical rules and physical constraints. Start by checking that every dimension uses compatible units. A rectangle 100 cm by 0.50 m is not 50 m²: convert 100 cm to 1.00 m, then compute 1.00 × 0.50 = 0.50 m². Similarly, a cylinder's height and radius must use consistent length units before volume is calculated.

For circles, check whether a drawing labels radius or diameter. For triangles, confirm that the stated height is perpendicular to the base. For right triangles, check that the angle really is 90° before using the Pythagorean theorem. For trigonometry, identify the angle being used and then relabel opposite and adjacent relative to it. A leg that is opposite one acute angle is adjacent to the other.

Worked error analysis

A student finds the area of a circular hole with diameter 0.30 m by calculating π(0.30)² = 0.283 m². The error is treating diameter as radius. The correct radius is 0.15 m, so A = π(0.15)² ≈ 0.0707 m². The incorrect answer is four times the correct one.

Another student finds the rise of a 5.0 m ramp at 10° by calculating 5.0 × tan(10°) ≈ 0.882 m. Tangent requires horizontal run, but the 5.0 m measurement is the hypotenuse. The correct formula is rise = 5.0 × sin(10°) ≈ 0.868 m. The two numbers are close because the angle is small, which makes this mistake particularly difficult to catch without checking the diagram.

Finally, consider a brace with legs 6 m and 8 m. The hypotenuse cannot be 2 m, even though 8 − 6 = 2. It must be longer than either leg. The Pythagorean theorem gives √(36 + 64) = 10 m. Use physical reasonableness as an independent test of the algebra.

SECTION 12

Independent Practice with Worked Solutions

For each problem, sketch the geometry, label known measurements, identify the unknown, choose a relationship, rearrange if necessary, substitute, and verify. Try each problem before opening its solution.

1. Panel perimeter

Problem: A 2.5 m by 0.8 m panel needs edge trim. How much?

Solution: P = 2L + 2w = 2(2.5) + 2(0.8) = 6.6 m. Units are length, not area.

2. Triangle area

Problem: A bracket has base 0.90 m and perpendicular height 0.40 m. Find area.

Solution: A = ½bh = ½(0.90)(0.40) = 0.18 m².

3. Circle circumference

Problem: A wheel has diameter 0.60 m. Find circumference.

Solution: C = πD = π(0.60) ≈ 1.885 m.

4. Circle area

Problem: A circular plate has radius 0.25 m. Find area.

Solution: A = πr² = π(0.25)² ≈ 0.1963 m².

5. Block volume

Problem: A block measures 0.50 m by 0.30 m by 0.20 m. Find volume.

Solution: V = Lwh = 0.50 × 0.30 × 0.20 = 0.030 m³.

6. Brace length

Problem: A right-triangle brace spans 5 m horizontally and 12 m vertically. Find its length.

Solution: c = √(a²+b²) = √(25+144) = 13 m.

7. Missing leg

Problem: A right triangle has hypotenuse 10 m and one leg 6 m. Find the other.

Solution: b = √(c²−a²) = √(100−36) = 8 m.

8. Ramp rise

Problem: A 6.0 m ramp is angled 20° above horizontal. Find rise.

Solution: rise = 6.0 sin(20°) ≈ 2.052 m.

9. Ramp run

Problem: For the same 6.0 m ramp at 20°, find horizontal run.

Solution: run = 6.0 cos(20°) ≈ 5.638 m. Check rise² + run² ≈ 36.

10. Find angle

Problem: A ramp rises 0.75 m over a horizontal run of 5.0 m. Find angle.

Solution: θ = arctan(0.75/5.0) = arctan(0.15) ≈ 8.53°.

11. Find height from tangent

Problem: A support is angled 25° with a 2.0 m horizontal run. Find rise.

Solution: rise = 2.0 tan(25°) ≈ 0.933 m.

12. Cylinder height

Problem: A cylinder has volume 0.0314 m³ and radius 0.10 m. Find height.

Solution: h = V/(πr²) = 0.0314/[π(0.10)²] ≈ 0.999 m, approximately 1.00 m.

Once you can solve these without looking at the worked solutions, return to the interactive lab and make your own numerical examples. Explain how you would verify each result using the original geometric relationship.

REFERENCE

Lesson Glossary

Select a term to reveal its definition. Use these terms when explaining your calculations or labeling diagrams.

Perimeter

Total distance around a two-dimensional shape; measured in length units.

Area

Amount of two-dimensional surface inside a boundary; measured in squared length units.

Volume

Amount of three-dimensional space occupied; measured in cubed length units.

Radius

Distance from the center of a circle to its edge.

Diameter

Distance across a circle through its center; twice the radius.

Circumference

Distance around a circle.

Pi (π)

A mathematical constant approximately equal to 3.14159; circumference divided by diameter.

Right triangle

A triangle containing one 90° angle.

Legs

The two sides that form the 90° angle of a right triangle.

Hypotenuse

The side opposite the right angle; the longest side in a right triangle.

Pythagorean theorem

For a right triangle, the sum of the squares of the legs equals the square of the hypotenuse.

Reference angle

The angle used to determine which triangle side is opposite or adjacent.

Opposite side

The side across from the selected reference angle.

Adjacent side

The leg touching the selected reference angle, excluding the hypotenuse.

Sine

Opposite side divided by hypotenuse for a right-triangle reference angle.

Cosine

Adjacent side divided by hypotenuse for a right-triangle reference angle.

Tangent

Opposite side divided by adjacent side for a right-triangle reference angle.

Inverse tangent (arctan)

The function that finds an angle from its tangent ratio.

Rise

Vertical height gained between two points.

Run

Horizontal distance between two points.

Grade

Rise divided by horizontal run, often expressed as a percentage.

Degree mode

Calculator setting for angles measured in degrees rather than radians.

UNIT TEST + FINAL EXAM SKILLS

Reading a Dimensioned Drawing: Plate Area with Holes

Mechanical drawings specify sizes using dimension lines and labels. A drawing may be not to scale, so never estimate a length by measuring the picture. For a flat plate with circular holes, calculate the rectangular face area first, then subtract the areas of the holes. Use the diameter given on the drawing to find each radius before applying A = πr².

Worked drawing — mounting plate with two holes:
  1. Read the drawing: Plate = 180 mm × 100 mm; two holes each have diameter 20 mm.
  2. Plate area: 180 × 100 = 18,000 mm².
  3. Radius of each hole: 20 ÷ 2 = 10 mm.
  4. Combined hole area: 2 × π × 10² ≈ 628.3 mm².
  5. Remaining face area: 18,000 − 628.3 = 17,371.7 mm².
  6. Interpretation: This is the remaining flat face area, not a complete assessment of plate strength.

Independent practice: A 150 mm × 80 mm plate contains three circular holes, each 10 mm in diameter. Calculate the remaining face area using π ≈ 3.14.

Show worked solution

Plate area = 150 × 80 = 12,000 mm². Radius = 10/2 = 5 mm. Removed area = 3 × 3.14 × 5² = 235.5 mm². Remaining area = 11,764.5 mm².

APPLY WHAT YOU LEARNED

Additional Practice: Dimensioned Drawings and Composite Geometry

Engineering diagrams are often labeled not to scale. This means the shape is a visual guide, but distances must come from the written dimensions rather than measuring the picture. When a hole is cut through a flat plate, the remaining face area equals the plate area minus the hole area. For a circular hole with diameter d, radius r = d/2 and area = πr².

Worked example — mounting plate with one hole:
  1. Plate dimensions: 200 mm × 100 mm. Hole diameter: 20 mm. Use π = 3.14.
  2. Plate area = 200 × 100 = 20,000 mm².
  3. Hole radius = 20 ÷ 2 = 10 mm.
  4. Hole area = 3.14 × 10² = 314 mm².
  5. Remaining face area = 20,000 − 314 = 19,686 mm².
Worked comparison — one large hole versus two smaller holes:
  1. Both plates are 240 mm × 150 mm, so each starts with 36,000 mm².
  2. Design A: one 40 mm-diameter hole has radius 20 mm and area 3.14 × 20² = 1,256 mm².
  3. Design B: two 20 mm-diameter holes each have radius 10 mm, so total removed area = 2 × 3.14 × 10² = 628 mm².
  4. Remaining area: A = 34,744 mm²; B = 35,372 mm².
  5. Decision: Design B retains 628 mm² more face area. This alone does not establish which design is structurally stronger, because hole locations and stresses also matter.

For a right-triangle support, label the angle before choosing a trigonometric ratio. If the cable length is the hypotenuse and the attachment height is opposite the ground angle, use height = cable length × sin(angle). If the known horizontal distance is adjacent to the angle, use height = horizontal distance × tan(angle).

Worked example — angled cable:
  1. Cable length = 10 m; angle with level ground = 60°.
  2. Height is opposite the angle; cable is the hypotenuse.
  3. sin(60°) = height/10.
  4. Height = 10 × sin(60°) ≈ 8.66 m.
  5. Check: height is less than the 10 m cable, as expected.

Try it: A 12 m cable rises at 30° above level ground. What is its attachment height?

Show reasoning

Height = 12 sin(30°) = 12 × 0.5 = 6 m.
