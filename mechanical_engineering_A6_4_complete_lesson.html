Prerequisite support

Math and vocabulary checkpoint

Read material-failure vocabulary carefully: Yielding is the onset of permanent deformation; ultimate tensile strength is the maximum engineering stress reached in a tensile test; fracture is separation of the material. Fatigue is progressive damage from repeated loading. These are different events, so a design that has not fractured may still have yielded or suffered fatigue damage.

Section 01

Engineering failure is more than a broken part

Imagine a packaging machine whose steel lifting bracket has begun to bend permanently. The bracket has not fractured, but the guide rollers no longer align. A mechanical engineer cannot call this part successful simply because it is still in one piece. In mechanical engineering, failure can mean loss of function through excessive elastic deformation, permanent yielding, fracture, fatigue cracking, instability, or wear.

To investigate the bracket, an engineer asks what loads it carries, whether the load is repeated, what material and geometry were specified, where cracks began, and whether the part experienced overload. A tensile test provides one important set of evidence: the material's engineering stress–strain curve. The curve does not describe every failure mode, but it helps engineers distinguish recoverable deformation, yielding, and ultimate tensile behavior.

Section 02

How engineers obtain a stress–strain curve

In a tensile test, a prepared specimen is gripped in a testing machine and pulled at a controlled rate. The machine measures applied force, while an extensometer or another measurement system records extension over a known gauge length. Engineers convert force to engineering stress using the original cross-sectional area and convert extension to engineering strain using the original gauge length.

The resulting curve is a map of the specimen's response under the test conditions. The initial slope helps estimate Young's modulus. Other features can indicate yielding, maximum engineering tensile stress, and fracture. The curve is specific to the tested material condition and loading environment; it should not be treated as a universal curve for all steels, aluminum alloys, or polymers.

Connection to A6.2 and A6.3: Engineering stress = F/A₀; engineering strain = ΔL/L₀. The original area and length are used in these introductory definitions.
Section 03

Read the key regions of the curve

In the initial linear elastic region, stress is approximately proportional to strain. Unloading in this region allows the specimen to return approximately to its original dimensions. When stress reaches the material's yield strength (or an agreed offset-yield value), permanent plastic deformation becomes significant. Beyond yielding, some metals continue carrying increasing engineering stress through strain hardening.

The highest engineering stress on a conventional tensile curve is the ultimate tensile strength (UTS). In a ductile metal specimen, localized necking may begin near this point. The engineering stress may then decrease until fracture. Ultimate tensile strength and fracture stress are not automatically the same value. Also, a part can be unfit for service well before reaching either value.

Section 04

Elastic recovery and plastic deformation

Suppose a tie rod is loaded within its elastic range. When the load is removed, its extension is approximately recovered. If it is loaded beyond its yield condition, unloading may recover some elastic strain but leave a permanent strain. That permanent change can interfere with the fit of a machine, even when the rod remains intact.

Worked engineering example: A bracket material has an illustrative yield strength of 250 MPa. A simplified uniform region of the bracket experiences 180 MPa. Under a yield-based screening comparison, 180 MPa is below 250 MPa. If the region experiences 280 MPa, it exceeds the stated yield strength and plastic deformation becomes a concern. Neither comparison alone certifies the entire bracket: geometry, multiaxial loading, stress concentrations, fatigue, and safety factors still matter.

Section 05

Yield strength versus ultimate tensile strength

Mechanical engineers must not substitute ultimate tensile strength for yield strength when the design requirement is to avoid permanent deformation. Yield strength is relevant to the onset of specified plastic deformation; UTS is the maximum engineering tensile stress reached in a tensile test. A ductile metal can yield at a much lower stress than its UTS.

Worked example: A material data sheet lists yield strength = 280 MPa and UTS = 440 MPa. A machine link sees 320 MPa nominal axial stress. The stress is below UTS, but above yield strength. The link may plastically deform and lose dimensional accuracy. The correct conclusion is not “safe because 320 is less than 440.” It fails a no-yield screening requirement.

Some materials do not exhibit a sharply defined yield point. Engineers may use a specified 0.2% offset yield strength or another standard measure. Always read the property definition supplied with the test data.

Section 06

Ductile and brittle fracture in machine components

A ductile tensile specimen can undergo appreciable plastic deformation before fracture. It may stretch, neck, or show other visible changes. A brittle specimen can fracture with little plastic deformation. Many ceramics are brittle under tensile loading; certain metals can also exhibit brittle behavior depending on temperature, processing, flaws, and loading conditions.

Consider two protective machine components. A ductile metal support may bend before breaking, giving an opportunity to detect damage. A hard ceramic wear insert may resist abrasion and heat yet crack suddenly under an impact. Neither material family is universally better. The engineer matches the failure behavior to the service conditions and provides appropriate inspection and protection.

Section 07

Fatigue: failure under repeated loading

A rotating shaft may experience millions of loading cycles during its service life. Even when the peak nominal stress is below the material's monotonic yield strength, repeated loading can initiate and grow a crack. This process is called fatigue. Fatigue cracks can begin at surface scratches, keyways, sharp corners, holes, or other locations where stress is concentrated.

Engineers distinguish a one-time static overload from repeated cyclic loading. For a rotating shaft, the design may require improved fillet radii, surface finish, material selection, stress reduction, inspection intervals, or fatigue-specific analysis. Simply checking that the maximum stress is below UTS is not enough.

Engineering example: A motor shaft repeatedly bends as it rotates. A crack begins at a sharp keyway corner, grows during service, and eventually leaves too little intact material to support the load. The final break may look sudden, but the damage accumulated over many cycles. This is why maintenance and geometry are part of engineering design.

Section 08

Stress concentrations and geometry

The average axial stress formula σ = F/A is a useful starting point, but it does not capture the local peak stresses near holes, notches, shoulders, or abrupt changes in cross section. These features can create a stress concentration. Engineers often use a stress-concentration factor Kₜ to describe the ratio of peak elastic stress to a specified nominal stress: Kₜ = σmax/σnominal.

Worked example: A notched component has a nominal stress of 60 MPa and an illustrative elastic stress-concentration factor of 2.5. The estimated local peak elastic stress is 2.5 × 60 = 150 MPa. This simplified elastic estimate does not automatically predict fracture or fatigue life, but it warns the engineer that the nominal stress may understate local demand.

Replacing a sharp corner with a suitable radius can reduce stress concentration in many geometries. The actual reduction depends on dimensions, loading, and manufacturing details and should be verified rather than guessed.

Section 09

Failure investigation: a cracked lifting bracket

A lifting bracket has a crack emerging from a bolt hole. A mechanical engineer documents the crack location, load history, geometry, material specification, manufacturing condition, and inspection records. The engineer considers several hypotheses: a single overload, cyclic fatigue, an unexpected impact, corrosion-assisted damage, or a combination of mechanisms.

Evidence matters. A history of many load cycles and a crack beginning at a geometric discontinuity may support a fatigue hypothesis, but does not prove it without further investigation. A permanently bent bracket suggests plastic deformation occurred somewhere, yet bending alone does not establish the precise load that caused it. Engineers use measured data, drawings, testing, and appropriate analysis to avoid unsupported conclusions.

The practical design question is not only “what broke?” It is “what requirement was violated, why did it happen, and what change would reduce the risk of recurrence?”

Section 10

Worked data interpretation: identify the failure landmarks

A teaching tensile-test dataset reports an initial straight-line region, then the following illustrative landmarks: a yield strength of 250 MPa, a maximum engineering tensile stress of 410 MPa, and fracture after substantial elongation. These values describe a hypothetical ductile metal, not a certified alloy.

If a candidate machine link is predicted to reach 230 MPa, it remains below the stated yield value in a simple comparison. At 275 MPa, it exceeds the yield value even though it remains below the 410 MPa UTS. At 430 MPa, it exceeds both listed values. Each comparison is a screening observation, not a complete safety analysis.

Students should be able to locate the initial elastic slope, yielding, UTS, and fracture on a graph, and explain what each means for an engineering design. They should also recognize that the point of maximum engineering stress is not necessarily the point of final fracture.

Step-by-step method · Interpret tensile-test landmarks

  1. Step 1: Find the initial linear elastic region on the stress–strain curve.
  2. Step 2: Read yield strength 250 MPa and UTS 410 MPa.
  3. Step 3: 230 MPa is below yield in this simplified comparison.
  4. Step 4: 275 MPa exceeds yield but is below UTS; plastic deformation is possible.
  5. Step 5: 430 MPa exceeds both listed values.
  6. Step 6: The UTS peak is not necessarily the fracture point; screening is not full safety verification.

Section 12

Independent engineering practice

Attempt each question before expanding the worked solution. State the engineering significance of the answer.

1. A metal has yield strength 260 MPa and UTS 430 MPa. A link reaches 300 MPa. Has it passed a no-yield check?

No. 300 MPa exceeds the 260 MPa yield strength even though it is below UTS. Permanent deformation is a concern.

2. A tensile curve rises to 450 MPa, then drops before fracture. What is 450 MPa?

It is the ultimate tensile strength, the maximum engineering stress reached during the test.

3. A specimen returns to its original length after unloading. What behavior was observed?

Approximately elastic deformation.

4. A rod retains permanent elongation after unloading. What behavior occurred?

Plastic deformation occurred.

5. A shaft fractures after millions of repeated cycles. Name a likely mechanism.

Fatigue is a plausible mechanism. A proper failure investigation is still required.

6. Nominal stress is 75 MPa and Kₜ = 2.0. Estimate local peak elastic stress.

σmax = Kₜσnom = 2 × 75 = 150 MPa, within the elastic concentration approximation.

7. Why is a brittle ceramic insert not automatically unsuitable?

It may provide valuable heat and wear resistance; the design must account for impact and crack sensitivity.

8. A crack starts at a sharp keyway corner. Name two engineering responses.

Investigate a suitable fillet radius and reduce cyclic stress; also consider material, finish, inspection, and fatigue analysis.

9. Why should engineers not use UTS alone to prevent permanent deformation?

Yielding can begin at stresses below UTS. A no-permanent-deformation requirement must use the relevant yield criterion.

10. What evidence would help distinguish a single overload from fatigue?

Load history, fracture-surface examination, crack origin, geometry, material testing, and inspection records.

Section 13

Vocabulary glossary

Select a term to expand its engineering definition.

Yield strength

Stress associated with the onset of a specified amount of plastic deformation.

Ultimate tensile strength (UTS)

Maximum engineering tensile stress recorded during a tensile test.

Fracture

Separation of material into pieces through crack growth or failure.

Elastic deformation

Reversible deformation recovered on unloading.

Plastic deformation

Permanent deformation remaining after unloading.

Ductility

Ability to undergo substantial plastic deformation before fracture.

Brittleness

Fracture with relatively little plastic deformation.

Necking

Localized reduction in a tensile specimen's cross section, often after UTS in ductile metals.

Fatigue

Damage accumulation and crack growth under repeated or fluctuating loading.

Stress concentration

Localized increase in stress near a discontinuity such as a hole or notch.

Stress-concentration factor

Ratio Kₜ = σmax/σnominal for a specified elastic geometry and loading case.

Strain hardening

Increase in resistance to further plastic deformation during continued straining.

0.2% offset yield strength

Conventional yield measure used when a clear yield point is absent.

Failure analysis

Systematic investigation of how and why a component lost its intended function.

Section 15

Lesson summary

Mechanical engineers interpret stress–strain curves to identify elastic behavior, yielding, ultimate tensile strength, and fracture. Yielding can cause a component to fail its functional requirement before it breaks. Ductile and brittle materials offer different failure characteristics; neither can be judged by a single property. Repeated loading can cause fatigue, and geometric discontinuities can increase local stress.

A sound engineering recommendation connects material behavior to component geometry, operating loads, failure mechanisms, and evidence. In A6.5, you will apply allowable stress, safety factors, and shear checks to choose between candidate machine designs.
