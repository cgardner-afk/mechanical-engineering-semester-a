Learning target: Distinguish mass and weight and calculate gravitational force.

SECTION 01

A crane lifts a machine

Imagine a construction crew lifting a heavy machine onto a platform. Before the lift, an engineer must check whether the crane, cable, and other equipment can safely hold it. The machine's mass is listed in kilograms, but the cable must support a force measured in newtons. The engineer needs to understand how these two measurements are connected.

In everyday conversation, people often use mass and weight to mean the same thing. Engineers cannot do that. A machine has the same mass on Earth and on the Moon, but it weighs less on the Moon because gravity is weaker there. Confusing kilograms with newtons could lead an engineer to choose equipment that cannot safely support a load.

In this lesson, you will learn what forces are, how mass differs from weight, and how to calculate the force of gravity. Then you will use a simulator to see what happens when you change an object's mass or the strength of gravity.

SECTION 02

A force is an interaction

A force is a push or pull. A cable pulls up on a hanging machine. A floor pushes up on a crate. Gravity pulls objects toward Earth. Every force has a magnitude, meaning its size, and a direction, meaning which way it acts. Engineers show forces with arrows that point in the direction of the force.

The SI unit of force is the newton (N). One newton is enough force to give a 1 kg object an acceleration of 1 m/s². In symbols, 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². We will study this relationship more closely in Lesson A4.2. For now, remember: kilograms measure mass, and newtons measure force.

An object can experience forces even when it is not moving. For example, a cable pulls up on a hanging load while gravity pulls down. If those forces balance, the load stays still. When analyzing a machine, engineers must identify all the forces acting on it—not just whether it moves.

SECTION 03

Mass measures matter and inertia

Mass tells us how much an object resists a change in its motion. This resistance is called inertia. Mass is also closely related to how much matter an object contains. We measure mass in kilograms (kg). If you take a 12 kg steel part from Earth to the Moon, it is still a 12 kg part. Changing its location does not change its mass.

Think about pushing two carts, one empty and one loaded with heavy boxes. With the same push, the loaded cart is harder to speed up. It has more mass and more inertia. Mass also affects an object's weight, but mass and weight are not the same thing.

Remember: Mass is measured in kilograms and stays the same when the object changes location. Weight is a force, measured in newtons, and can change when the local gravitational acceleration changes.
SECTION 04

Weight is the force of gravity

Gravity is a force of attraction between objects that have mass. Near Earth's surface, gravity causes a freely falling object to speed up downward by about 9.8 m/s each second, if we ignore air resistance. We write this acceleration as 9.8 m/s² and use the letter g for it. The exact value varies slightly from place to place, but 9.8 m/s² works well for most introductory problems.

An object's weight is the force of gravity pulling on it. Two things determine weight: the object's mass and the strength of gravity where it is located. Near Earth's surface, weight acts downward. We calculate it with this equation:

W = m × g
SymbolMeaningSI unit
WWeight (gravitational force)newtons (N)
mMasskilograms (kg)
gLocal gravitational accelerationmeters per second squared (m/s²)

Check the units: kilograms multiplied by meters per second squared gives kg·m/s². That is the same as a newton. This is why weight is measured in newtons, not kilograms.

You may have seen a bathroom scale display a number in kilograms. The scale actually responds to the force pressing on it, then uses Earth's gravity to estimate mass. In engineering, it is important to know whether a measurement represents mass or force.

SECTION 05

Calculate weight step by step

To calculate weight, first find the object's mass and the value of g. Next, write the equation W = mg and put the numbers in the correct places. Finally, multiply and label your answer in newtons. Following these steps helps you avoid confusing kilograms with newtons on engineering problems and assessments.

Worked example 1 · Machine component on Earth

  1. Identify the known values: mass m = 7.5 kg; g = 9.8 m/s².
  2. Write the equation: W = mg.
  3. Substitute: W = (7.5 kg)(9.8 m/s²).
  4. Multiply: W = 73.5 kg·m/s².
  5. Express the correct unit: W = 73.5 N downward.
  6. Reasonableness check: Each kilogram corresponds to about 9.8 N of weight near Earth, so 7.5 kg should weigh a little under 75 N.

Worked example 2 · A heavier industrial load

  1. Given: m = 32 kg; g = 9.8 m/s².
  2. Equation: W = mg.
  3. Substitute: W = 32 × 9.8.
  4. Calculate: W = 313.6 N downward.
  5. Interpret: A stationary, vertically suspended load would require a total upward supporting force of 313.6 N in this simplified situation. Real lifting systems also require allowances for acceleration and safety.
Your turn:

A 4.2 kg sensor package is supported near Earth, where g = 9.8 m/s². Calculate its weight. State both magnitude and direction.

Reveal the worked solution

W = mg = (4.2 kg)(9.8 m/s²) = 41.16 N downward. The unit is N because kg·m/s² = N.

SECTION 06

Solve for mass when weight is known

Sometimes an engineer knows an object's weight but needs to find its mass. For example, a force sensor might show a reading in newtons. We can use the same equation, W = mg, but rearrange it so mass is by itself. Divide both sides by g to get m = W/g. This is the same algebra skill you practiced in Unit A3.

m = W ÷ g

Worked example 3 · Find mass from a scale reading

  1. Known: weight W = 343 N; gravitational acceleration g = 9.8 m/s².
  2. Start: W = mg.
  3. Divide both sides by g: W/g = m.
  4. Substitute: m = 343 N ÷ 9.8 m/s².
  5. Calculate: m = 35 kg.
  6. Check: 35 × 9.8 = 343 N. The answer is in kilograms, not newtons.
Your turn:

A suspended component exerts a gravitational force of 588 N where g = 9.8 m/s². What is its mass?

Reveal the worked solution

m = W/g = 588 ÷ 9.8 = 60 kg. Check: 60 × 9.8 = 588 N.

SECTION 07

The same object under different gravity

Gravity is not equally strong everywhere. Near the Moon's surface, g is about 1.62 m/s². Near Mars's surface, it is about 3.71 m/s². An object's mass stays the same when it moves between these places, but its weight changes because gravity changes.

Imagine taking a 20 kg scientific instrument from Earth to the Moon. On Earth, it weighs 20 × 9.8 = 196 N. On the Moon, it weighs about 20 × 1.62 = 32.4 N. The instrument is still 20 kg, but gravity pulls on it with less force on the Moon.

LocationApproximate g (m/s²)Weight of 20 kg object
Earth9.80196 N
Moon1.6232.4 N
Mars3.7174.2 N

If gravity is cut in half, an object's weight is cut in half too, as long as its mass stays the same. If gravity doubles, its weight doubles. The equation W = mg shows this relationship. Remember that a change in weight does not mean the object's mass has changed.

Worked example 4 · Payload on another world

  1. Given: payload mass = 45 kg; local g = 4.0 m/s².
  2. Write: W = mg.
  3. Substitute: W = 45 × 4.0.
  4. Calculate: W = 180 N downward.
  5. Compare: On Earth, its weight would be 45 × 9.8 = 441 N, but its mass would still be 45 kg.
SECTION 08

Why the distinction matters in design

Engineers need mass and weight for different reasons. Mass helps them predict how hard it will be to start, stop, or speed up a load. Weight tells them how much gravitational force a support must hold. If a crane starts or stops suddenly, the cable may experience additional forces. The basic equation W = mg does not include those extra forces.

Suppose a platform must hold a 60 kg motor. Near Earth, the motor weighs 60 × 9.8 = 588 N. That is an important starting number, but it does not tell us whether the platform is safe. Engineers also need to consider the platform's own weight, where the motor sits, the strength of the materials, how parts are connected, and an appropriate safety margin.

Engineering limitation: W = mg calculates gravitational force for a specified local g. It does not by itself calculate cable tension during acceleration, contact forces in a complex structure, or the maximum safe load of a machine. Always identify what the equation does—and does not—describe.

SECTION 10

Practice without the simulator

Try these problems on your own before opening the answers. The numbers are different from the worked examples, but the method is the same. Decide whether you need W = mg or m = W/g, show your steps, and include the correct units.

Problem A · Calculate gravitational force

A 13 kg machine housing rests on a support near Earth. Using g = 9.8 m/s², determine the gravitational force acting on the housing.

Worked answer

W = mg = 13 × 9.8 = 127.4 N downward.

Problem B · Find the unknown mass

A hanging tool has a weight of 78.4 N where g = 9.8 m/s². Find its mass.

Worked answer

m = W/g = 78.4 ÷ 9.8 = 8 kg.

Problem C · Change locations

A 24 kg exploration instrument is moved to a world where g = 3.5 m/s². What are its mass and weight there? How do they compare with its values on Earth?

Worked answer

Mass remains 24 kg. New weight = 24 × 3.5 = 84 N. On Earth it weighs 24 × 9.8 = 235.2 N. Weight changes because g changes; mass does not.

Problem D · Explain the engineering error

A student writes, “The lifting force is 25 kg.” What is wrong with the statement, and what additional information is needed to calculate the gravitational force on a 25 kg object?

Worked answer

Kilograms measure mass, not force. The lifting force should be reported in newtons. To calculate weight, the student needs local gravitational acceleration g. Near Earth, W = 25 × 9.8 = 245 N downward; a stationary vertical support would balance that weight in the simplified model.

VOCABULARY & GLOSSARY

Key engineering terms

Force

A push or pull with magnitude and direction, measured in newtons (N).

Mass

A measure of inertia, commonly associated with the amount of matter in an object; measured in kilograms (kg).

Weight

The gravitational force acting on an object; W = mg and measured in newtons (N).

Gravitational acceleration (g)

The acceleration due to local gravity, approximately 9.8 m/s² near Earth's surface.

Inertia

An object's resistance to changes in velocity; greater mass means greater inertia.

Newton (N)

The SI unit of force: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

Lesson summary

Mass and weight are related, but they are not interchangeable. Mass is measured in kilograms and represents inertia; weight is a gravitational force measured in newtons. The equation W = mg connects mass with local gravitational acceleration. Rearranging it gives m = W/g. When an object moves to a location with different gravity, its mass stays the same while its weight changes. Engineers use these ideas to estimate gravitational loads, while recognizing that real structures and moving machines may experience additional forces.
