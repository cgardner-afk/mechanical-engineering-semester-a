CURRICULUM ALIGNMENT

Semester objective A-O4: Analyze forces and motion using Newton's laws, vectors, and free-body diagrams.

Unit essential question: How do forces determine the motion or equilibrium of mechanical systems?

Learning target: Distinguish mass and weight and calculate gravitational force.

Objectives: Define force and its SI unit; distinguish mass, weight, and inertia; use W = mg to calculate gravitational force; rearrange the formula to find mass; compare weight under different gravitational accelerations; and interpret gravitational loading in a realistic engineering situation.

SECTION 01

A crane lifts a machine

Imagine a construction crew installing a heavy machine on an elevated platform. Before lifting the machine, the engineer must decide whether the crane, cable, and lifting hardware can safely support the load. The equipment supplier describes the machine's mass in kilograms, but the cable experiences a pulling force measured in newtons. The engineer must connect those two quantities correctly.

In everyday conversation, people sometimes use mass and weight as though they mean the same thing. In engineering, that distinction matters. A machine can have the same mass in two different locations but exert a different gravitational force in each location. Using kilograms as though they were newtons can lead to a serious sizing error.

This lesson develops the language and mathematics needed to describe that gravitational load. You will begin with forces, learn what mass means, and use gravity to calculate weight. You will then test how changing mass and gravitational acceleration affects the force a support must carry.

SECTION 02

A force is an interaction

A force is a push or pull exerted through an interaction. A cable can pull on a hanging machine, a floor can push upward on a crate, and Earth can attract an object through gravity. A force has both magnitude (how large it is) and direction (where it acts). Engineers often draw forces as arrows: the arrow points in the force's direction, and its label states the magnitude.

The SI unit of force is the newton (N). One newton is the force that produces an acceleration of one meter per second squared on a one-kilogram mass: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². We will investigate that relationship in greater detail in Lesson A4.2. For now, remember that kilograms measure mass, while newtons measure force.

Forces do not always cause an object to move. A stationary load can experience large forces that balance each other. The key is to identify the forces acting on the particular object, not simply whether that object is moving.

SECTION 03

Mass measures matter and inertia

Mass describes a body's inertia—its resistance to changes in motion—and is commonly associated with how much matter it contains. Mass is measured in kilograms (kg). If an engineer moves a 12 kg steel component from Earth to the Moon, it remains a 12 kg component. Its location does not change the amount of material or its inertia.

Inertia is important when a machine starts, stops, or changes direction. A heavily loaded cart is generally harder to accelerate than an otherwise identical lightly loaded cart under the same net force. That relationship will become quantitative when we study Newton's second law. Mass also matters when calculating the gravitational force acting on an object, but it is not itself a force.

Remember: Mass is measured in kilograms and stays the same when the object changes location. Weight is a force, measured in newtons, and can change when the local gravitational acceleration changes.
SECTION 04

Weight is the force of gravity

Gravity is an attractive interaction between objects with mass. Near Earth's surface, gravity causes freely falling objects to accelerate downward at approximately 9.8 m/s², when air resistance is negligible. We represent this gravitational acceleration by the letter g. In introductory engineering problems near Earth, 9.8 m/s² is a standard approximation, although actual gravitational acceleration varies slightly by location.

An object's weight is the gravitational force acting on it. Weight depends on two quantities: the object's mass and the local gravitational acceleration. For the simple situations in this lesson, weight is directed downward toward the nearby planet or moon.

W = m × g
SymbolMeaningSI unit
WWeight (gravitational force)newtons (N)
mMasskilograms (kg)
gLocal gravitational accelerationmeters per second squared (m/s²)

Check the units before using the equation: kilograms multiplied by meters per second squared gives kg·m/s², which is a newton. The units show why a result for weight should be reported in newtons rather than kilograms.

In everyday life, a bathroom scale may display kilograms even though it senses a supporting force and converts that measurement using an assumed gravitational acceleration. Engineers must distinguish the displayed mass-equivalent reading from the underlying force.

SECTION 05

Calculate weight step by step

When solving a gravitational-force problem, begin by identifying the mass and the value of g specified in the question. Write the equation before substituting values. Then calculate and check that the result has the correct unit. This routine helps prevent mixing up mass and force, particularly when an assessment presents both kilograms and newtons as possible answers.

Worked example 1 · Machine component on Earth

  1. Identify the known values: mass m = 7.5 kg; g = 9.8 m/s².
  2. Write the equation: W = mg.
  3. Substitute: W = (7.5 kg)(9.8 m/s²).
  4. Multiply: W = 73.5 kg·m/s².
  5. Express the correct unit: W = 73.5 N downward.
  6. Reasonableness check: Each kilogram corresponds to about 9.8 N of weight near Earth, so 7.5 kg should weigh a little under 75 N.

Worked example 2 · A heavier industrial load

  1. Given: m = 32 kg; g = 9.8 m/s².
  2. Equation: W = mg.
  3. Substitute: W = 32 × 9.8.
  4. Calculate: W = 313.6 N downward.
  5. Interpret: A stationary, vertically suspended load would require a total upward supporting force of 313.6 N in this simplified situation. Real lifting systems also require allowances for acceleration and safety.
Your turn:

A 4.2 kg sensor package is supported near Earth, where g = 9.8 m/s². Calculate its weight. State both magnitude and direction.

Reveal the worked solution

W = mg = (4.2 kg)(9.8 m/s²) = 41.16 N downward. The unit is N because kg·m/s² = N.

SECTION 06

Solve for mass when weight is known

Engineers sometimes know the force a load exerts but not its mass. A force sensor or lifting scale may report a value in newtons. Because W = mg, you can find the mass by dividing both sides of the equation by g. This is the same equation-rearranging method used in Unit A3.

m = W ÷ g

Worked example 3 · Find mass from a scale reading

  1. Known: weight W = 343 N; gravitational acceleration g = 9.8 m/s².
  2. Start: W = mg.
  3. Divide both sides by g: W/g = m.
  4. Substitute: m = 343 N ÷ 9.8 m/s².
  5. Calculate: m = 35 kg.
  6. Check: 35 × 9.8 = 343 N. The answer is in kilograms, not newtons.
Your turn:

A suspended component exerts a gravitational force of 588 N where g = 9.8 m/s². What is its mass?

Reveal the worked solution

m = W/g = 588 ÷ 9.8 = 60 kg. Check: 60 × 9.8 = 588 N.

SECTION 07

The same object under different gravity

Gravitational acceleration is not the same everywhere. Near the Moon's surface, it is approximately 1.62 m/s²; near Mars's surface, approximately 3.71 m/s². These are useful approximate values for introductory calculations. An object's mass does not change simply because it moves between these locations, but its weight does because the value of g changes.

Suppose a spacecraft carries a 20 kg scientific instrument from Earth to the Moon. Its mass remains 20 kg. On Earth its weight is 20 × 9.8 = 196 N. On the Moon its weight is approximately 20 × 1.62 = 32.4 N. The instrument contains the same amount of matter and has the same inertia, but gravity pulls on it less strongly at the Moon's surface.

LocationApproximate g (m/s²)Weight of 20 kg object
Earth9.80196 N
Moon1.6232.4 N
Mars3.7174.2 N

If the same mass is taken to a place where g is half as large, its weight is also half as large. If g doubles, weight doubles. This direct relationship follows from W = mg when mass remains fixed. Do not conclude that mass becomes smaller when weight decreases; those are different physical quantities.

Worked example 4 · Payload on another world

  1. Given: payload mass = 45 kg; local g = 4.0 m/s².
  2. Write: W = mg.
  3. Substitute: W = 45 × 4.0.
  4. Calculate: W = 180 N downward.
  5. Compare: On Earth, its weight would be 45 × 9.8 = 441 N, but its mass would still be 45 kg.
SECTION 08

Why the distinction matters in design

A lifting engineer must understand which quantity is needed for each decision. Mass helps determine how difficult a payload is to accelerate. Weight tells the engineer the downward gravitational force that a stationary support must resist. A load rating may also account for forces caused by starting, stopping, swinging, or impact. These additional effects are not included in the simple stationary-load equation W = mg.

Consider a service platform that will hold a 60 kg motor. Near Earth, the motor's gravitational force is 60 × 9.8 = 588 N. That number gives an important starting point for evaluating the load, but it is not enough by itself to select a safe platform. An engineer would also consider the platform's own weight, how the load is distributed, possible movement, the strengths of the materials and connections, and an appropriate safety margin. Later units will introduce methods for analyzing those concerns.

Engineering limitation: W = mg calculates gravitational force for a specified local g. It does not by itself calculate cable tension during acceleration, contact forces in a complex structure, or the maximum safe load of a machine. Always identify what the equation does—and does not—describe.

SECTION 10

Practice without the simulator

Work through these problems before opening the solutions. They use unfamiliar values so you practice selecting and applying the method, not memorizing a particular example.

Problem A · Calculate gravitational force

A 13 kg machine housing rests on a support near Earth. Using g = 9.8 m/s², determine the gravitational force acting on the housing.

Worked answer

W = mg = 13 × 9.8 = 127.4 N downward.

Problem B · Find the unknown mass

A hanging tool has a weight of 78.4 N where g = 9.8 m/s². Find its mass.

Worked answer

m = W/g = 78.4 ÷ 9.8 = 8 kg.

Problem C · Change locations

A 24 kg exploration instrument is moved to a world where g = 3.5 m/s². What are its mass and weight there? How do they compare with its values on Earth?

Worked answer

Mass remains 24 kg. New weight = 24 × 3.5 = 84 N. On Earth it weighs 24 × 9.8 = 235.2 N. Weight changes because g changes; mass does not.

Problem D · Explain the engineering error

A student writes, “The lifting force is 25 kg.” What is wrong with the statement, and what additional information is needed to calculate the gravitational force on a 25 kg object?

Worked answer

Kilograms measure mass, not force. The lifting force should be reported in newtons. To calculate weight, the student needs local gravitational acceleration g. Near Earth, W = 25 × 9.8 = 245 N downward; a stationary vertical support would balance that weight in the simplified model.

VOCABULARY & GLOSSARY

Key engineering terms

Force

A push or pull with magnitude and direction, measured in newtons (N).

Mass

A measure of inertia, commonly associated with the amount of matter in an object; measured in kilograms (kg).

Weight

The gravitational force acting on an object; W = mg and measured in newtons (N).

Gravitational acceleration (g)

The acceleration due to local gravity, approximately 9.8 m/s² near Earth's surface.

Inertia

An object's resistance to changes in velocity; greater mass means greater inertia.

Newton (N)

The SI unit of force: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

Lesson summary

Mass and weight are related, but they are not interchangeable. Mass is measured in kilograms and represents inertia; weight is a gravitational force measured in newtons. The equation W = mg connects mass with local gravitational acceleration. Rearranging it gives m = W/g. When an object moves to a location with different gravity, its mass stays the same while its weight changes. Engineers use these ideas to estimate gravitational loads, while recognizing that real structures and moving machines may experience additional forces.
