Section 01

What is mechanical work?

Imagine a worker pushing a heavy crate across a warehouse floor. The worker applies a force, and the crate moves. Mechanical engineers describe the energy transferred by that force during the movement as work. Work is not the same as feeling tired. You might push hard against a wall and become exhausted, but if the wall does not move, your push does zero mechanical work on the wall.

In everyday conversation, work means effort. In engineering, work has a precise definition: a force must act on an object while the object undergoes displacement, and the force must have a component in the direction of that displacement. A force is a push or pull; a displacement is the change from starting position to ending position. You learned displacement in Unit 7. Here, we use it to understand energy transfer.

Work matters because engineers need to size motors, estimate energy use, design lifting equipment, and compare methods of moving loads. We will begin with a force pointing in exactly the same direction as motion, then consider angled and perpendicular forces.

Section 02

Three quantities and their units

Force is measured in newtons (N). Displacement is measured in meters (m). Work is measured in joules (J). One joule is one newton-meter: 1 J = 1 N × 1 m. A force of 1 N acting along a displacement of 1 m performs 1 J of work.

For this lesson, we will assume forces are constant during each movement unless a problem states otherwise. A constant force means the size and direction of the force do not change while the object moves. When the force is parallel to the movement and points the same way, the work equation is simple.

Work = force × displacement
W = Fd   (parallel, same direction)

The letters are shorthand: W means work in joules, F means force in newtons, and d means displacement in meters. In this simple case, the force and displacement are aligned.

Worked example: warehouse crate

A motor pushes a crate horizontally with a steady force of 120 N. The crate moves 5 m in the same direction. What work does the motor's force perform?

Step 1: Identify the values: F = 120 N and d = 5 m.

Step 2: Select W = Fd because the force points along the motion.

Step 3: Substitute: W = 120 × 5 = 600 J.

Step 4: Interpret: the motor's push transfers 600 joules of energy to the crate through this movement. This is the work done by the specified force, not necessarily the total electrical energy consumed by the motor.

Section 03

How to solve for an unknown distance

Sometimes an engineer knows how much work a machine performs and the force it applies, but needs to determine how far the object moved. We can rearrange the same equation rather than memorizing a new formula. Begin with W = Fd. Divide both sides by F. The F on the right cancels, leaving d = W/F.

d = W ÷ F   (when the force is parallel to motion)

Worked example: A machine performs 840 J of work while pushing with a steady 140 N force in the direction of travel. Distance = 840 ÷ 140 = 6 m. Check the answer by multiplying 140 N × 6 m = 840 J.

If the question asks for force instead, divide work by displacement: F = W/d. The relationship works because multiplication and division undo one another.

Section 04

Direction matters: positive, zero, and negative work

Mechanical work depends on the relationship between the direction of a force and the direction of an object's movement. A force helping the motion does positive work. A force acting opposite the motion does negative work. A force acting exactly perpendicular—at a right angle—to the motion does zero work on that movement.

Picture a cart rolling to the right. A motor pushing right does positive work. Friction acting left does negative work because it resists the motion. A support force from the floor points upward, while the cart's displacement is horizontal. That upward support force does zero work on the horizontal movement, even though the force is real and important.

Each cart moves right. The force direction changes the sign of the work.

Do not confuse negative work with negative energy being created. Negative work means the force removes mechanical energy from the object during the displacement. Friction commonly transfers mechanical energy into thermal energy.

Section 05

Angled forces: why only part of a pull does work

Workers often pull carts using ropes that angle upward rather than pointing straight forward. An angled pull has two components: a horizontal component and a vertical component. The horizontal component points along the cart's motion and performs work on its horizontal displacement. The vertical component may reduce how hard the cart presses against the floor, but it does no work on a purely horizontal displacement.

The angle θ (theta) is measured between the force arrow and the displacement arrow. Engineers use the cosine function to find what fraction of the angled force points along the displacement. You do not need to know trigonometry beforehand: a cosine value is a number between −1 and +1 that describes alignment. For these problems, the cosine value is supplied, so you only need to multiply.

W = Fd cos θ
Work = force × displacement × cosine of the angle between them
Diagram not to scale. The angle is measured from the rightward displacement arrow to the rope-force arrow.

Worked example: angled rope

A rope pulls a cart with 200 N of force at 60° above the horizontal. The cart moves 6 m horizontally. The problem supplies cos 60° = 0.50.

Step 1: F = 200 N; d = 6 m; cos θ = 0.50.

Step 2: Choose W = Fd cos θ because the rope is angled.

Step 3: W = 200 × 6 × 0.50 = 600 J.

Check: The forward component of the pull is 200 × 0.50 = 100 N. A forward 100 N force acting over 6 m does 600 J of work.

Common mistake: Do not multiply 200 × 6 and stop. That would incorrectly treat all 200 N as forward force. Also do not confuse the rope's angle with the slope of a ramp; always identify the angle between the force and the displacement.
Section 06

Three special angles worth remembering

When the force and displacement point in the same direction, their angle is 0°, and cos 0° = 1. Thus W = Fd. When they are perpendicular, the angle is 90°, and cos 90° = 0. Thus W = 0. When they point in opposite directions, the angle is 180°, and cos 180° = −1. Thus W = −Fd.

0°Same direction+1Positive
60°Partly forward+0.50Positive, reduced
90°Perpendicular0Zero
180°Opposite−1Negative

Worked example: A braking force of 80 N points opposite a cart's 4 m forward displacement. Work by the brake = 80 × 4 × (−1) = −320 J. A vertical support force of 500 N on the same horizontally moving cart performs 0 J of work, because cos 90° = 0.

Section 07

How an engineer chooses the right equation

Before solving any work problem, ask four questions. First, which force does the question ask about? A cart can have several forces acting on it. Second, does the object actually move? Third, which direction does it move? Fourth, what angle separates that force from the displacement?

If the force is parallel and forward, use W = Fd. If the force is angled, use W = Fd cos θ. If the force is perpendicular, work by that force is zero. If the force points backward, the work is negative. If you are asked for displacement from known work and a forward parallel force, use d = W/F.

Decision example: a rolling cart

A cart moves 3 m right while a worker pulls right with 50 N, friction pulls left with 10 N, and the floor supports upward with 200 N. The worker does +150 J of work. Friction does −30 J. The support force does 0 J. The net work from these three forces is 150 − 30 + 0 = +120 J. This illustrates why engineers identify which force the question names. The unit assessment asks about work by particular forces, not always the total from every force.

Section 08

Engineering design: choosing a cart-pulling arrangement

A designer needs to move equipment carts through a factory. A rope pulled straight forward makes the full pulling force available in the direction of motion. A rope angled upward produces less forward force for the same rope tension, but it can also reduce the downward contact force and therefore potentially reduce friction. Engineers must consider both effects instead of assuming the straight rope is always the best design.

Our simplified work equation calculates the work performed by the specified pulling force. It does not, by itself, determine the actual cart acceleration, the motor's electrical consumption, or the friction force. Those depend on other properties of the system. A good engineer states the model's assumptions and does not claim more than the calculation supports.

Section 10

Independent practice: try before revealing the solution

For each problem, first identify the force, displacement, and angle. Choose the equation before calculating. Write the unit J for work or m for displacement. Open the explanation only after attempting the problem.

1. A conveyor pushes a box with 90 N for 7 m in the same direction. Find work.

W = Fd = 90 × 7 = 630 J.

2. A winch cable applies 240 N parallel to a load's 3 m movement. Find work.

W = 240 × 3 = 720 J.

3. A machine performs 960 J of work using a 160 N forward force. How far did the object move?

d = W/F = 960/160 = 6 m.

4. A cart moves 8 m right while a 30 N force acts straight upward. How much work does that upward force perform?

The force is perpendicular to the displacement: W = 30 × 8 × cos 90° = 0 J.

5. A rope pulls with 150 N at 60° above horizontal while a cart moves 4 m horizontally. Use cos 60° = 0.50.

W = Fd cos θ = 150 × 4 × 0.50 = 300 J.

6. A rope pulls with 250 N at 60° while the cart moves 8 m. Use cos 60° = 0.50.

W = 250 × 8 × 0.50 = 1,000 J.

7. A brake resists motion with 45 N while a cart moves forward 5 m. Find work by the brake.

Opposite direction means cos 180° = −1. W = 45 × 5 × (−1) = −225 J.

8. A worker pushes against an unmoving crate with 500 N for 10 seconds. How much work does the worker's force do on the crate?

Displacement is zero. W = 500 × 0 = 0 J. The time spent pushing does not replace displacement.

9. A horizontal 100 N pull and a 40 N backward friction force act on a cart that moves 6 m forward. Find work by each force and net work.

Pull: +100 × 6 = +600 J. Friction: −40 × 6 = −240 J. Net work = 600 − 240 = +360 J.

10. Explain why the floor's upward support force can be large while its work on a horizontally moving cart is zero.

Work depends on the force component along displacement. The floor's force is upward, at 90° to horizontal movement, so its parallel component and work are both zero.

Section 11

Glossary: mechanical work vocabulary

Click a term to reveal its definition. Return to these entries whenever you encounter an unfamiliar word or symbol.

Force

A push or pull on an object. Force is measured in newtons (N).

Displacement

The change in an object's position, including direction. Displacement is measured in meters (m).

Mechanical work

Energy transferred when a force acts through a displacement. For a constant force, W = Fd cos θ. Work is measured in joules (J).

Joule

The unit used to measure work and energy. One joule (1 J) equals one newton-meter (1 N·m).

Parallel

Along the same line. When force and displacement point in the same direction, the angle between them is 0°.

Perpendicular

At a right angle (90°). A force perpendicular to an object's displacement performs zero work on that displacement.

Force component

The part of a force pointing along a chosen direction. For a horizontal displacement, the forward component of an angled pull is F cos θ.

Cosine

A mathematical ratio that tells us how much of a force points along the displacement. For example, cos 60° = 0.50; in lesson problems, this value is supplied.

Positive work

Work done by a force with a component in the direction of an object's displacement. It transfers energy to the object's mechanical motion.

Negative work

Work done by a force with a component opposite the displacement, such as friction or a brake resisting forward motion.

Zero work

Work equal to zero because the object does not move or because the specified force is perpendicular to its displacement.

Net work

The sum of the work done by all forces acting on an object over the displacement. Positive and negative contributions must be combined using their signs.

Section 12

Lesson summary and engineering connection

Mechanical work is not simply effort. It is energy transferred when a force acts through a displacement. For a constant force, calculate work using W = Fd cos θ, where θ is the angle between force and displacement. A forward force does positive work, a backward force does negative work, and a perpendicular force does zero work. When force and displacement are aligned, the equation simplifies to W = Fd. If work and force are known, d = W/F.

Mechanical engineers use these ideas when designing conveyors, winches, factory carts, and other machines. In A8.2 we will examine kinetic energy, the energy a moving object has because of its motion, and connect energy calculations to real equipment design.
