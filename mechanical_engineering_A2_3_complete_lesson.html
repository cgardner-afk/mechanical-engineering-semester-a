SECTION 01

Why Engineers Generate Alternatives

Imagine a school needs a portable ramp that allows a loaded equipment cart to cross a low step. A single sketch may seem convincing, but it is only one possible solution. A folding aluminum ramp, a modular composite ramp, and a fixed steel ramp could all address the same need in different ways. They may differ in cost, mass, storage convenience, durability, and required maintenance. Engineers improve decisions by exploring alternatives before committing resources to a prototype.

Concept generation is the process of proposing different approaches to a defined problem. The purpose is not to produce the greatest possible number of drawings. It is to uncover meaningful differences in mechanisms, materials, geometry, and operation. A team that generates several nearly identical designs may still overlook a better approach. A team that explores distinct concepts has more opportunities to discover useful solutions.

In Lesson A2.2 you learned that a candidate must satisfy every mandatory constraint before its preferred characteristics can be compared. That rule remains essential here. This lesson teaches how to generate ideas, evaluate them with a weighted decision matrix, calculate weighted totals correctly, and test whether the preferred concept changes when priorities change.

SECTION 02

Brainstorming Before Judging

Brainstorming is an early idea-generation method in which a team deliberately separates producing possibilities from judging them. During the generation stage, members record ideas without immediately dismissing unusual or unfamiliar suggestions. Early criticism can discourage participation and cause the group to settle on the first familiar concept. Once a broad range of ideas exists, the team can evaluate feasibility and performance more carefully.

Useful brainstorming begins with a clear problem statement. For the ramp project, the team might ask, “How could a ramp safely support the required load while being practical to store?” It can then vary the basic approach: folding versus removable sections, different structural shapes, different surface materials, or different ways to secure the ramp. Sketching quick concepts helps people communicate ideas that are difficult to describe with words alone.

Engineers can improve brainstorming by combining and modifying ideas. A folding ramp may use the surface treatment proposed for a modular ramp. A strong but heavy support arrangement might inspire a lighter version with reinforcing ribs. After generation, the team removes exact duplicates, clarifies the purpose of each concept, and records enough detail to evaluate it fairly. This is the appropriate time to identify weak ideas, not during the first few seconds after someone proposes them.

SECTION 03

From Many Ideas to Feasible Candidates

Not every brainstormed idea deserves a detailed numerical comparison. The team first performs a feasibility screening against hard constraints. Suppose the ramp must support a 250 kg load, fit within a 900 mm storage width, and cost no more than $220 to build. A candidate that fails a required load test is not acceptable simply because it is attractive or lightweight. A design exceeding the available storage space must be modified or excluded.

For a teaching example, assume three ramp concepts have already been checked against those mandatory limits and all three pass. The concepts are: A, Folding Aluminum, which stores compactly but has a more complex hinge; B, Modular Composite, which uses removable sections but may require careful joint maintenance; and C, Fixed Steel, which is simple and robust but relatively heavy. Because all three are feasible in this hypothetical scenario, the team can now compare preferences such as portability, affordability, and maintenance convenience.

This separation matters: a weighted score does not certify structural safety, and a high score cannot cancel a failed mandatory limit. Engineers should keep the evidence for compliance separate from the preference-ranking matrix.

SECTION 04

How a Weighted Decision Matrix Works

A weighted decision matrix is a table that compares design concepts using several criteria. The team assigns each criterion a weight indicating its relative importance. It then assigns each design a rating describing how well that design performs on that criterion. Multiplying the weight by the rating produces a weighted contribution. Adding the contributions gives the design's total weighted score.

For example, suppose portability matters most, affordability is next, and maintenance convenience is somewhat less important. The team assigns weights of 5 for portability, 3 for affordability, and 2 for maintenance. It uses a consistent rating scale from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent), where higher always means more desirable. A high affordability rating means the design is more affordable, not that its price is higher. All candidates must be judged using the same criteria and rating rules.

These ratings are illustrative judgments, not measured properties of real ramp products. In an actual project, engineers would support ratings with test results, cost estimates, or documented stakeholder evaluations. A decision matrix makes assumptions visible so the team can discuss them rather than hiding them inside an informal preference.

SECTION 05

Calculating Weighted Scores, Step by Step

The calculation uses multiplication and addition. For each criterion, multiply its weight by the design's rating. Then add those products. The formula is:

Total weighted score = Σ(weight × rating)

The Greek capital letter Σ, pronounced sigma, means “add the following terms.” Here it tells you to multiply each weight by its matching rating and add all the products. The result is a score in points, not a physical measurement such as newtons or kilograms.

These are three steps in one calculation—not three separate techniques. We will follow Concept A from the original decision matrix all the way to its final score. Then we will repeat the same three steps for Concepts B and C.

Step 1 — Identify each criterion's weight and the design's rating

Look at the original decision matrix in Section 4. The weights are 5 for portability, 3 for affordability, and 2 for maintenance. These weights are the same for every concept because the project's priorities do not change when we evaluate a different design. Concept A's ratings are 5 for portability, 3 for affordability, and 3 for maintenance. Write each weight next to its matching rating before calculating anything.

CriterionWeight (importance)Concept A rating (performance)
Portability55
Affordability33
Maintenance23

Step 2 — Multiply each weight by its matching rating

Now calculate the weighted contribution for each row. Portability contributes 5 × 5 = 25 points. Affordability contributes 3 × 3 = 9 points. Maintenance contributes 2 × 3 = 6 points. Do not add the weights and ratings separately; multiply each pair first.

Step 3 — Add the contributions to get one total score

Finally, add the three results from Step 2: 25 + 9 + 6 = 40 points. That is Concept A's single weighted decision score. The full calculation is (5 × 5) + (3 × 3) + (2 × 3) = 40 points. The multiplication tells us how strongly A performs on each priority; the addition combines those contributions into one number.

Repeat Steps 1–3 for the other concepts

To compare designs fairly, use the same weights for every concept, but substitute each design's own ratings. Concept B has ratings of 4, 2, and 4, so its score is (5 × 4) + (3 × 2) + (2 × 4) = 20 + 6 + 8 = 34 points. Concept C has ratings of 1, 5, and 5, so its score is (5 × 1) + (3 × 5) + (2 × 5) = 5 + 15 + 10 = 30 points. We are repeating the same procedure for each design, not learning a different formula.

DesignPortabilityAffordabilityMaintenanceTotal
A5 × 5 = 253 × 3 = 92 × 3 = 640

Now that each concept has one total score, compare the results. Concept A ranks first with 40 points, B follows with 34, and C has 30. A leads B by 40 − 34 = 6 points. The difference reflects this scoring system and its priorities, not a physical measurement. A tie would mean that the designs score equally under the current assumptions.

Decimal and percentage weights

Weights can also be expressed as fractions of a total. The three weights 5, 3, and 2 sum to 10. Dividing each by 10 gives normalized weights of 0.50, 0.30, and 0.20, or 50%, 30%, and 20%. Concept A's normalized score is 0.50 × 5 + 0.30 × 3 + 0.20 × 3 = 2.5 + 0.9 + 0.6 = 4.0 out of 5. Its original score was 40 out of 50. Both methods give the same ranking because every weight was divided by the same positive number. Be careful: entering 50 rather than 0.50 changes the size of the score, even though consistent scaling leaves the ranking unchanged.

SECTION 07

Weight Sensitivity and the Limits of Rankings

A decision matrix is only as defensible as its criteria, weights, ratings, and evidence. Weight sensitivity describes how the preferred alternative changes—or remains the same—when the importance of criteria changes. For example, if affordability becomes far more important than portability, Concept C may rise in the ranking because its affordability rating is high. That does not prove the original arithmetic was wrong. It shows that different priorities can produce different rational choices.

To test sensitivity, engineers deliberately change a weight and recalculate every candidate. If the same design remains first across reasonable weight choices, the ranking is relatively stable. If small changes reverse the ranking, the decision deserves closer discussion. The team might ask stakeholders to clarify priorities or gather better performance evidence before making a costly commitment.

Ratings also deserve scrutiny. A “4” for maintenance should have a clear meaning: perhaps a documented service-time range or a consistent expert rubric. Two evaluators can disagree when ratings are based only on impressions. Teams should record the reasons behind their ratings and avoid giving a design an artificially high score simply because someone favors it.

Most importantly, hard constraints are not ordinary weighted criteria. A design that fails a required load test cannot be selected because it earns high scores for appearance and price. The correct sequence remains: check mandatory requirements, compare feasible designs, examine sensitivity, and explain the recommendation.

SECTION 08

Worked Recommendation and Documentation

Suppose the team retains the original weights: portability 5, affordability 3, and maintenance 2. It verifies that the three ramp concepts meet the project's mandatory load, storage, and budget requirements. The decision matrix then gives A 40 points, B 34 points, and C 30 points. Under those stated priorities, Concept A is the preferred candidate. Its strong portability rating is especially valuable because portability carries the highest weight.

A defensible recommendation would include the requirement-screening evidence, the rating scale, the weights, the matrix calculations, and an explanation of uncertainty. For example: “We recommend the folding aluminum concept for prototyping because it satisfies the mandatory constraints and receives the highest weighted score under the current portability-focused priorities. Its hinge arrangement requires further testing, and the ranking should be reviewed if affordability becomes the primary concern.”

This is more informative than saying “A wins.” It explains the basis of the decision, acknowledges the assumptions, and identifies what should happen next. Engineering teams frequently use the outcome of a concept comparison to select a prototype for more detailed testing rather than treating the matrix as the final proof of performance.

Lesson glossary

Select a term to reveal its definition.

Alternative

One candidate solution to an engineering problem.

Brainstorming

Generating ideas before evaluating them.

Criterion

A feature used to compare feasible designs.

Constraint

A mandatory requirement.

Decision matrix

A structured table comparing alternatives across criteria.

Weight

A value representing a criterion’s relative importance.

Rating

A score describing a design’s performance on one criterion.

Weighted contribution

The weight multiplied by the rating for one criterion.

Weighted total

The sum of a design’s weighted contributions.

Sensitivity

How rankings change when assumptions, such as weights, change.

Feasible

Meeting all mandatory constraints.
