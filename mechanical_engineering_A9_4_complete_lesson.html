Section 01

A complete system must move and stop safely

A machine can be strong enough to carry its load and still be unsafe when it moves. An automated cart needs enough force to accelerate, a motor capable of supplying energy, and a braking system that can stop the cart within the available distance. A conveyor must move its load without exceeding a motor's rating, and an impact protector must reduce the force transferred during a collision. Engineers must examine motion, energy, and safety together.

In this lesson you will evaluate a warehouse transport system using the same kinds of diagrams, data tables, and calculations that appear in integrated engineering assessments. Each calculation answers a specific question. No single result proves that the entire machine is safe.

Design challenge

A powered cart carries a 300 kg total moving mass at 4 m/s toward a loading station. The available stopping distance is 2 m. The engineer must check the stopping system, compare candidate brakes, and justify a choice using evidence.

Section 03

Stopping distance from speed and deceleration

For constant acceleration, the kinematic equation is v² = v₀² + 2aΔx. Here v₀ is initial speed, v is final speed, a is signed acceleration, and Δx is displacement. During stopping, final speed v = 0 and acceleration is negative if forward is positive. Solving for the magnitude of braking deceleration gives abrake = v₀²/(2d). Solving for distance gives d = v₀²/(2abrake), where abrake is positive magnitude.

Worked example 1 — Required deceleration

A cart travels at 4 m/s and must stop within 2 m. Required deceleration magnitude = 4²/(2 × 2) = 16/4 = 4 m/s². The cart must be able to decelerate by at least 4 m/s² under the stated model.

If the cart's total mass is 300 kg, required net braking force magnitude is F = ma = 300 × 4 = 1,200 N. This is the net horizontal force needed for the specified deceleration. The actual brake-system force may differ if other resistive or driving forces also act.

Because speed is squared, doubling speed makes the stopping distance four times as large when braking deceleration is unchanged. This is why higher speed can dramatically increase stopping hazards.

Step-by-step worked method · Find required braking deceleration and force

  1. Step 1: Read initial speed 4 m/s, maximum stopping distance 2 m, cart mass 300 kg.
  2. Step 2: Use a_brake = v₀²/(2d).
  3. Step 3: Calculate 4²/(2 × 2) = 4 m/s².
  4. Step 4: Net braking force magnitude = ma = 300 × 4 = 1,200 N.
  5. Step 5: Check the result is net force; actual brake-system force depends on other forces.
Section 04

Use stopping-distance test data

Engineers often compare measured stopping distances rather than assuming all brakes behave ideally. Suppose a cart travels at the same initial speed on the same surface during each trial. The maximum allowed stopping distance is 2.0 m. A brake passes only if its observed distance is at or below that limit.

BrakeMeasured stopping distanceLimitResult
A2.6 m2.0 mFail
B1.9 m2.0 mPass
C1.5 m2.0 mPass

Brake A fails because 2.6 m exceeds the limit. Brakes B and C meet this single requirement. Brake C has the shorter measured distance, but that does not automatically make it the best overall choice. Engineers may also need to check peak deceleration, load security, cost, wear, and reliability.

Worked example 2 — Evaluate the test evidence

The specification says “stop within 2.0 m.” A brake that stops in 2.1 m does not pass just because it is close. A brake that stops in 1.9 m passes the distance criterion under the test conditions. If the specification also sets a maximum allowable deceleration for protecting cargo, the designer must check that second requirement separately.

Step-by-step worked method · Evaluate stopping-distance evidence

  1. Step 1: Read stopping limit 2.0 m.
  2. Step 2: Compare measured stops A = 2.6 m, B = 1.9 m, C = 1.5 m.
  3. Step 3: Reject A; B and C pass this single criterion.
  4. Step 4: Check force, wear, cost, and repeatability before choosing between B and C.
Section 05

Momentum and stopping time

Momentum is mass times velocity: p = mv. Momentum is a vector, so direction matters. To bring a moving cart to rest, the braking system must change its momentum. The impulse–momentum relationship is FavgΔt = Δp for the average net force over a time interval. Increasing the stopping time for the same momentum change reduces the magnitude of average net force.

Worked example 3 — Compare stopping times

A 200 kg cart moves at 3 m/s. Initial momentum = 200 × 3 = 600 kg·m/s. Final momentum is zero, so the magnitude of the momentum change is 600 kg·m/s.

If the cart stops in 0.5 s, average net force magnitude = 600/0.5 = 1,200 N. If a protective system increases stopping time to 1.5 s, average net force magnitude = 600/1.5 = 400 N. Tripling the stopping time reduces average force to one-third under the stated assumptions.

This is the principle behind many impact-protection systems. However, the peak force may differ from the average force, so designers must examine measured force–time data when peak limits matter.

Step-by-step worked method · Compare stopping times and forces

  1. Step 1: Read mass 200 kg and initial speed 3 m/s.
  2. Step 2: Initial momentum p = 200 × 3 = 600 kg·m/s.
  3. Step 3: Magnitude of momentum change to rest is 600 kg·m/s.
  4. Step 4: For 0.5 s, |F_avg| = 600/0.5 = 1,200 N.
  5. Step 5: For 1.5 s, |F_avg| = 600/1.5 = 400 N; peak force may differ.
Section 06

Kinetic energy and stopping work

Motion also stores kinetic energy: KE = ½mv². When a cart stops, its kinetic energy is transferred into other forms, commonly thermal energy in brakes and tires, deformation, sound, or energy stored in a recovery system. The energy does not simply disappear.

Worked example 4 — Energy that must be managed

A 300 kg cart moves at 4 m/s. Its kinetic energy is ½ × 300 × 4² = 150 × 16 = 2,400 J. The stopping system must account for this energy transfer. If an ideal constant braking force removes that energy over 2 m, average force magnitude from work–energy is F = 2,400/2 = 1,200 N. This agrees with the net force calculated using deceleration.

For the same mass, doubling speed from 4 m/s to 8 m/s increases kinetic energy from 2,400 J to 9,600 J. That is four times as much energy for the braking or protective system to manage.

Step-by-step worked method · Calculate kinetic energy and stopping work

  1. Step 1: Read mass 300 kg and speed 4 m/s.
  2. Step 2: KE = ½mv² = 0.5 × 300 × 4² = 2,400 J.
  3. Step 3: Over a 2 m stop, average net braking-force magnitude = 2,400/2 = 1,200 N.
  4. Step 4: At 8 m/s with the same mass, KE = 9,600 J: four times as much.
  5. Step 5: Explain that the energy is transferred into other forms during stopping.
Section 07

Collision protection: longer stops, smaller average forces

In a collision, engineers may add padding, crushable structures, springs, or other protective devices that increase the stopping distance or stopping time. For the same initial speed and mass, a longer stopping distance lowers the average net stopping force when energy is dissipated over that distance. The simplified work–energy relation is Favg d = KE in magnitude.

Worked example 5 — Two impact cushions

A 50 kg test cart moves at 2 m/s. KE = ½ × 50 × 2² = 100 J. Cushion A stops it over 0.10 m: average stopping force = 100/0.10 = 1,000 N. Cushion B stops it over 0.25 m: average stopping force = 100/0.25 = 400 N. Cushion B has the lower average stopping force in this model.

That does not establish that cushion B is universally safer. Engineers must also check maximum available space, peak acceleration, repeatability, material recovery, and the way the protected object or person responds to the force.

Step-by-step worked method · Compare impact cushions

  1. Step 1: Read cart mass 50 kg and speed 2 m/s.
  2. Step 2: Calculate KE = 0.5 × 50 × 2² = 100 J.
  3. Step 3: Cushion A: average stopping force 100/0.10 = 1,000 N.
  4. Step 4: Cushion B: average stopping force 100/0.25 = 400 N.
  5. Step 5: Compare average forces only; peak force and other constraints need separate tests.
Section 08

Conveyors: work, power, and motor capacity

A conveyor must supply mechanical power to move its load against resistance. When the force is aligned with motion, Pmechanical = Fv. Here F is the force delivered by the drive in newtons and v is belt speed in m/s. If efficiency η is the fraction of electrical input converted to useful mechanical output, Pinput = Fv/η.

Worked example 6 — Select a conveyor motor

A conveyor must deliver 600 N of driving force while moving at 2 m/s. Required mechanical output power = 600 × 2 = 1,200 W. At 75% efficiency, required electrical input power = 1,200/0.75 = 1,600 W. An input-rated motor limited to 1,400 W would fail this simplified demand check. An appropriately rated 1,800 W input motor could pass the average input-power screen, subject to torque, startup, duty-cycle, and other constraints.

Check the rating type. An electrical input rating and a mechanical output rating are not interchangeable. If a question supplies a mechanical output rating, compare it directly with mechanical output demand rather than dividing by efficiency again.

Step-by-step worked method · Select a conveyor motor

  1. Step 1: Read drive force 600 N, belt speed 2 m/s, efficiency 75%.
  2. Step 2: Mechanical output power = Fv = 600 × 2 = 1,200 W.
  3. Step 3: Convert η = 0.75; input power = 1,200/0.75 = 1,600 W.
  4. Step 4: A 1,400 W input-rated motor fails the average demand screen; 1,800 W passes it.
  5. Step 5: Verify whether motor ratings refer to electrical input or mechanical output.
Section 09

Integrating multiple safety requirements

Consider a cart with three mandatory criteria: stopping distance ≤ 2.0 m, average braking force ≤ 1,500 N, and motor electrical input power ≤ 2,000 W. A design passes only if it satisfies all three criteria under the specified conditions. A low stopping distance may require a high braking force, creating a trade-off. A higher-power motor may improve acceleration but exceed an electrical capacity limit.

A1.4 m1,714 N1,700 WFails force limit
B1.8 m1,333 N1,800 WPasses all
C2.3 m1,043 N1,600 WFails distance limit

These forces are illustrative averages based on a 2,400 J stopping-energy demand: F = KE/d. Design B is the only candidate that meets all three limits. This is stronger evidence than choosing the option with the shortest stop or lowest electrical input alone.

Step-by-step worked method · Check all safety requirements

  1. Step 1: List mandatory limits: distance ≤ 2.0 m, average braking force ≤ 1,500 N, input power ≤ 2,000 W.
  2. Step 2: Option A fails force: 1,714 N > 1,500 N.
  3. Step 3: Option B passes: 1.8 m, 1,333 N, 1,800 W.
  4. Step 4: Option C fails distance: 2.3 m > 2.0 m.
  5. Step 5: Recommend B only for the stated simplified criteria; document remaining checks.

Section 11

Engineering decision process: diagnose the limiting component

When a complete system fails, identify which mandatory requirement is violated before recommending a change. If a cart stops too late, examine braking capability, speed, and available distance. If the average force is too high, a longer stop may help—but only if space permits. If a conveyor exceeds its motor rating, calculate the power demand and evaluate a suitably rated motor or a reduction in required force or speed. A change that fixes one failure can create another.

Worked example 7 — Evidence-based recommendation

A 200 kg cart moves at 5 m/s and must stop within 2.5 m. Required deceleration = 5²/(2 × 2.5) = 5 m/s². Required average net braking force = 200 × 5 = 1,000 N. A brake that can provide only 3 m/s² would stop the cart in 25/(2 × 3) ≈ 4.17 m, so it fails the distance requirement. Recommend a brake capable of the necessary deceleration, while separately checking traction, force limits, and stopping reliability.

Section 12

Independent practice with guided solutions

Try each problem independently before opening its solution.

1. A cart moves at 6 m/s and brakes at 3 m/s². Find stopping distance.

d = 6²/(2 × 3) = 36/6 = 6 m.

2. A cart moves at 4 m/s and must stop in 2 m. Find required deceleration magnitude.

a = 4²/(2 × 2) = 4 m/s².

3. A 250 kg cart decelerates at 4 m/s². Find average net braking force magnitude.

F = ma = 250 × 4 = 1,000 N.

4. A 100 kg cart moves at 3 m/s. Find kinetic energy.

KE = ½ × 100 × 3² = 450 J.

5. If speed doubles while mass stays constant, how does kinetic energy change?

It becomes four times as large because KE depends on v².

6. A 200 kg cart at 2 m/s stops in 1 s. Find average net stopping force magnitude.

Initial momentum = 400 kg·m/s. Average force = 400/1 = 400 N.

7. The same cart stops in 0.5 s instead. Find average force.

400/0.5 = 800 N, twice as large.

8. A 50 kg cart at 2 m/s stops over 0.2 m. Find average stopping force.

KE = 100 J; F = 100/0.2 = 500 N.

9. A conveyor needs 500 N of drive force at 3 m/s. Find mechanical output power.

P = Fv = 500 × 3 = 1,500 W.

10. The conveyor in problem 9 is 75% efficient. Find electrical input power.

1,500/0.75 = 2,000 W.

11. A brake stops in 2.3 m but the limit is 2.0 m. Does it pass?

No. The measured distance exceeds the mandatory limit.

12. Why might the shortest-stopping brake not be the safest overall choice?

It may create excessive deceleration or peak forces, or fail other requirements. The engineer must evaluate all relevant constraints.

Section 13

Glossary: motion, energy, and safety

Select each aqua vocabulary term to reveal its meaning.

Stopping distance

Distance traveled during the braking interval before motion ceases.

Deceleration

Acceleration opposite the direction of motion, reducing speed.

Kinematic equation

An equation relating motion quantities such as speed, acceleration, time, and displacement.

Net force

The vector sum of forces acting on an object.

Momentum

Mass multiplied by velocity: p = mv.

Impulse

Force acting over time, equal to the change in momentum for net force.

Average force

A force value that produces the same total impulse or work over a specified interval as the actual varying force, as defined by the context.

Peak force

The largest instantaneous force during an event.

Kinetic energy

Energy of motion, equal to one-half mass times speed squared.

Work–energy principle

Net work equals the change in kinetic energy.

Mechanical output power

Rate of useful mechanical energy transfer, often Fv for aligned force and speed.

Electrical input power

Rate of electrical energy supplied to a machine.

Efficiency

Useful output energy or power divided by input energy or power for consistent operating conditions.

Design constraint

A mandatory numerical or operational limit.

Safety trade-off

A design change that improves one safety measure while potentially worsening another.

Operating hazard

A condition that can cause harm or system failure during use.

Section 14

Lesson summary and next steps

To evaluate motion and safety, begin with a diagram and identify speed, mass, braking distance, and force directions. Use d = v²/(2a) for constant-deceleration stopping distance, F = ma for net braking force, p = mv and impulse for stopping-time effects, and KE = ½mv² for energy that must be managed. Use P = Fv and efficiency when screening conveyor or motor requirements. Always check every mandatory constraint; passing one calculation is not proof of complete safety.
