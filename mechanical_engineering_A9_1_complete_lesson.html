Section 01

Why engineering starts with a design brief

Imagine that a warehouse manager asks your engineering team to build a lift. “Make a good lift” sounds like a request, but it does not tell you what a successful design must do. How heavy is the load? How high must it travel? How quickly must it arrive? Without answers, two teams could build very different machines and both claim success. Engineers begin by translating a need into a design brief: a clear description of the problem, intended users, required performance, and limits.

In this unit, you will act as an engineer who must make defensible decisions about machines. You will use forces, motion, energy, power, materials, safety, and testing to decide whether a proposed system is acceptable. Lesson A9.1 begins with the foundation of that process: stating requirements precisely and designing tests that can show whether they have been met.

Example design brief

“Develop a warehouse lift that raises a 150 kg load vertically through 3 m in no more than 10 seconds. The lift must fit within the available space and satisfy specified safety requirements.”

Unlike “make a good lift,” this statement includes values that can be measured. It does not dictate which brand, motor, or mechanism the team must use.

Section 02

Measurable success criteria

A success criterion describes a condition that must be met for a design to count as successful. A measurable criterion contains a quantity, a unit, and often a comparison such as “at least,” “at most,” or “no more than.” A testable requirement identifies what to measure and what result counts as passing. A statement such as “the cart should be attractive” is subjective unless the client defines a measurable way to evaluate appearance.

Consider the requirement “raise 120 kg by 2 m within 12 s.” The mass is 120 kilograms (kg), the vertical distance is 2 meters (m), and the maximum allowed time is 12 seconds (s). A team can load the lift with 120 kg, measure its rise, time its motion, and compare the observations with the stated limits. That makes the requirement testable. The requirement does not guarantee that the machine is safe or that it is the best possible design; those claims require additional evidence.

Worked example 1 — Rewrite a vague request

Vague: “The cart must be fast and carry a lot.”

Measurable: “The cart must carry a 100 kg load over a 10 m level course in at most 15 s.”

The revised statement specifies a load, distance, time limit, and units. An engineer could repeat the test and determine whether the design passes.

Step-by-step worked method · Turn a vague request into a testable requirement

  1. Step 1: Identify the intended behavior (for example, lift a rated load).
  2. Step 2: Specify numerical load, vertical travel, and time limit with units.
  3. Step 3: Write a pass/fail rule, such as lift 120 kg by 2 m within 12 s.
  4. Step 4: Describe how to measure load, height, and elapsed time.
  5. Step 5: Avoid calling the design safe until separate safety criteria are verified.
Section 03

Requirements, constraints, and preferences

A requirement states what the system must accomplish. A constraint is a mandatory limit within which the solution must operate. A preference is desirable but not essential. Requirements and constraints are both mandatory; engineers often distinguish them by describing requirements as desired performance and constraints as restrictions on the solution. A design that violates a mandatory limit is not acceptable even if it performs well in other ways.

Suppose a cart must carry 150 kg and fit through a doorway 0.8 m wide. The carrying capacity is a performance requirement; the doorway width is a physical constraint. If the team would also prefer blue paint, that is a preference. The team should not sacrifice the width limit to achieve the preferred color.

Worked example 2 — Check the constraint

Doorway clearance = 0.80 m. Cart width = 0.75 m. Since 0.75 m is less than 0.80 m, the cart meets the stated width limit. The remaining clearance is 0.80 − 0.75 = 0.05 m, or 5 cm. In real design, engineers must also consider manufacturing tolerances and maneuvering clearance before declaring a fit safe.

When comparing options, check mandatory conditions first. Only then compare preferences such as color, appearance, convenience, or lower cost when cost is not a mandatory cap.

Step-by-step worked method · Check doorway clearance

  1. Step 1: Read doorway width 0.80 m and cart width 0.75 m.
  2. Step 2: Compare measured width against the mandatory maximum.
  3. Step 3: Calculate clearance: 0.80 − 0.75 = 0.05 m.
  4. Step 4: Since 0.75 < 0.80, the width criterion passes.
  5. Step 5: Check any required operating clearance separately.
Section 04

How engineers choose a valid test

A requirement is useful only if the team can verify it. A verification test measures the specific quantity named in a requirement. For example, if the requirement is to lift 150 kg through 3 m in no more than 10 s, a suitable test loads the machine with 150 kg, measures the vertical travel, and times the lift. Measuring the motor's color or listening to its noise does not directly test the stated lifting performance.

Tests should be repeatable: the same procedure, equipment, and operating conditions should produce results that can be compared. A controlled test changes one planned variable while keeping other important variables as consistent as possible. Engineers record measurements rather than relying on impressions. Safety-critical tests must use appropriate procedures and supervision; a classroom simulator is not a substitute for testing a real load-handling machine.

Worked example 3 — Match the test to the claim

Claim: “The lift raises 150 kg through 3 m within 10 s.”

Valid test: Apply a verified 150 kg load, mark the start and end heights 3 m apart, and use a timer to measure the lift duration. Repeat trials under consistent conditions.

Pass criterion: The machine reaches the 3 m height with the required load in 10 s or less. A 9.4 s trial passes; an 11.2 s trial fails.

Step-by-step worked method · Choose a verification test

  1. Step 1: Read the claim: lift 150 kg by 3 m within 10 s.
  2. Step 2: Match each quantity to a measurement: load, vertical displacement, elapsed time.
  3. Step 3: Run the test under specified conditions using suitable equipment.
  4. Step 4: Compare the measured result with every stated limit.
  5. Step 5: Record the result and do not infer unrelated safety claims.
Section 05

A testing plan and evidence table

A test plan records the requirement, what will be measured, how it will be measured, and the passing threshold. A test result is an observed measurement; it is not the same thing as the original requirement. Keeping these separate prevents teams from changing their goals after seeing the results.

Step-by-step worked method · Interpret a test-results table

  1. Step 1: List each mandatory threshold: load ≥ 150 kg, rise ≥ 3 m, time ≤ 10 s, width < 0.80 m.
  2. Step 2: Compare each measured value with its corresponding threshold.
  3. Step 3: Record passes for load 150 kg, rise 3.0 m, and width 0.75 m.
  4. Step 4: Record failure for time 10.8 s because 10.8 > 10.
  5. Step 5: Reject overall compliance, revise the slow subsystem, and retest.
Section 06

Why a shorter deadline demands more power

The Unit 9 test also asks what happens when a lifting deadline becomes shorter. This requires a concept from Unit 8. A lift raises a mass m through vertical height h. Its useful lifting energy is E = mgh, where g is approximately 9.8 N/kg near Earth's surface. Average useful power is Puseful = E/t = mgh/t, where t is the time in seconds. A watt (W) is one joule per second.

If the load and height do not change, useful energy does not change. If time decreases, the same energy must be delivered more quickly, so required average power increases. A hoist's electrical input power also depends on efficiency. If efficiency is unchanged, Pinput = Puseful ÷ efficiency as a decimal.

Worked example 4 — The deadline is cut in half

A lift raises 150 kg through 3 m. Useful energy = 150 × 9.8 × 3 = 4,410 J. At 70% efficiency, η = 0.70.

20-second lift: Useful power = 4,410/20 = 220.5 W. Electrical input power = 220.5/0.70 = 315 W.

10-second lift: Useful power = 4,410/10 = 441 W. Electrical input power = 441/0.70 = 630 W.

The time was halved, so the required average electrical input power doubled. The useful energy remained 4,410 J. The example assumes constant efficiency and ignores startup and transient motor demands, which must be evaluated separately in real equipment selection.

Decision shortcut

For the same load, same height, and unchanged efficiency: half the time → twice the average input power; twice the time → half the average input power. The relationship is inverse, not squared.

Step-by-step worked method · Calculate power when a deadline shrinks

  1. Step 1: Read m = 150 kg, h = 3 m, efficiency η = 0.70.
  2. Step 2: Useful energy E = mgh = 150 × 9.8 × 3 = 4,410 J.
  3. Step 3: For 20 s: useful power 4,410/20 = 220.5 W; input power 220.5/0.70 = 315 W.
  4. Step 4: For 10 s: useful power 4,410/10 = 441 W; input power 441/0.70 = 630 W.
  5. Step 5: Conclude that halving time doubles required average power if efficiency is unchanged.

Section 08

Reading an engineering diagram without guessing

Engineering diagrams communicate the values needed for calculations. A drawing may be labeled not to scale, meaning you must use the printed dimensions rather than measuring the picture. Identify the object, the direction of motion, the given quantities, and the quantity the problem asks you to find. For a lifting requirement, distinguish mass in kg from weight in newtons, vertical height in m from a cable or ramp length, and time in s from power in watts.

Worked example 5 — Read the schematic

A schematic labels a load 200 kg, vertical rise 4 m, maximum time 8 s, and efficiency 80%. First calculate useful energy: 200 × 9.8 × 4 = 7,840 J. Next find useful power: 7,840/8 = 980 W. Convert 80% to 0.80 and calculate electrical input power: 980/0.80 = 1,225 W. The diagram's size on screen has no effect on the result.

These calculations help estimate performance. They do not, by themselves, establish that the structure is safe or that a particular motor meets all operational requirements.

Section 09

Independent practice with guided solutions

Try each problem independently before revealing its solution. Explain why the stated requirement is or is not testable whenever a question asks for a judgment.

1. Why is “raise 180 kg through 4 m in 15 s” testable?

It states measurable quantities: mass (180 kg), vertical height (4 m), and time (15 s). A team can measure each one.

2. A cart must carry 120 kg, fit through a 0.8 m doorway, and preferably be blue. Identify the constraint and preference.

Doorway width is a mandatory physical constraint. Blue paint is a preference. Carrying 120 kg is a performance requirement.

3. What test directly verifies that a lift raises 150 kg through 3 m in at most 10 s?

Load it with 150 kg, measure a 3 m vertical rise, time the motion, and verify the time is 10 s or less.

4. A lift meets its height and load requirements but takes 11 s against a 10 s limit. Does it pass?

No. It fails a mandatory time requirement.

5. A lift does 6,000 J of useful work in 20 s. Find useful power.

6,000/20 = 300 W.

6. The same lift now has a 10 s deadline. Find useful power.

6,000/10 = 600 W. Halving time doubles power.

7. Useful power is 600 W and efficiency is 75%. Find input power.

600/0.75 = 800 W.

8. A 100 kg load rises 5 m in 10 s. Find useful energy and power.

Energy = 100 × 9.8 × 5 = 4,900 J; useful power = 4,900/10 = 490 W.

9. A design requires a cart width below 0.80 m. One prototype measures 0.82 m. Can it be approved?

No. Its width exceeds the mandatory constraint by 0.02 m (2 cm).

10. A machine takes 9.5 s to complete a lift with a 10 s limit. What can you conclude?

It passes the stated time requirement, but this result alone does not establish structural safety, efficiency, or compliance with other requirements.

11. A 200 kg load rises 2 m in 8 s at 80% efficiency. Find average electrical input power.

Useful energy = 200 × 9.8 × 2 = 3,920 J; useful power = 3,920/8 = 490 W; input power = 490/0.80 = 612.5 W.

12. Explain why testing the motor's color does not verify a lifting-time requirement.

Color is unrelated to the measured time. A valid verification test must directly measure the load, rise, and elapsed time specified in the requirement.

Section 10

Glossary: engineering design and verification

Select each aqua term to reveal its definition.

Engineering design

The process of developing and evaluating solutions to a defined need.

Design brief

A statement of the problem, purpose, and major expectations for a proposed design.

Requirement

A mandatory function or performance condition a design must achieve.

Constraint

A mandatory limit on a design, such as maximum size, time, mass, or cost.

Preference

A desirable feature that is not mandatory.

Success criterion

A defined condition used to decide whether a design is successful.

Measurable

Expressed in a way that can be checked with observations or numerical values.

Verification test

A procedure that directly checks whether a stated requirement is satisfied.

Repeatable test

A test performed using a consistent procedure so results can be compared.

Controlled test

A test in which the intended variable changes while other important conditions are kept consistent.

Test result

A measured observation obtained during a test.

Pass criterion

The threshold a measurement must meet to count as passing.

Useful energy

Energy transferred into the intended task, such as raising a load.

Average power

Work or energy transferred divided by elapsed time, measured in watts.

Efficiency

The fraction of input energy converted to useful output energy.

Feasible design

A design that satisfies every mandatory requirement and constraint.

Section 11

Lesson summary and next steps

Engineering design begins with a precise problem statement. A testable requirement specifies measurable performance, while constraints define mandatory limits and preferences describe optional features. Engineers choose verification tests that measure the quantities named in the requirements, record results, and reject designs that fail mandatory criteria.

When a lift must perform the same task in less time, its required average power rises. Halving the time doubles average power if useful work and efficiency remain unchanged. In A9.2, you will use force and acceleration calculations alongside power and safety considerations to screen proposed machine designs.
