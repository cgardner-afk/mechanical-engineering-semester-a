Section 01

Energy changes form, but does not disappear

Imagine a cart starting at the top of a sloping test track. Before it moves, its height gives it gravitational potential energy. As it rolls downhill, it loses height and gains speed. The energy associated with height decreases while the energy associated with motion increases. This is an example of energy transformation: energy changes from one form to another.

The law of conservation of energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can be transferred between objects or transformed into different forms. Mechanical engineers use this principle to analyze roller coasters, lifting systems, moving carts, machine brakes, and many other devices.

It is important to define what we are studying. A system is the group of objects we choose to analyze. If our system includes a cart, the track, and the surrounding environment, energy transferred into heat or sound still belongs in the total accounting. If we examine only the cart's mechanical energy, that amount can decrease when friction transfers energy into thermal energy.

Words to know before we begin

Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic energy and potential energy. Kinetic energy is energy of motion. Gravitational potential energy is energy associated with height. Friction is a contact force that resists sliding or rolling motion and can convert mechanical energy into thermal energy.

Section 02

Review the two forms of mechanical energy

In A8.2, you calculated kinetic energy with KE = ½mv². Mass m is measured in kilograms (kg), speed v in meters per second (m/s), and energy in joules (J). Remember that speed is squared: 4² = 4 × 4 = 16.

In A8.3, you calculated gravitational potential energy with GPE = mgh, where g is approximately 9.8 N/kg near Earth's surface and h is vertical height in meters above a chosen reference level. The two forms of energy can be added because they share the same unit.

Mechanical energy = kinetic energy + gravitational potential energy
ME = KE + GPE = ½mv² + mgh

A cart at rest at the top of a hill has no translational kinetic energy because its speed is zero. A moving cart at the reference height has zero GPE relative to that level, but it can still have kinetic energy. Neither observation means the cart has no energy of any kind.

Section 03

Conservation on an ideal, frictionless track

Begin with a simplified engineering model: a cart moves along a track with negligible friction, negligible air resistance, and no motor pushing or brake acting. We also treat the cart as a particle or a nonrotating sliding body so we do not need to calculate energy in rotating wheels. Under these assumptions, its mechanical energy stays constant.

KEinitial + GPEinitial = KEfinal + GPEfinal

The words initial and final mean the beginning and ending positions of the interval being studied. A high starting point may have large GPE and small KE; a low point may have small GPE and large KE. The total remains the same even though the individual forms change.

Conceptual track: at a higher point the cart has more GPE; at a lower point it has more KE. The total mechanical energy is unchanged in this ideal model.

Worked example 1 — Track energy table

A cart begins with 600 J of mechanical energy. At point A, it has 500 J of GPE and 100 J of KE. At point B, it has 150 J of GPE. Because total mechanical energy remains 600 J, KE at B = 600 − 150 = 450 J.

Check: 150 J + 450 J = 600 J. No energy has disappeared; its form has changed.

Step-by-step worked method · Conserve mechanical energy

  1. Step 1: Identify initial KE and GPE at the first point.
  2. Step 2: Add to obtain total initial mechanical energy.
  3. Step 3: For an ideal frictionless, unpowered system set KE_i + GPE_i = KE_f + GPE_f.
  4. Step 4: Subtract the known final energy component to find the unknown.
  5. Step 5: Check both sides sum to the same total joules.
Section 04

Find missing energy from a diagram or table

Many engineering problems give a diagram with two or three locations and ask for a missing kinetic-energy or potential-energy value. First identify the total mechanical energy at a location where both values are known. Then use that same total at another location, provided the problem says friction and other losses are negligible.

At position A, the cart begins at rest, so KE = 0 J and total mechanical energy is 800 J. At position B, 500 J remains as GPE, so KE = 800 − 500 = 300 J. At position C, the cart reaches the chosen zero-height level, so GPE = 0 J and all 800 J of the ideal mechanical energy is kinetic.

Worked example 2 — Missing energy at a middle point

A cart has 1,200 J of total mechanical energy. At one location its kinetic energy is 850 J. What is its GPE there? Use GPE = ME − KE = 1,200 − 850 = 350 J. Check that 850 + 350 = 1,200 J.

Step-by-step worked method · Find missing energy in a table

  1. Step 1: Read total mechanical energy = 800 J.
  2. Step 2: At middle point, GPE = 500 J.
  3. Step 3: Use KE = total − GPE = 800 − 500 = 300 J.
  4. Step 4: Check 500 + 300 = 800 J.
  5. Step 5: This equality assumes negligible mechanical energy losses.
Section 05

Calculate speed from a vertical drop

Conservation of energy can predict how fast a cart moves after dropping through a vertical height. Consider an ideal cart that starts from rest and descends a height h to a point where the reference GPE is zero. Its starting energy is mgh. At the bottom, its energy is ½mv². Since mechanical energy is conserved, the two are equal.

Derive the speed equation one step at a time

Step 1: Set initial GPE equal to final KE: mgh = ½mv².

Step 2: Divide both sides by m (mass is nonzero): gh = ½v². This shows why mass cancels for this ideal situation.

Step 3: Multiply both sides by 2: 2gh = v².

Step 4: Take the positive square root because we are finding speed, a nonnegative quantity: v = √(2gh).

The square-root symbol √ means the nonnegative number that produces the quantity inside when multiplied by itself. For example, √49 = 7 because 7 × 7 = 49. On a calculator, enter the entire quantity 2 × g × h before taking its square root.

Worked example 3 — Speed after a 5 m drop

A cart starts from rest and descends 5 m on an ideal frictionless track. Use g = 9.8 m/s². First calculate 2gh = 2 × 9.8 × 5 = 98. Then take the square root: v = √98 ≈ 9.90 m/s. Notice that mass was not needed.

This shortcut applies only under the stated assumptions: starts from rest, a known vertical drop, negligible losses, and no additional energy stored in rotation or other forms. For a cart that already has initial speed, use the full energy equation rather than this simplified shortcut.

Step-by-step worked method · Calculate speed from height drop

  1. Step 1: Assume the cart starts at rest and energy losses are negligible.
  2. Step 2: Set mgh = ½mv².
  3. Step 3: Cancel mass, rearrange v² = 2gh.
  4. Step 4: Take the positive square root: v = √(2gh).
  5. Step 5: Substitute the stated vertical drop and report speed in m/s.
Section 06

What changes when friction is present?

Real machines are not perfectly frictionless. As a cart moves, friction and air resistance can transfer some of its mechanical energy into thermal energy in the wheels, track, or air. Some energy may also be transferred into sound or deformation. Total energy is still conserved when we account for all forms, but the cart's mechanical energy may decrease.

Initial mechanical energy = final mechanical energy + energy transferred to other forms

For a cart descending a track with no motor input, an energy accounting equation can be written as MEinitial = MEfinal + Eloss, where Eloss represents mechanical energy transferred into thermal energy and other nonmechanical forms. The word loss does not mean that energy was destroyed; it means that energy is no longer available as the cart's mechanical energy.

Worked example 4 — Energy lost to friction

A cart starts with 1,000 J of mechanical energy. After descending a track, it has 720 J of kinetic energy and 80 J of gravitational potential energy. Final mechanical energy = 720 + 80 = 800 J. Energy transferred to other forms = 1,000 − 800 = 200 J.

Interpretation: If friction is the only important loss mechanism, approximately 200 J became thermal energy. The cart has less mechanical energy, but total energy has not vanished.

Step-by-step worked method · Account for friction losses

  1. Step 1: Read initial total mechanical energy and energy transferred to other forms.
  2. Step 2: Use ME_initial = ME_final + E_loss.
  3. Step 3: Rearrange ME_final = ME_initial − E_loss.
  4. Step 4: Check all terms are in joules and loss energy is not negative.
  5. Step 5: Explain that energy was transferred, not destroyed.
Section 07

Use energy accounting to compare ideal and real motion

An ideal model is useful because it gives a clear reference prediction. Suppose a 100 kg cart starts from rest 4 m above the bottom of a track. Its initial GPE is 100 × 9.8 × 4 = 3,920 J. In the frictionless model, all of this becomes kinetic energy at the bottom, so the ideal speed is √(2 × 9.8 × 4) = √78.4 ≈ 8.85 m/s.

Now suppose 720 J of mechanical energy is transferred to thermal energy along the way. The bottom kinetic energy is 3,920 − 720 = 3,200 J. To find the actual speed, start from KE = ½mv², multiply by 2, divide by m, and take a square root: v = √(2KE/m). Here v = √(2 × 3,200/100) = √64 = 8 m/s.

Worked example 5 — Compare the two results

Ideal bottom speed: approximately 8.85 m/s. Speed with losses: 8.00 m/s. The real cart reaches the bottom more slowly because some starting gravitational potential energy became thermal energy rather than kinetic energy.

Do not subtract joules directly from meters per second. Energy and speed have different units. Subtract energy losses from the available mechanical energy first, then use the kinetic-energy equation to calculate speed if needed.

Step-by-step worked method · Compare ideal and real speeds

  1. Step 1: Read m = 100 kg, h = 4 m, and energy loss = 720 J.
  2. Step 2: Initial GPE = 100 × 9.8 × 4 = 3,920 J.
  3. Step 3: Ideal bottom speed = √(2 × 9.8 × 4) ≈ 8.85 m/s.
  4. Step 4: Actual bottom KE = 3,920 − 720 = 3,200 J.
  5. Step 5: Actual speed = √(2 × 3,200/100) = 8.0 m/s; explain why it is lower.

Section 09

Avoid these common energy mistakes

The first mistake is assuming energy disappears when a cart slows down. Instead, energy is transferred into other forms. The second is assuming mechanical energy is always conserved: it is conserved in an ideal model with no mechanical-energy transfers out, but real friction can reduce it. The third is using ramp length instead of vertical drop height in mgh. The fourth is forgetting to square speed in kinetic energy or forgetting to take a square root when solving for speed.

Energy problem decision guide

Asked for a missing energy on an ideal track? Add KE + GPE at a known location, then subtract the given energy at the other location. Asked for speed after a drop from rest? If losses are negligible, use v = √(2gh). Asked where energy went with friction? Subtract final mechanical energy from initial mechanical energy. Asked for speed after known losses? Find the remaining kinetic energy, then use v = √(2KE/m).

Assessment-style energy diagram: from height to speed

On a frictionless track, a cart released from rest at height 5 m loses gravitational potential energy as it descends. At the bottom, the lost GPE becomes kinetic energy. The diagram gives a vertical drop, not the distance traveled along the curved track.

Read: vertical drop h = 5 m; initial speed = 0; friction = 0. Choose: mgh = ½mv². Cancel mass from both sides: gh = ½v². Multiply by 2: 2gh = v². Take the square root: v = √(2gh). Calculate: √(2 × 9.8 × 5) = √98 ≈ 9.9 m/s. If the track has friction, this ideal speed would be an overestimate.

Section 10

Independent practice with guided solutions

Work each problem before opening its solution. Write down what is known, select an energy equation, show the arithmetic, and state the answer with the correct unit.

1. An ideal cart has 900 J of total mechanical energy and 350 J of GPE. Find its KE.

KE = ME − GPE = 900 − 350 = 550 J.

2. A cart has 420 J of KE and 180 J of GPE. Find its total mechanical energy.

ME = 420 + 180 = 600 J.

3. A cart starts from rest and drops 3 m without losses. Find its bottom speed.

v = √(2 × 9.8 × 3) = √58.8 ≈ 7.67 m/s.

4. A cart starts from rest and drops 8 m without losses. Find its bottom speed.

v = √(2 × 9.8 × 8) = √156.8 ≈ 12.52 m/s.

5. A cart begins with 1,500 J of mechanical energy and ends with 1,200 J. How much transferred to other forms?

Energy transferred = 1,500 − 1,200 = 300 J.

6. An ideal cart has 2,000 J of ME. At a point where KE = 1,300 J, find GPE.

GPE = 2,000 − 1,300 = 700 J.

7. A 50 kg cart starts at rest 4 m above a track bottom. Find initial GPE.

GPE = 50 × 9.8 × 4 = 1,960 J.

8. In problem 7, what is its ideal KE at the bottom?

With no losses, all 1,960 J of starting GPE becomes 1,960 J of KE.

9. A cart has 1,000 J of initial ME and 250 J transfers into thermal energy. What is its final ME?

Final ME = 1,000 − 250 = 750 J.

10. A 100 kg cart has 3,200 J of KE at the bottom. Find its speed.

v = √(2 × 3,200/100) = √64 = 8 m/s.

11. Two carts of different mass descend the same vertical drop from rest on ideal tracks. Which has greater bottom speed?

Neither. Under the stated ideal assumptions, v = √(2gh), so mass cancels and both have the same speed.

12. Why can a real cart lose mechanical energy while total energy is conserved?

Friction and other resistive effects transfer some mechanical energy into thermal energy, sound, and other forms. Total energy remains accounted for when those forms are included.

Section 11

Glossary: energy conservation vocabulary

Click a term to reveal its definition. The expandable entries follow the same vocabulary format as the previous lessons.

Conservation of energy

Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can be transferred or transformed.

Energy transformation

A change from one form of energy to another, such as GPE becoming KE.

Mechanical energy (ME)

The sum of kinetic and potential energy in the model being studied.

Kinetic energy (KE)

Energy of motion; for a nonrotating body, KE = ½mv².

Gravitational potential energy (GPE)

Energy associated with height in a gravitational field; GPE = mgh relative to a chosen reference level.

Friction

A contact force resisting relative motion that can transform mechanical energy into thermal energy.

Thermal energy

Energy associated with microscopic motion and interactions of particles; often increases due to friction.

Energy loss

In a mechanical-energy calculation, energy transferred into forms not included in the mechanical total; energy is not destroyed.

Frictionless model

An idealized situation where friction is negligible, allowing mechanical energy to remain constant if no other transfers occur.

System

The objects or region selected for analysis.

Initial

At the beginning of the interval being analyzed.

Final

At the end of the interval being analyzed.

Vertical drop

The decrease in height between two positions, measured in meters.

Square root

The nonnegative number that produces a value when squared; √64 = 8.

Speed

How fast an object moves, measured in meters per second (m/s).

Energy accounting

Tracking all relevant forms and transfers of energy so the totals balance.

Section 12

Lesson summary and next steps

Energy cannot be created or destroyed. On an ideal frictionless track, a cart's mechanical energy remains constant even as gravitational potential energy changes into kinetic energy. Use ME = KE + GPE and KEinitial + GPEinitial = KEfinal + GPEfinal to solve missing-energy problems.

For a cart released from rest through a vertical drop with negligible losses, v = √(2gh). Friction can transfer some mechanical energy into thermal energy and other forms, reducing the cart's speed without violating conservation of total energy.
