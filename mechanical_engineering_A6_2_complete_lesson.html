Section 01

Why mechanical engineers calculate stress

A machine designer may know that a lifting mechanism pulls on a steel rod with 12,000 N, but force alone does not tell the engineer whether the rod is appropriately sized. The same force acting through a thin rod produces a greater average stress than it does through a thick rod. Engineers use stress to connect an external load to the size and internal loading of a component.

Consider a packaging machine that lifts a heavy platen using two tension rods. If a rod is too thin, the material may yield or fracture. If it is unnecessarily thick, the mechanism may be heavier, more expensive, and harder to assemble. The engineering task is to determine an appropriate cross-sectional area, then verify that the chosen material, geometry, and operating conditions meet the design requirements.

In this lesson we focus on average axial normal stress in straight components carrying centered tensile or compressive loads. This is a deliberately simplified but important first design model. Later lessons will address deformation, failure, safety factors, and additional loading modes.

Section 02

Tension and compression in machine parts

Tension pulls a component along its axis. Tie rods, suspension links, and certain fasteners can carry axial tensile loads. Compression pushes a component along its axis. Machine legs, press columns, and some support struts can carry axial compressive loads. Both produce normal stress because the internal force acts perpendicular to the cross section being examined.

For a straight prismatic bar with a centered axial load, the average normal stress is calculated using the axial force divided by the cross-sectional area. A common sign convention takes tension as positive and compression as negative, but engineering design checks often compare the magnitude of stress against an allowable magnitude.

Section 03

The engineering stress equation

The equation for average axial normal stress is σ = F/A, where σ (sigma) is normal stress, F is the axial force, and A is the area of the cross section perpendicular to the force. The result is an average because real components may have nonuniform stress near holes, shoulders, connections, or other geometric features.

The SI unit of stress is the pascal (Pa), equal to one newton per square meter (N/m²). Engineers often work with megapascals (MPa), because ordinary component stresses can be millions of pascals. An especially useful unit relationship is 1 MPa = 1 N/mm². If force is in newtons and area is in square millimeters, the numerical result is immediately in MPa.

Units check: 8,000 N ÷ 100 mm² = 80 N/mm² = 80 MPa. Do not report 80 Pa. Converting mm² to m² requires squaring the length conversion: 100 mm² = 0.000100 m².
Section 04

Worked example 1 · Stress in a lifting rod

An industrial lifting fixture uses a straight tie rod carrying 9,600 N of axial tension. The rod has a net cross-sectional area of 160 mm². Determine the average tensile stress.

Step 1: Identify the known values: F = 9,600 N and A = 160 mm². Step 2: Apply σ = F/A. Step 3: Calculate σ = 9,600/160 = 60 MPa.

This number describes the average axial stress under the assumed loading. It does not prove the rod is safe by itself. The engineer must compare the stress to an allowable value, account for connections and stress concentrations, and verify the material and service conditions.

Step-by-step method · Stress in a lifting rod

  1. Step 1: Given: F = 9,600 N; net area A = 160 mm².
  2. Step 2: Unknown: average tensile stress σ.
  3. Step 3: Formula: σ = F/A.
  4. Step 4: Substitute: 9,600/160 = 60 N/mm² = 60 MPa.
  5. Step 5: Check against allowable stress; assess connections separately.
Section 05

Worked example 2 · Compression in a machine support

A vertical machine support carries 18,000 N of centered compression. Its cross-sectional area is 300 mm². The average compressive stress magnitude is 18,000/300 = 60 MPa. If using a signed normal-stress convention, σ = −60 MPa for compression.

Suppose a second support carries the same 18,000 N but has twice the area, 600 mm². Its average compressive stress magnitude is 18,000/600 = 30 MPa. Doubling area halves average stress when force is unchanged.

For a long slender column, buckling may govern before the material reaches its compressive strength. The simple F/A calculation does not evaluate buckling. This lesson's area comparisons explicitly assume centered loads and ignore buckling so that students can isolate the stress relationship.

Step-by-step method · Compression in machine supports

  1. Step 1: Given: F = 18,000 N; areas 300 and 600 mm².
  2. Step 2: Use average stress magnitude |σ| = F/A.
  3. Step 3: First support: 18,000/300 = 60 MPa.
  4. Step 4: Second: 18,000/600 = 30 MPa.
  5. Step 5: Doubling area halves stress; the model does not check buckling.
Section 06

Read cross-sectional areas from engineering drawings

Mechanical engineers routinely receive loads and dimensions from CAD drawings rather than from neatly arranged equations. A rectangular tie bar with width b and thickness t has cross-sectional area A = bt. A solid circular rod with diameter d has area A = πd²/4. A hole reduces the available net section and can create a local stress concentration; both effects require attention in detailed design.

Worked example 3: A flat bar is 20 mm wide and 8 mm thick, so A = 20 × 8 = 160 mm². Under 12,800 N of tension, σ = 12,800/160 = 80 MPa. If the thickness increases to 10 mm while force and width stay constant, area becomes 200 mm² and stress falls to 64 MPa.

Section 07

Compare candidate bars and stress ratios

Engineering drawings often show several bars that must be checked against the same stress limit. A bar passes an average-stress screening check if its calculated stress magnitude is at or below the specified allowable stress. You must check each candidate separately, rather than assuming the thickest or lightest-looking bar passes.

Worked example 4: Three tension bars carry the following loads: Bar A, 6,000 N through 100 mm²; Bar B, 8,000 N through 200 mm²; and Bar C, 9,000 N through 150 mm². Their stresses are 60 MPa, 40 MPa, and 60 MPa respectively. With an allowable stress of 50 MPa, only B passes.

To compare stress in A and C, calculate the ratio σA/σC = 60/60 = 1:1. Ratios must be formed from stresses, not directly from loads unless the cross-sectional areas are equal. Two bars can have different forces and still experience the same average stress if the force-to-area ratios match.

Section 08

Design a minimum cross-sectional area

Engineers frequently reverse the stress equation to size a component. If the design specifies a maximum allowable average stress σallow, then Amin = F/σallow. This gives the minimum theoretical cross-sectional area for the simplified axial model.

Worked example 5: A lifting link must carry 15,000 N while average axial stress remains at or below 50 MPa. Because 50 MPa = 50 N/mm², Amin = 15,000/50 = 300 mm². A 20 mm × 15 mm rectangular cross section has exactly 300 mm². In practice, the engineer might select a larger standard size after checking manufacturing tolerances, holes, fatigue, safety factors, and other requirements.

Worked example 6: A solid round rod must carry 10,000 N at an allowable average stress of 80 MPa. Amin = 125 mm². Using A = πd²/4, the theoretical minimum diameter is d = √(4A/π) ≈ 12.62 mm. A designer would choose an available diameter above this minimum and then verify the complete design.

Step-by-step method · Find minimum section and pin diameter

  1. Step 1: For the link, F = 15,000 N and allowable stress = 50 MPa.
  2. Step 2: Rearrange A_min = F/σ_allow = 15,000/50 = 300 mm².
  3. Step 3: Verify rectangular section 20 × 15 = 300 mm².
  4. Step 4: For the separate round rod, A_min = 10,000/80 = 125 mm².
  5. Step 5: Solve d = √(4A/π) ≈ 12.62 mm; select a practical diameter above the minimum.
Section 09

Stress and engineering design decisions

Reducing component area saves material and may lower weight, but increases stress at a given load. Increasing area lowers average stress, but may increase mass, cost, and interference with nearby machine components. The correct choice depends on the constraints, not merely on minimizing the calculated stress.

Consider a robotic lifting linkage where several candidate rod sizes all meet the average-stress limit. The engineer should also examine elastic deformation, fatigue, connection details, corrosion, assembly clearances, and the actual load spectrum. The F/A model is useful for preliminary screening, but it is not a substitute for complete component verification.

The same reasoning applies to compressive members. Even when average compressive stress is low, a long slender support can buckle. Engineers identify which failure mode is relevant before approving a design. That is the difference between solving a physics exercise and performing engineering analysis.

Section 11

Independent engineering practice

Work these problems before expanding their solutions. Include units and explain the engineering implication.

1. A tie rod carries 7,500 N through 150 mm². Calculate tensile stress.

σ = 7,500/150 = 50 MPa.

2. A compression post carries 16,000 N through 400 mm². Find the stress magnitude.

|σ| = 16,000/400 = 40 MPa. Check buckling separately if the member is slender.

3. A rectangular bar is 18 mm wide and 10 mm thick. What is its area?

A = 18 × 10 = 180 mm².

4. A 10,800 N load acts on the bar in problem 3. Calculate stress.

σ = 10,800/180 = 60 MPa.

5. A rod carries 14,000 N. The allowable average stress is 70 MPa. Find minimum area.

Amin = 14,000/70 = 200 mm².

6. Bar A carries 4,000 N over 100 mm²; bar B carries 9,000 N over 150 mm². Compare stresses.

A: 40 MPa; B: 60 MPa. Ratio σA:σB = 2:3.

7. Two identical loads act on two columns, but column B has twice A's area. Compare average stress.

σB = ½σA under centered axial loading, ignoring buckling.

8. Why does a low average stress not guarantee that a hoist bracket is safe?

Local stress concentrations, connections, bending, fatigue, material properties, manufacturing defects, and other failure modes may govern. The axial model checks only one condition.

Section 12

Vocabulary and key concepts

Expand each term to review its engineering meaning.

Normal stress

Internal force per unit area acting perpendicular to a cross section.

Axial load

Force directed along a component's longitudinal axis.

Tension

Axial pulling action that tends to elongate a member.

Compression

Axial pushing action that tends to shorten a member.

Cross-sectional area

Area of a section perpendicular to the applied axial force.

Pascal

SI stress unit: 1 Pa = 1 N/m².

Megapascal

One million pascals; numerically 1 N/mm².

Allowable stress

Specified maximum stress for a particular design check.

Net section

Effective remaining cross-sectional area after features such as holes.

Buckling

Instability that can cause a compressed slender member to deflect laterally.

Stress concentration

Localized stress amplification near changes in geometry or discontinuities.

Design margin

Difference or ratio between a component's demand and an applicable limit.

Section 14

Lesson summary

Mechanical engineers use average normal stress to connect axial force and cross-sectional area: σ = F/A. For force in newtons and area in square millimeters, stress is measured directly in megapascals. Increasing area decreases average stress for the same force; increasing force increases stress for the same area. Engineers can rearrange the equation to size a minimum cross section from an allowable-stress requirement.

These calculations help compare candidate bars, read dimensioned drawings, and justify preliminary sizing choices. They do not by themselves establish that a component is safe. In A6.3, you will examine how materials and components deform under load and why meeting a stress limit does not necessarily prevent excessive deflection.
