Section 01

From prototype to tested design

Engineers rarely build a perfect machine on the first attempt. They create a prototype, measure its performance, compare the results with requirements, and revise the design. This process is called iteration. A change is an improvement only when evidence shows that it better meets the project's objectives without violating mandatory constraints.

Our semester-ending design challenge combines several ideas you have studied: a powered hoist must lift a load, use a support structure, operate within an electrical power limit, and complete the lift before a deadline. You will compare designs using calculations and an interactive simulation rather than guessing which machine looks strongest.

Engineering brief

Design a hoist to raise a 250 kg payload through 4 m in 8 s or less. Available electrical input power is limited to 2,000 W. The support structure must have a factor of safety of at least 2.0, a maximum deflection of 5 mm, and a maximum frame mass of 100 kg. Total equipment cost must not exceed $2,500. All of these are mandatory requirements.

Section 02

Section 03

Calculate the minimum energy before selecting a motor

Raising a load increases its gravitational potential energy. The minimum useful energy transferred to the load is Euseful = mgh. For this project, m = 250 kg, g = 9.8 N/kg, and h = 4 m.

Worked example 1 — Useful energy and power

Euseful = 250 × 9.8 × 4 = 9,800 J. To finish in 8 s, the hoist must provide average useful power of at least 9,800/8 = 1,225 W. This is power delivered to the load, not necessarily the electrical power drawn from the outlet.

Real systems lose energy through friction, heating, electrical resistance, and other processes. If the overall efficiency is η, expressed as a decimal, then Pinput = Puseful/η. The input must be greater than or equal to useful output because efficiency cannot exceed 100% for the complete energy conversion.

Worked example 2 — Efficiency determines feasibility

At 70% efficiency, required input power is 1,225/0.70 = 1,750 W, which fits under the 2,000 W limit. At 60% efficiency, the required input is 1,225/0.60 ≈ 2,041.7 W, which exceeds the limit. A motor can have sufficient useful output power in principle but still fail the electrical supply constraint because of losses.

Step-by-step worked method · Calculate lifting energy and input demand

  1. Step 1: Read m = 250 kg, h = 4 m, t_max = 8 s.
  2. Step 2: Useful energy E = 250 × 9.8 × 4 = 9,800 J.
  3. Step 3: Minimum average useful power = 9,800/8 = 1,225 W.
  4. Step 4: At η = 0.70, input demand = 1,225/0.70 = 1,750 W.
  5. Step 5: Compare with 2,000 W input limit; at 60% efficiency, demand exceeds the limit.
Section 04

Time, power, and the limiting requirement

Suppose a candidate motor draws 1,800 W and its overall efficiency is 75%. Its useful output power is 1,800 × 0.75 = 1,350 W. The predicted lifting time is useful energy divided by useful power: t = Euseful/(Pinputη).

Worked example 3 — Predict completion time

t = 9,800/(1,800 × 0.75) = 9,800/1,350 ≈ 7.26 s. The motor passes the 8-second deadline and the 2,000 W electrical limit in this idealized average-power model.

If you reduce the electrical power to 1,500 W without changing efficiency, the predicted time becomes 9,800/(1,500 × 0.75) ≈ 8.71 s. The design then fails the deadline even though it consumes less electrical power. Lower energy use per second is not automatically a better design if the machine must complete a task by a deadline.

These calculations assume constant average power and efficiency during the lift. Actual motors require checks for startup torque, acceleration, continuous and peak ratings, duty cycle, and thermal performance.

Step-by-step worked method · Predict lift time from motor power

  1. Step 1: Read useful energy 9,800 J, input power 1,800 W, efficiency 0.75.
  2. Step 2: Useful output power = 1,800 × 0.75 = 1,350 W.
  3. Step 3: Time = 9,800/1,350 ≈ 7.26 s.
  4. Step 4: Compare with 8 s deadline: passes.
  5. Step 5: At 1,500 W, time ≈ 8.71 s and fails despite lower input power.
Section 05

Check the structure independently

Even when the motor meets its requirements, the frame may fail. A structure's factor of safety compares capacity with working demand for a specified failure mode. Deflection measures how far the structure bends or moves under load. A strong structure can still be too flexible, and a stiff structure can still fail a strength requirement.

Frame A fails the FoS minimum. Frame C exceeds the mass limit. Frame D exceeds the deflection limit. Only Frame B passes all three structural requirements. These are illustrative engineering-test results, not certified specifications for real lifting equipment.

Real hoists also need suitable cables, hooks, anchors, brakes, and connections. The classroom model screens only the stated requirements; it does not authorize construction or operation.

Step-by-step worked method · Diagnose a failing structural candidate

  1. Step 1: List FoS, deflection, and mass requirements before reading candidates.
  2. Step 2: Frame A fails FoS 1.8; Frame C fails mass 115 kg; Frame D fails deflection 7 mm.
  3. Step 3: Frame B has FoS 2.6, deflection 3 mm, and mass 82 kg.
  4. Step 4: Check Frame B against every other listed constraint.
  5. Step 5: Choose B only within the scope of the simplified test evidence.
Section 06

A prototype can pass one test and fail another

Consider a first prototype with 60% efficiency, 1,900 W electrical input, and Frame A. Its predicted useful output power is 1,900 × 0.60 = 1,140 W, so its lift time is 9,800/1,140 ≈ 8.60 s. It misses the deadline. Frame A also has FoS 1.8, below the required 2.0. The design has at least two separate failures.

Worked example 4 — Revise without hiding the problem

Change to an 80%-efficient motor drawing 1,700 W and Frame B. Useful power = 1,700 × 0.80 = 1,360 W. Predicted time = 9,800/1,360 ≈ 7.21 s. The electrical input is below 2,000 W, and Frame B meets FoS, deflection, and mass requirements. If this motor costs $1,300, total equipment cost is $1,300 + $900 = $2,200, below the $2,500 budget. This revised candidate passes all modeled checks.

Notice the revision improves two different subsystems: motor performance and frame selection. Engineers should record the original results, describe what changed, and repeat the tests rather than simply reporting the final successful attempt.

Step-by-step worked method · Recalculate after a design revision

  1. Step 1: Baseline: useful power = 1,900 × 0.60 = 1,140 W; time = 9,800/1,140 ≈ 8.60 s, failing the deadline.
  2. Step 2: Also record Frame A FoS 1.8 < 2.0: a separate structural failure.
  3. Step 3: Revised motor: 1,700 × 0.80 = 1,360 W useful power; time ≈ 7.21 s.
  4. Step 4: Frame B passes the listed structural checks; total cost = $1,300 + $900 = $2,200.
  5. Step 5: Document both changes and retest each subsystem; use one-variable-at-a-time trials when diagnosing causes.
Section 07

Choose the most informative test

Tests should measure the requirement they claim to verify. To check lifting time, use a stopwatch or time-stamped motion data while raising the specified payload through the specified vertical height. To check electrical input power, use an appropriate power measurement at the defined operating conditions. To check deflection, measure displacement under the required load. A photograph of the machine cannot establish its efficiency or factor of safety.

Worked example 5 — Test plan for a slow hoist

If a hoist takes 9.1 seconds to complete an 8-second task, the next useful measurements are actual electrical input power, motor operating efficiency, and load motion over time. These data help determine whether losses, insufficient input power, or startup behavior explain the delay. Replacing the support frame without evidence of structural failure would not directly address the measured timing problem.

For repeatable tests, keep the load, height, and measurement method consistent. Record the trial conditions, numerical outcome, pass/fail result, and any unusual observations. Repeating a test helps reveal whether a single measurement was unusual.

Step-by-step worked method · Choose a test that diagnoses a slow hoist

  1. Step 1: Identify failed criterion: 9.1 s measured versus 8 s maximum.
  2. Step 2: Keep payload, vertical rise, and measurement method constant.
  3. Step 3: Measure input power, operating efficiency, and motion versus time.
  4. Step 4: Compare data with the predicted time model to locate the limiting cause.
  5. Step 5: Change one variable, rerun the test, and record before/after evidence.

Section 09

Optimize only among feasible designs

Optimization means improving a selected objective while obeying constraints. A designer might minimize cost, electrical input power, machine mass, or lifting time. These goals can conflict. A lower-power motor might save energy per second but fail the deadline. A lighter frame might fail the required safety factor. A faster motor might exceed the available electrical supply.

In the laboratory, the motor cost slider is independent of efficiency and input power so that you can practice multi-constraint decision-making. This is a simplified teaching model; real product costs and efficiency would not be freely independent. A recommendation should name the objective being optimized and the assumptions that support the comparison.

Worked example 6 — Which passing design is preferable?

Prototype X passes every requirement at $2,200 total cost and 1,700 W electrical input. Prototype Y also passes at $2,350 and 1,550 W. If the priority is lowest initial cost, X is preferable. If the priority is lower electrical power draw at the specified operating point, Y may be preferable. Neither can be called universally best without specifying the decision criteria and considering total energy use, duty cycle, and operating costs.

Step-by-step worked method · Optimize among feasible designs

  1. Step 1: Verify that X and Y meet all mandatory criteria.
  2. Step 2: Record X: $2,200 and 1,700 W; Y: $2,350 and 1,550 W.
  3. Step 3: If lowest initial cost is the preference, choose X.
  4. Step 4: If lowest electrical input power is the preference, choose Y.
  5. Step 5: State the chosen objective and trade-off; neither preference excuses a hard-constraint failure.

Section 11

Independent practice with guided solutions

Complete each calculation or design judgment before opening the solution.

1. Find useful lifting energy for a 250 kg load raised 4 m.

E = 250 × 9.8 × 4 = 9,800 J.

2. What useful power is needed to complete that lift in 8 s?

9,800/8 = 1,225 W.

3. At 70% efficiency, what electrical input power is required for the 8 s deadline?

1,225/0.70 = 1,750 W.

4. At 60% efficiency, does a 2,000 W electrical limit allow an 8 s lift?

No. Required input = 1,225/0.60 ≈ 2,041.7 W, above the limit.

5. At 1,800 W input and 75% efficiency, find useful output power.

1,800 × 0.75 = 1,350 W.

6. At 1,350 W useful power, find the time needed to transfer 9,800 J.

9,800/1,350 ≈ 7.26 s.

7. A frame has FoS 1.9 and the minimum is 2.0. Does it pass?

No. It fails the safety requirement.

8. A frame deflects 6 mm under load, but the maximum is 5 mm. Does it pass?

No. It fails the deflection limit.

9. Motor cost is $1,350 and frame cost is $900. Find total equipment cost.

$1,350 + $900 = $2,250.

10. A prototype passes every requirement except the 8 s deadline. What should be investigated?

Measure lift time, electrical input power, operating efficiency, and startup behavior to diagnose the timing failure.

11. Two prototypes pass all constraints. One costs less, the other draws less power. Which is best?

It depends on the stated optimization objective. Neither is universally best without priorities.

12. Why must an engineer retest after changing a motor or frame?

A change can improve one requirement while worsening another. Retesting provides evidence that all mandatory constraints remain satisfied.

Section 12

Glossary: prototype testing and optimization

Select each aqua vocabulary term to reveal its definition.

Prototype

An early version of a system built or modeled to evaluate a design.

Iteration

A cycle of testing, revising, and testing again.

Acceptance criterion

A measurable condition a design must satisfy to pass a requirement.

Constraint

A mandatory design limit such as maximum mass, time, power, or cost.

Feasible design

A design that meets every mandatory requirement.

Optimization

Improving an objective while keeping all constraints satisfied.

Useful energy

Energy transferred to accomplish the intended task.

Input power

The rate of energy supplied to a system.

Efficiency

Useful output divided by input for corresponding energy or power quantities.

Factor of safety

A capacity-to-demand ratio for a specified failure mode.

Deflection

Displacement of a loaded structural member.

Test plan

A documented procedure specifying conditions, measurements, and pass/fail criteria.

Controlled variable

A condition kept the same so that test results can be compared fairly.

Design trade-off

A situation in which improving one objective can worsen another.

Verification

Checking with evidence whether a design meets its specified requirements.

Model assumption

A simplification used to make an engineering calculation or simulation manageable.

Section 13

Lesson summary and next steps

A successful engineering prototype must satisfy every mandatory requirement. Begin with measurable criteria, calculate useful energy and power, check motor efficiency and electrical limits, and verify the structure independently. When a design fails, identify the specific failure, make a justified change, and repeat the test. After finding feasible designs, optimize according to a clearly stated objective rather than assuming that the fastest, cheapest, or strongest option is automatically best.

Exam readiness: diagnose and correct excessive beam deflection

A prototype can satisfy its mass and strength requirements but still fail because it bends too far. Deflection is a displacement under load, not a measure of whether the material has yielded. When a test reports deflection above the allowable limit, the next step is to investigate stiffness while preserving the other design constraints.

Prototype test · Identify the failed requirement

A support beam must deflect no more than 4.0 mm under a specified test load and have mass no more than 2.0 kg. Prototype A has mass 1.45 kg and measured deflection 5.8 mm. The mass requirement passes, but the deflection requirement fails. The engineer should not claim the design passes because it is lightweight.

Why greater section depth can help

For the same material, span, support conditions, and load, the bending deflection of a beam depends strongly on its cross-sectional geometry. A deeper section generally increases the second moment of area, making it harder to bend in the relevant direction. For a solid rectangular cross-section, I = bh³/12, where b is width and h is depth measured in the bending direction. Doubling h at fixed b increases I eightfold, but it also doubles the section's area and mass for the same length. Real redesigns must check both stiffness and mass.

Revise one design feature and retest both limits

The engineer increases beam section depth and runs the same loaded test with unchanged span, supports, and applied load. The revised beam's mass is 1.82 kg, and measured deflection is 3.6 mm. Both limits now pass. The evidence supports adopting the revised design for these tested requirements, subject to any additional strength, connection, and service requirements.

Good experimental practice: Change the section depth, document the before-and-after dimensions, repeat the deflection test at the same load, and measure the revised mass. Changing the load, span, and beam depth all at once makes it harder to identify the cause of improvement.

Try it independently

Practice A · A beam weighs 1.6 kg and deflects 4.7 mm. Limits are 2.0 kg and 4.0 mm. What failed?

Mass passes, but deflection fails. Investigate a stiffer geometry or other suitable design change.

Practice B · A deeper replacement deflects 3.2 mm but weighs 2.1 kg. Is the redesign acceptable?

No. It passes deflection but violates the 2.0 kg mass limit.

Practice C · Which evidence best supports a claim that a deeper beam fixed the problem?

Before-and-after section drawings, deflection measurements under the same load and supports, and both mass measurements.

Practice D · For a rectangular beam, if section depth doubles at constant width, what happens to I = bh³/12?

Second moment of area increases by 8 times (2³ = 8). Actual deflection changes also depend on the full beam and loading configuration.
