REVIEW 01

Unit 1 · What mechanical engineers do

The final exam distinguishes design engineering from routine maintenance, inspection, and manufacturing work. Mechanical engineers apply physics and mathematics to design machines and components that perform useful functions within constraints. A manufacturing engineer focuses on production methods, tooling, and repeatability; a test engineer gathers performance evidence; an analysis engineer may use simulation to predict performance before a prototype exists.

Recognize a system's input, process, and intended output. For a bicycle drive train, the rider applies torque to pedals, a chain and sprockets transmit motion, and the driven wheel rotates to propel the bicycle. If a conveyor motor shaft rotates normally but the belt slips, investigate the transmission interface rather than assuming the controller is at fault.

Final-exam thinking

Repeated loading can cause fatigue cracks even when a component survives a single static test. A crack beginning at a sharp corner and growing over thousands of cycles is evidence of fatigue. Engineering ethics requires reporting a known hazard and taking appropriate action; a warning label alone does not resolve an unaddressed failure mechanism.

REVIEW 02

Unit 2 · Design process, decisions, and drawings

The engineering design process is iterative: define the problem, establish measurable criteria, generate concepts, build or model a prototype, test it, and revise it based on evidence. If a prototype fails, identify the cause, make a justified change, and retest. Do not simply delete the failed requirement.

To screen candidate designs, apply all mandatory constraints first. Only then use cost, mass, or other preferences to rank passing options. If priorities conflict and the customer has not ranked them, ask the customer to clarify the priorities. A weighted decision matrix multiplies each rating by its weighting and adds the products.

Worked example · Weighted decision score

Strength rating 5 at 50% weight, cost rating 3 at 30%, and mass rating 4 at 20% gives 5(0.50) + 3(0.30) + 4(0.20) = 2.5 + 0.9 + 0.8 = 4.2.

Read engineering drawings using labeled dimensions, not the apparent size of a sketch. Front and top views together reveal width, height, and depth. Dimensions and tolerances are important: a bracket may align with mounting holes but still interfere with a neighboring part if thickness was omitted. For controlled experiments, vary the independent variable while holding relevant conditions constant. Detailed dated notebooks preserve test evidence and design decisions. A patent, unlike a trademark or copyright, may protect a qualifying invention.

REVIEW 03

Unit 3 · Units, mathematics, graphs, and measurement

Before calculating, convert quantities to compatible units. 1 kN = 1,000 N; 1 m = 1,000 mm. Check dimensions: speed in m/s multiplied by time in seconds produces meters. When solving P = F/A, minimum contact area is A = F/Pallowable. If F = 9,000 N and allowable pressure is 3 N/mm², area must be at least 3,000 mm².

Use geometry accurately. Rectangle area is length × width; circle area is πr². When removing holes from a plate, subtract the total hole area. Use the Pythagorean theorem for right triangles: a² + b² = c². For a 6–8–10 triangle, the hypotenuse is 10 units.

Worked example · Reading experimental data

A spring extends 3 mm under 15 N and 9 mm under 45 N. Slope of force versus extension = (45 − 15)/(9 − 3) = 5 N/mm. A position–time graph with a constant slope indicates constant velocity. A velocity–time graph's slope gives acceleration, while its area gives displacement.

Distinguish accuracy (closeness to the true value) from repeatability/precision (closeness of repeated measurements to one another). Readings clustered near 20.0 mm when the verified dimension is 20.5 mm are repeatable but systematically inaccurate. Averages summarize repeated trials; a single trial is not enough to prove a universal relationship.

REVIEW 04

Unit 4 · Forces, free-body diagrams, and friction

Forces have magnitude and direction. Draw a free-body diagram showing the weight downward, the normal force perpendicular to a surface, applied forces, tension, and friction where relevant. Weight is W = mg, using g ≈ 9.8 m/s². If two identical vertical cables share a stationary load equally, each cable carries half the total weight.

Static friction adjusts to match an applied force until its maximum is reached. Kinetic friction acts during sliding and may be smaller than the maximum static friction. Newton's first law describes balanced forces and unchanged velocity. Newton's second law is ΣF = ma. Newton's third law pairs equal and opposite forces acting on different objects.

Worked example · Net horizontal force

A 60 kg cart is pushed right with 240 N while friction acts left with 60 N. Net force = 240 − 60 = 180 N right. Acceleration = 180/60 = 3 m/s² right. If the cart moves at constant velocity instead, net force must be zero.

Pay attention to signs and force directions. A larger applied force does not always mean acceleration equals applied force divided by mass; opposing forces must be included first.

REVIEW 05

Unit 5 · Moments, equilibrium, and machines

Torque or moment is the turning effect of a force about a pivot: τ = Fd⊥, where d⊥ is the perpendicular moment arm. In static equilibrium, ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0. These conditions allow you to calculate reactions and compare lever designs.

Worked example · Balance a lever

A 150 N force acts 0.4 m to the left of a pivot, creating 60 N·m of torque. A force applied 0.3 m to the right must be 60/0.3 = 200 N in the opposite turning direction for equilibrium.

Simple machines can trade force for distance. A pulley arrangement may reduce the input force required but increase the rope length that must be pulled. Mechanical advantage does not create energy. A gear train changes torque and speed according to gear ratio; increasing output torque generally decreases output rotational speed in an ideal transmission. For a system to be stable, structural members must provide appropriate load paths and resist sideways motion; a triangulated frame generally resists racking better than an unbraced rectangle.

REVIEW 06

Unit 6 · Materials, stress, strain, and structures

Stress is force divided by area: σ = F/A. With force in newtons and area in mm², the result is N/mm² or MPa. Strain is change in length divided by original length and has no units. In the linear elastic region, Young's modulus relates stress and strain: E = σ/ε.

Worked example · Safety factor

A rod supports 12,000 N over 150 mm². Stress = 12,000/150 = 80 MPa. If yield strength is 240 MPa, the yield-based factor of safety is 240/80 = 3.0. A required minimum FoS of 2.0 is met for this defined failure mode.

Strength and stiffness are different. Strength concerns resisting failure; stiffness concerns resisting deformation. A beam may pass a stress limit but fail a deflection limit. For material selection, eliminate candidates that fail required strength, stiffness, temperature, corrosion, or other conditions before selecting the lightest or cheapest acceptable one. Lower density reduces mass for a fixed volume, but density alone does not establish structural adequacy.

REVIEW 07

Unit 7 · Motion, graphs, momentum, and collisions

Distance is the total path traveled, while displacement includes direction from start to finish. Average velocity = displacement/time; average speed = distance/time. Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by time: a = Δv/Δt. For constant acceleration, use v = v₀ + at and Δx = v₀t + ½at².

Worked example · Velocity–time graph

A cart's speed increases linearly from 2 m/s to 10 m/s over 4 s. Acceleration = (10 − 2)/4 = 2 m/s². Distance traveled is the area under the graph: average velocity × time = [(2 + 10)/2] × 4 = 24 m, assuming velocity remains positive.

Momentum is p = mv. In a collision of objects that stick together, total momentum is conserved if external impulse is negligible. Impulse = Δp = FavgΔt. Extending stopping time lowers average net force for the same momentum change. When selecting a crash barrier, first apply constraints such as maximum deformation and cost; then compare average stopping forces among feasible options.

REVIEW 08

Unit 8 · Work, energy, power, and efficiency

Work done by a constant force is W = Fd cos θ, where θ is the angle between the force and displacement. Only the component parallel to motion contributes to work. Kinetic energy is KE = ½mv²; doubling speed multiplies kinetic energy by four, and tripling speed multiplies it by nine.

Gravitational potential energy changes by ΔPE = mgh. For an ideal frictionless descent from rest, the loss of potential energy becomes kinetic energy: mgh = ½mv², giving v = √(2gh). Average power is work or energy transferred per unit time: P = W/t. Efficiency is useful output divided by input, expressed as a decimal in formulas or percentage in reports.

Worked example · Choose a feasible lift

A load gains 4,900 J of potential energy. Lift A takes 7 s and uses 6,000 J input; Lift B takes 11 s and uses 5,500 J input. If the deadline is 9 s, only A qualifies, even though B is more energy efficient. Lift A efficiency = 4,900/6,000 ≈ 81.7%. This is a constraints-first decision, not simply a search for the highest efficiency.

In an energy-flow diagram, input energy equals useful output plus non-useful transfers. If input is 4,000 J, useful output 2,700 J, and measured heat transfer 800 J, remaining non-useful transfers total 500 J.

REVIEW 09

Unit 9 · Integrated design, testing, and trade-offs

The final unit combines the preceding skills. A design brief should contain measurable success criteria. To find maximum cart mass, use m = (Fdrive − Fresist)/a. To size a lift's average electrical input power, use Pinput = mgh/(tη). Select a motor rating that is at least the calculated demand, while remembering that startup torque, duty cycle, and peak conditions require separate checks.

Worked example · Material selection

A 100 mm² tension member carries 10,000 N and requires FoS ≥ 2 against yielding. Working stress = 100 MPa; minimum yield strength = 2 × 100 = 200 MPa. Candidate X has density 2.8 g/cm³ and yield strength 180 MPa; Y has density 4.5 and yield strength 250 MPa; Z has density 7.8 and yield strength 400 MPa. X fails strength. Y is the lowest-density passing material.

For stopping systems, remember d ∝ v² at constant braking deceleration and Favg = |Δp|/Δt. For conveyors, useful output power must satisfy the requirement and electrical input must stay within its limit. If a prototype meets mandatory requirements but misses a preference such as a lower mass target, improve the preference while preserving safety and compliance. Change and test variables systematically, record before-and-after evidence, and support a final safety claim with relevant verified calculations and rated-load testing.

REVIEW 10

How to approach the final examination

The supplied final-exam bank contains multiple-choice questions with both numerical and conceptual reasoning. Many questions present a table, graph, or labeled engineering diagram. Some concepts appear in alternate versions, so memorize neither a single number nor the position of an answer choice. Learn the method that works when values change.

Use this four-step routine: (1) identify what is asked and which limits are mandatory; (2) write the governing relationship and convert units; (3) calculate carefully or compare each candidate against every constraint; (4) check whether the result is physically reasonable. For diagram questions, use the printed dimensions, not the image's apparent scale. For graph questions, decide whether you need the slope, the area, or a trend.
