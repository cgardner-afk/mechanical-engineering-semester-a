Semester objective A-O4: Analyze forces and motion using Newton's laws, vectors, and free-body diagrams.

Unit essential question: How do forces determine the motion or equilibrium of mechanical systems?

Learning target: Explain Newton's three laws and use net force, mass, and acceleration to solve introductory engineering problems.

SECTION 01

Why engineers study motion

A forklift starts moving, a car slows down, and a rocket rises into the sky. All three involve forces. Engineers need to predict what happens when a force acts on an object so they can design machines that move safely and do the job they are supposed to do.

In Lesson A4.1, you learned that a force is a push or pull, measured in newtons. You also learned that mass is measured in kilograms. Now we will connect force and mass to acceleration, which is how quickly velocity changes. Three rules, called Newton's laws of motion, explain much of the motion we see in everyday machines.

These laws help engineers answer practical questions. Why does a loaded cart take more force to speed up? Why do seat belts matter when a vehicle stops? Why can a rocket move forward by pushing exhaust gases backward? By the end of the lesson, you will be able to explain these situations and calculate motion using net force.

SECTION 02

Newton's first law: inertia

Newton's first law says that an object at rest stays at rest, and an object moving at a constant velocity continues moving at that velocity, unless a net external force acts on it. Constant velocity means moving at the same speed in the same direction. A change in speed or direction counts as acceleration.

This law is often called the law of inertia. Inertia is an object's resistance to a change in motion. A loaded shopping cart has more mass and therefore more inertia than an empty cart. It is harder to get moving, harder to stop, and harder to turn.

Imagine a puck sliding across smooth ice. If there were no friction or other unbalanced forces, it would keep moving in a straight line at a constant speed. In the real world, friction and air resistance gradually slow it down. The puck does not stop because motion naturally runs out; it stops because forces act on it.

Important distinction: A moving object does not need a forward net force to keep moving at constant velocity. It needs a net force to change its velocity.
Think like an engineer:

A truck stops suddenly, but a loose toolbox slides toward the front of the truck. Why?

Show explanation

The toolbox tends to keep its previous forward velocity because of inertia. A force, such as friction or contact with the truck's front wall, is needed to slow it down.

SECTION 03

Net force and balanced forces

More than one force can act on an object at the same time. The net force is the combined effect of all the forces acting on that object. Forces have directions, so forces pointing opposite ways subtract rather than add. In a simple one-dimensional problem, we can choose right as positive and left as negative.

If the net force is zero, the forces are balanced. The object does not accelerate: it either remains at rest or continues moving with constant velocity. If the net force is not zero, the forces are unbalanced and the object accelerates in the direction of the net force.

Worked example 1 · Find the net force

  1. Choose a positive direction: right is positive.
  2. List the forces: 90 N right and 30 N left.
  3. Add with signs: Fnet = +90 N − 30 N.
  4. Calculate: Fnet = 60 N to the right.
  5. Interpret: The forces are unbalanced, so the cart accelerates to the right.
Your turn:

Two students push a box: 55 N east and 55 N west. What is the net force? What can you conclude about its acceleration?

Show worked answer

Fnet = 55 − 55 = 0 N. The box has zero acceleration. It could be stationary or moving at constant velocity.

SECTION 04

Newton's second law: F = ma

Newton's second law describes how a net force changes an object's motion. It states that the object's acceleration depends on the net force acting on it and its mass. A larger net force causes greater acceleration when mass stays the same. A larger mass causes less acceleration when net force stays the same.

Fnet = m × a
SymbolMeaningSI unit
FnetCombined external forcenewtons (N)
mMasskilograms (kg)
aAccelerationmeters per second squared (m/s²)

The word net matters. If an engine pushes a cart forward with 200 N while friction pulls backward with 50 N, the net force is 150 N forward—not 200 N. Use that net force in Fnet = ma.

The units also help check your work: kg × m/s² = N. If you are solving for acceleration, the result should be in m/s². If you are solving for mass, the result should be in kg.

Worked example 2 · Calculate force

  1. Given: m = 12 kg and a = 2.5 m/s² to the right.
  2. Write the equation: Fnet = ma.
  3. Substitute: Fnet = (12 kg)(2.5 m/s²).
  4. Calculate: Fnet = 30 N to the right.
  5. Check: kg·m/s² equals newtons.
SECTION 05

Solving for acceleration or mass

Engineers do not always need to find force. Sometimes they know the net force and mass and need to predict acceleration. Other times they know the net force and acceleration and need to find mass. Rearrange the same equation instead of memorizing a different formula for each problem.

a = Fnet ÷ m    |    m = Fnet ÷ a

Worked example 3 · Find acceleration

  1. Given: a 20 kg cart experiences a net force of 80 N to the right.
  2. Start: Fnet = ma.
  3. Divide by mass: a = Fnet/m.
  4. Substitute: a = 80 N ÷ 20 kg.
  5. Calculate: a = 4 m/s² to the right.

Worked example 4 · Find mass

  1. Given: a net force of 150 N produces an acceleration of 3 m/s².
  2. Start: Fnet = ma.
  3. Divide by acceleration: m = Fnet/a.
  4. Calculate: m = 150 ÷ 3 = 50 kg.
  5. Check: 50 kg × 3 m/s² = 150 N.
Your turn:

A 16 kg cart experiences 120 N forward and 40 N backward. Find its net force and acceleration.

Show worked answer

Fnet = 120 − 40 = 80 N forward. Then a = 80 ÷ 16 = 5 m/s² forward.

SECTION 06

How mass and force affect acceleration

Newton's second law lets us make predictions before testing a machine. If mass stays constant and net force doubles, acceleration doubles. If net force stays constant and mass doubles, acceleration is cut in half. These relationships explain why a small electric cart accelerates more quickly when it is empty than when it carries a heavy load, assuming the motor provides the same net force.

Net forceMassAcceleration
40 N10 kg4 m/s²
80 N10 kg8 m/s²
40 N20 kg2 m/s²
80 N20 kg4 m/s²

Read the table in pairs. The first and second rows keep mass at 10 kg while doubling force from 40 N to 80 N. Acceleration doubles from 4 m/s² to 8 m/s². The first and third rows keep force at 40 N while doubling mass. Acceleration drops from 4 m/s² to 2 m/s².

Be careful: More force does not always mean more speed at every moment. Force causes acceleration, a change in velocity. To find the object's actual speed, you also need information about its starting velocity and how long the acceleration acts.
SECTION 07

Newton's third law: force pairs

Newton's third law says that when object A pushes or pulls on object B, object B pushes or pulls back on object A with an equal-magnitude force in the opposite direction. These are called an action–reaction pair. Both forces occur at the same time.

For example, when you push against a wall, the wall pushes back on you. When a tire pushes backward on the road, the road pushes forward on the tire. A rocket pushes exhaust gases backward, and the gases push the rocket forward.

The most important detail is that the two forces act on different objects. That is why they do not cancel each other when you calculate the net force on one object. To identify a third-law pair, name both objects: “the tire pushes the road backward” and “the road pushes the tire forward.”

Your turn:

A swimmer pushes water backward with their hands. What is the matching third-law force, and what object does it act on?

Show explanation

The water pushes the swimmer forward with an equal and opposite force. The backward force acts on the water; the forward force acts on the swimmer.

SECTION 08

Why equal forces do not always mean equal motion

Students sometimes wonder how a small car can push on a large truck with the same force that the truck pushes on the car. Newton's third law says the forces are equal, but Newton's second law explains why the resulting accelerations may be different. If two objects experience forces of the same magnitude, the one with less mass has greater acceleration, assuming the stated forces are the relevant net forces.

Imagine two people wearing ice skates, standing face-to-face on a smooth sheet of ice. An ice skate has a narrow metal blade that slides easily over ice, so there is very little friction to stop the people from moving. The two people are initially standing still and touch their hands together. When Skater A pushes against Skater B's hands, both people slide backward, away from each other. Skater A moves left and Skater B moves right. Skater B does not have to make a separate effort to push back: the two equal-and-opposite interaction forces happen at the same time.

Now suppose Skater A has a mass of 40 kg and Skater B has a mass of 60 kg. During the push, each experiences a force of 60 N in opposite directions. For Skater A, acceleration = 60 ÷ 40 = 1.5 m/s² to the left. For Skater B, acceleration = 60 ÷ 60 = 1.0 m/s² to the right. The forces are equal, but the lighter person accelerates more because the same force acts on less mass. We are treating friction as small enough to ignore during the brief push.

Also remember that a third-law pair is not the same as two balanced forces on one object. A book resting on a table has gravity pulling down on the book and the table pushing up on the book. Those forces can balance, but they are not a third-law pair because both act on the book. The matching third-law partner of the table's upward force on the book is the book's downward force on the table.

SECTION 09

Applying Newton's laws to machines

Engineers use Newton's laws when designing vehicles, conveyors, robots, and lifting equipment. The first law explains why an object continues moving unless a net force changes its velocity. The second law helps calculate how much net force is needed to accelerate a known mass. The third law helps identify how interacting objects push and pull on each other.

Suppose an automated warehouse cart carries a 30 kg load, and the cart itself has a mass of 20 kg. The total mass is 50 kg. If the engineer wants the cart to accelerate at 1.2 m/s², the required net force is Fnet = 50 × 1.2 = 60 N. If friction opposes motion with 15 N, the drive system must supply 75 N forward to leave a net force of 60 N forward.

Worked example 5 · Motor force with resistance

  1. Find total mass: 20 + 30 = 50 kg.
  2. Calculate required net force: 50 × 1.2 = 60 N forward.
  3. Include resistance: motor force − 15 N = 60 N.
  4. Solve: motor force = 75 N forward.
  5. Check: 75 − 15 = 60 N net; 60 ÷ 50 = 1.2 m/s².

This is a simplified straight-line model. A real design also considers changing friction, slopes, wheel traction, braking, and other forces. Later lessons in Unit A4 will show how to draw force diagrams and analyze friction in more detail.

SECTION 11

Independent engineering practice

Try each problem before opening its answer. Read carefully to decide whether you need to add forces, subtract forces, or rearrange Fnet = ma.

Problem A · Balanced forces

A crate experiences 65 N right and 65 N left. Find its net force and acceleration.

Show worked solution

Net force = 65 − 65 = 0 N. Acceleration = 0 m/s². The crate could be at rest or moving at constant velocity.

Problem B · Calculate acceleration

A 24 kg cart has a net force of 96 N to the left. Find its acceleration.

Show worked solution

a = Fnet/m = 96 ÷ 24 = 4 m/s² to the left.

Problem C · Calculate net force

A 35 kg machine part accelerates at 2.4 m/s² forward. What net force acts on it?

Show worked solution

Fnet = ma = 35 × 2.4 = 84 N forward.

Problem D · Applied force versus net force

A robot applies 110 N forward to a 15 kg cart while resistance is 35 N backward. Find net force and acceleration.

Show worked solution

Net force = 110 − 35 = 75 N forward. Acceleration = 75 ÷ 15 = 5 m/s² forward.

Problem E · Action–reaction

A skateboarder pushes backward on the ground. Identify the matching third-law force and explain why these two forces do not cancel on the skateboarder.

Show explanation

The ground pushes forward on the skateboarder. The backward force acts on the ground; the forward force acts on the skateboarder. Because they act on different objects, they do not cancel when finding the net force on the skateboarder.

VOCABULARY & GLOSSARY

Vocabulary and glossary

Newton's first law

An object maintains its state of rest or constant-velocity motion unless a net external force acts on it.

Inertia

Resistance to a change in motion; an object with greater mass has greater inertia.

Net force

The combined effect of all external forces acting on a chosen object.

Balanced forces

Forces that combine to give zero net force, so acceleration is zero.

Newton's second law

Fnet = ma: net force equals mass multiplied by acceleration.

Acceleration

The rate at which velocity changes, including changes in speed or direction; measured in m/s².

Newton's third law

Two interacting objects exert equal-magnitude, opposite-direction forces on each other.

Action–reaction pair

A pair of forces that act on two different objects during the same interaction.

Lesson summary

Newton's first law explains inertia: motion does not change without a net external force. Newton's second law connects net force, mass, and acceleration through Fnet = ma. Always identify the object you are analyzing, combine forces with their directions, and use net force—not just one applied force—in calculations.
