SECTION 01

Why Engineers Need Technical Drawings

A sketch helps an engineer communicate an early idea, but a manufacturer needs more precise information to make a part. Imagine designing a rectangular mounting plate that will attach a small mechanism to a frame. A rough picture can show the general shape, yet it may not tell a machinist the plate's exact width, depth, thickness, or hole locations. Different people could build different parts from the same vague sketch.

Engineers use technical drawings to communicate shape, size, and manufacturing requirements. A useful drawing answers several questions: What does the object look like from each direction? How large is it? Where are features located? Which measurements are critical? What material and allowable dimensional variation are specified? These details help people manufacture, inspect, assemble, and revise a product consistently.

Throughout this lesson we will study a simple mounting plate: 120 mm wide, 80 mm deep, and 10 mm thick, with two circular through-holes of diameter 12 mm. Their centers are 80 mm apart and symmetrically placed across the width. This same part will appear in drawings, worked calculations, and the interactive lab so the concepts remain connected.

SECTION 02

Sketches, Diagrams, and Specifications

A freehand engineering sketch is a quick visual record of an idea. It may not be perfectly to scale, but it should still be legible, labeled, and clear about the intended arrangement. Engineers use sketches to explore concepts, communicate during brainstorming, and record possible improvements before preparing a more precise drawing.

A technical drawing uses standardized conventions to describe a part accurately. It may include several views, dimensions, symbols, notes, and a title block. A diagram often emphasizes relationships or functions rather than exact physical shape; for example, a diagram of a mechanism may show how motion passes from a motor to a gear and then to a shaft. Do not assume that every diagram gives manufacturing dimensions.

A specification is a written requirement that complements the drawing. It might state the material, surface finish, allowable tolerance, or required strength. For our mounting plate, a simple specification could say: “Plate thickness 10 mm; two through-holes Ø12 mm; nominal width 120 mm; nominal depth 80 mm; dimensions in millimeters.” These numbers are illustrative design requirements, not a claim that a particular plate is safe for any load.

SECTION 03

Understanding Orthographic Views

It is difficult to measure an object precisely from a single three-dimensional-looking picture because perspective can hide features and distort lengths. Orthographic projection solves this problem by showing an object as viewed straight on from specific directions. The most common views are front, top, and right side. Each view shows two of the object's three main dimensions.

For a rectangular plate lying flat, the top view shows its width and depth, so you can see the outlines of both holes as circles. The front view shows width and thickness, so the plate looks like a thin rectangle. The right-side view shows depth and thickness. The holes run through the plate's thickness; in the front and side views their internal edges may be represented with hidden lines where needed, but the top view is the clearest place to dimension their circular openings.

To interpret an unfamiliar drawing, first identify the labeled view. Then ask which two dimensions that viewing direction reveals. Finally, match features across views. The same plate cannot be 120 mm wide in the top view and 110 mm wide in the front view unless the drawing depicts different geometry or contains an error.

Drawing-layout caution: In formal drafting, view placement depends on whether first-angle or third-angle projection is used. This lesson labels the views explicitly rather than relying on their positions. Do not infer a projection convention from the illustration alone.

SECTION 04

Dimension Lines, Symbols, and Hole Locations

A dimension is a numerical statement of size or location. In technical drawings, dimension lines typically carry the measurement and terminate at arrowheads; extension lines show which edges or points are being measured. A dimension should be read from its stated number and units, not estimated by measuring pixels on a screen. The drawing may be resized on a phone, a projector, or a printed page.

For our plate, the top view specifies 120 mm overall width and 80 mm overall depth. The symbol Ø means diameter, so Ø12 mm identifies a circular hole that is 12 mm across. A centerline indicates the center or axis of a feature. Center-to-center spacing is measured from the middle of one hole to the middle of another, not between their nearest edges.

Assume the holes lie on the plate's depth centerline, halfway between its front and back edges. That puts each hole center 80 ÷ 2 = 40 mm from either depth edge. If their horizontal center-to-center spacing is 80 mm and the layout is symmetric across a 120 mm width, each hole center lies (120 − 80) ÷ 2 = 20 mm from the nearest side edge. These are location dimensions; they are different from the holes' 12 mm diameters.

SECTION 05

Calculation 1: Scale Drawings

Sometimes a part is too large to fit on a page or too small to see clearly. A scale expresses the relationship between a length on the drawing and the corresponding real length. At 1:2 scale, one unit on paper represents two units on the actual part. At 2:1 scale, two units on paper represent one unit on the actual part. A scale of 1:1 is full size.

Use this three-step procedure to avoid reversing the ratio.

Step 1 — Read the scale as drawing : actual

A scale of 1:2 means the drawing length is half the actual length. A scale of 2:1 means the drawing is twice the actual length.

Step 2 — Write the relationship

Drawing length = Actual length × (drawing scale number ÷ actual scale number)

To find the actual length instead, reverse the ratio: Actual length = Drawing length × (actual scale number ÷ drawing scale number).

Step 3 — Substitute and calculate

Our plate is 120 mm wide. At a scale of 1:2, the drawing width is 120 × (1 ÷ 2) = 60 mm. If a different drawing measures 45 mm at a scale of 1:3, the real feature is 45 × (3 ÷ 1) = 135 mm. Keep both lengths in the same units while using the ratio. The printed dimension, when provided, takes precedence over a ruler measurement on a resized digital drawing.

Exam skill: A 60 mm line drawn at 1:2 represents 120 mm in reality—not 30 mm. Read the order “drawing : actual” before multiplying.
SECTION 06

Calculation 2: Rectangle Area and Circular Holes

Engineers calculate the area of a flat part to estimate material coverage, plan fabrication, or compare designs. The area of a rectangle is its width multiplied by its depth. A hole removes area from the face, so the area remaining is the outer rectangle's area minus the area of each circular opening. This is a two-dimensional face-area calculation, not a stress analysis or a full structural safety assessment.

Step 1 — Calculate the full rectangular area

Rectangle area = width × depth

For our 120 mm by 80 mm plate, 120 × 80 = 9,600 mm². The squared unit matters because area describes a surface rather than a length.

Step 2 — Calculate the area of one circular hole

Circle area = π × radius²; radius = diameter ÷ 2

Each hole has a diameter of 12 mm, so its radius is 12 ÷ 2 = 6 mm. The area of one hole is π × 6² = 36π ≈ 113.10 mm². Be careful not to use the diameter as the radius; that would make the calculated hole area four times too large.

Step 3 — Subtract the area of all holes

Remaining face area = rectangle area − (number of holes × one-hole area)

There are two holes, so the total removed area is 2 × 113.10 ≈ 226.19 mm². The remaining face area is 9,600 − 226.19 ≈ 9,373.81 mm². We round only the final displayed result. This method assumes the circular holes lie entirely within the rectangle and do not overlap.

Notice that the plate's 10 mm thickness is not needed to calculate its top-face area. Thickness would matter for a volume calculation, which is a different measurement.

SECTION 07

Calculation 3: Hole Spacing and Symmetry

Engineers often locate holes by measuring from edges and centerlines. If two identical holes are placed symmetrically across a plate, the space between their centers plus the two equal edge-to-center offsets must equal the total plate width. This relationship lets a drawing specify precise locations without guessing from appearance.

Step 1 — Identify the overall width and center spacing

Our plate is 120 mm wide, and the two hole centers are 80 mm apart.

Step 2 — Subtract the center spacing from the width

The combined edge-to-center distance is 120 − 80 = 40 mm.

Step 3 — Divide the remainder equally between the two ends

Symmetric edge-to-center offset = (plate width − center spacing) ÷ 2

Each offset is 40 ÷ 2 = 20 mm. The center positions measured from the left edge are therefore 20 mm and 100 mm. Checking: 100 − 20 = 80 mm between centers, and 120 − 100 = 20 mm at the right edge. If you instead need the distance between the nearest edges of the holes, subtract one diameter from the center spacing: 80 − 12 = 68 mm. The question must specify whether it asks for center-to-center or edge-to-edge distance.

SECTION 09

Tolerances, Notes, and Drawing Quality

A real manufactured part is rarely exactly the nominal dimension to infinitely many decimal places. A tolerance specifies the permitted variation. For example, a width of 120 ± 0.5 mm means the acceptable width ranges from 119.5 mm to 120.5 mm. A measured width of 120.3 mm passes that dimensional requirement; 120.8 mm does not. Tolerance is not the same as drawing scale: tolerance governs what measurements are acceptable on the manufactured part.

Technical drawings should identify units and use consistent dimensions. The same feature should not be given contradictory dimensions in different views. Important holes need unambiguous diameter and location information. Notes may identify material, finish, revision, or inspection requirements. A title block commonly records the part name, drawing number, scale, date, revision, and other document information so teams can distinguish current drawings from obsolete versions.

Engineers also distinguish visible edges from hidden features. A solid visible line commonly marks an edge that can be seen from the chosen direction; dashed hidden lines can represent edges behind the visible surface. Centerlines locate axes and symmetrical features. These conventions make drawings more useful than an unlabeled picture, but their precise implementation depends on the drawing standard being used.

SECTION 10

Worked Interpretation: Read the Plate Before You Calculate

Imagine a manufacturing technician receives the plate drawing from this lesson and must confirm that the design is understood correctly. A reliable interpretation follows a connected sequence.

Step 1 — Identify the views and dimensions

The top view gives the 120 mm width and 80 mm depth and shows the two circular holes. The front view shows the 120 mm width and 10 mm thickness. The right-side view shows the 80 mm depth and 10 mm thickness. The hole diameter is Ø12 mm, the horizontal center spacing is 80 mm, and the layout is symmetric.

Step 2 — Translate the drawing into calculations

The overall face area is 120 × 80 = 9,600 mm². Each hole has radius 6 mm and area π × 6² ≈ 113.10 mm². With two holes, the remaining face area is 9,600 − 2π × 6² ≈ 9,373.81 mm². Each horizontal edge-to-center offset is (120 − 80) ÷ 2 = 20 mm. At 1:2 scale, the 120 mm width would occupy 60 mm on an accurately printed drawing.

Step 3 — Check the result against the specification

The technician checks that the hole centers and diameters match the dimensioned drawing, that units are millimeters, and that the manufactured part falls within any specified tolerances. A correct area calculation does not prove that the part can carry a particular load. Load capacity would require additional mechanical analysis or testing in later units.

Lesson summary

Engineering sketches and diagrams communicate how a proposed design looks, fits, and works. Clear labels, dimensions, units, views, and specifications help other people interpret the design correctly. A drawing should provide enough information for its intended purpose without unnecessary detail. Engineers check that dimensions are consistent and that the design meets its stated requirements.
