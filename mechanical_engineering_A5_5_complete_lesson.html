SECTION 01

How trusses carry structural loads

A bridge or roof must transfer its loads to supports without collapsing or deforming excessively. One way to do this is with a truss: an arrangement of straight members connected at joints, often forming triangles. A truss does not make the load disappear. It distributes the load through its members until the forces reach the supports and, ultimately, the ground.

In Lesson A5.4, you calculated how a beam's supports share applied loads. Now we will look inside a structure to see how forces travel through its members. The essential questions are which members are in tension or compression, how forces balance at joints, and why the geometry of a truss matters. These ideas are central to introductory truss analysis and to the bridge investigation in Lesson A5.6.

SECTION 02

The ideal truss model and its assumptions

For this lesson, an ideal truss consists of straight members connected by frictionless pins. External loads and support reactions act at the joints, and the weight of each member is neglected unless otherwise stated. Under these assumptions, a member connected at only its two ends is a two-force member. Its forces act along the member's own axis, rather than as distributed bending or twisting loads.

Real bridges and roof structures are more complicated. Their connections may resist some bending, members have weight, and loads may enter between joints. However, the ideal pin-jointed model provides a useful first step for predicting force paths. It allows engineers to reason about individual members as carrying axial tension or axial compression.

SECTION 03

Tension and compression in two-force members

A member is in tension when forces at its ends tend to pull it apart. In a joint free-body diagram, a member in tension pulls away from the joint along its own axis. A member is in compression when forces at its ends tend to squeeze it together. In a joint free-body diagram, a member in compression pushes toward the joint. These descriptions refer to the force exerted by the member on the joint, not the direction of the external load.

Imagine holding a rope taut between your hands: the rope pulls on each hand, so it is in tension. Now imagine a rigid post wedged between two surfaces: it pushes on both surfaces, so it is in compression. An ideal truss member can carry either type of axial force, depending on the loading and geometry.

Worked example 1 · Interpret member-force arrows

At a pin joint, a horizontal member to the right exerts a 75 N force pulling the joint toward the right. The force points away from the joint along the member. The member is in tension. If the same member instead pushes the joint toward the left, the member is in compression.

When solving a joint, engineers often assume an unknown member is in tension and draw its arrow away from the joint. If the calculation produces a negative value, the actual force acts opposite the assumed direction, meaning the member is in compression. This sign interpretation prevents a negative answer from being mistaken for an impossible force.

SECTION 04

Why triangular arrangements are useful

A triangle made from three rigid bars connected at their ends retains its shape unless a bar changes length or a connection fails. By contrast, a four-sided frame with only pin joints can change shape into a parallelogram without changing its member lengths. Adding a diagonal divides the four-sided frame into triangles and makes it geometrically stable under the idealized model.

That is why engineers frequently use triangulation in roof trusses, bridge frameworks, and bracing. However, a triangular shape does not guarantee that a real structure is safe. Members must still resist their calculated axial forces, compression members may buckle, and supports and connections must be designed appropriately.

SECTION 05

Use force equilibrium at a joint

A truss joint is modeled as a point where several forces meet. If the structure is stationary, the forces at each joint must balance horizontally and vertically: ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0. In a joint free-body diagram, draw every member force along its member's axis, include any applied load or support reaction, and assign a consistent positive direction.

ΣFx = 0    and    ΣFy = 0

Worked example 2 · Horizontal joint equilibrium

Two horizontal members meet at a joint. The left member pulls the joint 115 N to the left. To maintain horizontal equilibrium, the right member must pull 115 N to the right. Taking rightward as positive gives ΣFx = −115 + F = 0, so F = 115 N rightward.

Worked example 3 · An additional applied force

A joint has a 140 N member force to the left and a 35 N external force to the right. The unknown rightward member force must satisfy −140 + 35 + F = 0. Therefore F = 105 N rightward.

For a simple horizontal joint, adding rightward and leftward forces may be enough. For a triangular truss, the diagonal members contribute both horizontal and vertical components. That is the next step.

SECTION 06

Resolve diagonal member forces

When a truss member is inclined at angle θ above the horizontal, an axial force of magnitude F has a horizontal component of magnitude F cos θ and a vertical component of magnitude F sin θ. The signs of those components depend on the direction of the force arrow. If a member slopes upward and rightward away from a joint, a tensile force has positive horizontal and vertical components at that joint.

Fx = F cos θ    |    Fy = F sin θ

Worked example 4 · Component of a diagonal force

A diagonal truss member exerts a 250 N force at 30° above the horizontal. Use sin 30° = 0.5 and cos 30° ≈ 0.866. The vertical component magnitude is 250 × 0.5 = 125 N. The horizontal component magnitude is 250 × 0.866 ≈ 216.5 N. The actual signs depend on whether the member pulls or pushes and on which direction it slopes.

Worked example 5 · A 3–4–5 member

A diagonal has a horizontal run of 4 m and a vertical rise of 3 m, so its length is 5 m. Thus cos θ = 4/5 = 0.8 and sin θ = 3/5 = 0.6. For a 180 N member force, the horizontal component magnitude is 144 N and the vertical component magnitude is 108 N. You do not need to calculate the angle explicitly when the geometry gives these ratios.

SECTION 07

Analyze a symmetric triangular truss

Consider a triangular roof truss with a downward load at the top joint and two identical diagonal members descending to supports. At the top joint, the diagonal members must provide upward components to balance the load. Because compression members push on the joint along their axes, the two diagonals are in compression in this idealized arrangement. Their horizontal components oppose each other and cancel because of symmetry.

Worked example 6 · Member forces at a top joint

A symmetric triangular truss has a 300 N downward load at its top joint. Each diagonal makes a 45° angle with the horizontal. Assume the two diagonal forces have equal magnitude C and use sin 45° ≈ 0.707.

  1. Draw the downward load and two diagonal compression forces pushing upward toward the top joint.
  2. Horizontal equilibrium holds by symmetry: one horizontal component points right and the other left.
  3. Vertical equilibrium requires 2C sin 45° = 300 N.
  4. Solve: C = 300/(2 × 0.707) ≈ 212.2 N compression in each diagonal.

This example demonstrates why an axial force in a diagonal member can be larger than its vertical contribution. The members carry forces along their sloping axes, not simply straight up.

The bottom chord of a typical triangular truss may be in tension because it resists the outward horizontal components from the compressed diagonals. A complete member-force analysis must consider the support conditions and joint forces rather than assuming every horizontal member is always in tension.

SECTION 08

Trace the load path to the supports

A load path describes how an applied force is transferred through the structure to the supports. In an ideal truss, a load enters at a joint, is balanced by axial forces in the connected members, travels to neighboring joints, and eventually reaches the bearings or supports. The supports then transfer the load into the foundation or ground.

For a simple roof truss, a downward load at the top joint can produce compression in sloping members. Those members push on lower joints, where other members and the support reactions balance the forces. Tracing the path requires attention to both force direction and member connections. An arrow drawn along a member represents a transmitted force, not the physical movement of material.

Worked example 7 · Identify a valid load path

A pin-jointed roof truss carries a downward load at an upper joint. The load is transmitted through axial forces in connected members, which pass forces through other joints to the supports. It would be incorrect to claim that frictionless pins transmit bending moments between ideal truss members, or that the roof covering bypasses the structural members and delivers the load directly to the ground.

SECTION 10

Structural decisions and engineering judgment

When a truss must carry a larger load, its members generally experience larger axial forces. Geometry also matters. For the symmetric top-joint model, the required diagonal compression is C = P/(2 sin θ). A shallow truss with a small diagonal angle has a small vertical force component for a given axial force, so its diagonals must carry larger forces to support the same downward load. Steeper diagonals provide a greater vertical component per unit of axial force.

However, steeper members may increase structural height, material requirements, and clearance demands. A designer must balance force requirements against space, cost, material strength, and stability. Compression members deserve special attention because slender members can buckle before their material reaches its crushing strength. Those material considerations will be developed further in Unit A6.

For introductory truss problems, identify the joints, draw forces along member axes, distinguish tension from compression, resolve diagonals into components, check horizontal and vertical equilibrium, and describe the path from the applied load to the supports.

SECTION 11

Common mistakes and useful checks

A frequent mistake is deciding that every member under a downward load must be in compression. A truss may contain both compression and tension members. Determine the force type from the direction of the member force at the joint. Another mistake is using the full diagonal force as its vertical component. Unless the member is vertical, the vertical component is smaller than the total force and is calculated using the appropriate trigonometric ratio.

Students sometimes assume that pin-connected truss members transmit large bending moments through their joints. In the ideal model used here, straight two-force members transmit axial forces along their lengths. Finally, do not stop after balancing one component: a joint is in equilibrium only when both horizontal and vertical force sums are zero.

INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Independent practice with worked solutions

Work each problem before opening its solution. Use the ideal pin-jointed truss assumptions stated in the lesson.

A · Recognize tension

A member to the right of a joint pulls that joint 95 N to the right. Is the member in tension or compression?

Show worked solution

It pulls away from the joint along its axis, so the member is in tension.

B · Recognize compression

A horizontal member to the right of a joint pushes the joint 120 N toward the left. Identify the member force type.

Show worked solution

The member pushes toward the joint, so it is in compression.

C · Horizontal joint balance

A member pulls a joint 175 N leftward. What rightward force is required for equilibrium if these are the only horizontal forces?

Show worked solution

ΣFx = −175 + F = 0, so F = 175 N rightward.

D · Vertical component

A diagonal member exerts a 220 N axial force at 30° to the horizontal. Find the vertical component magnitude. Use sin 30° = 0.5.

Show worked solution

Fy = 220 × 0.5 = 110 N.

E · Horizontal component

A 200 N diagonal member force makes a 60° angle with the horizontal. Find its horizontal component magnitude. Use cos 60° = 0.5.

Show worked solution

Fx = 200 × 0.5 = 100 N.

F · Symmetric apex

A symmetric triangular truss carries a 400 N downward apex load. Each diagonal is at 30° to the horizontal. Find the compression force in each diagonal.

Show worked solution

2C sin 30° = 400; 2C(0.5) = 400; C = 400 N compression per diagonal.

G · Triangular stability

Why is a pin-jointed rectangle less geometrically stable than the same frame with a diagonal brace?

Show worked solution

The unbraced rectangle can change shape into a parallelogram without changing member lengths. The diagonal divides it into triangles and restricts that change in shape.

H · Load path

A roof load acts downward at a top truss joint. Explain how the ideal truss delivers the load to the ground.

Show worked solution

Axial member forces carry the load from the top joint through connected members and joints to the supports. Support reactions transfer the load into the supporting structure and ground.

VOCABULARY & GLOSSARY

Vocabulary and key concepts

Truss

A framework of straight members joined at nodes, commonly arranged in triangles.

Two-force member

A member acted on only at its two ends, carrying equal and opposite axial forces under ideal equilibrium assumptions.

Tension

Axial pulling force that tends to stretch a member.

Compression

Axial pushing force that tends to shorten a member.

Joint

A connection where truss members meet and transmit forces.

Load path

The route through which forces are transmitted from an applied load to the supports.

Force components

Horizontal and vertical parts of a force, found using geometry and trigonometry.

Triangulation

Using triangles to make a pin-jointed framework geometrically stable.

Step-by-step problem-solving guide: Truss member forces and load paths

These are new examples practicing the same skills found on the Unit 5 assessment and Semester A final. Work through the numbered steps in order. For a diagram question, read the printed labels; never measure the drawing with a ruler unless instructed.

Identify tension and compression

Practice-style example: At a joint, a straight horizontal member pulls away from the joint to the right.

  1. Step 1. Draw the joint as a dot and the member as a straight line.
  2. Step 2. Mark the force arrow pointing away from the joint.
  3. Step 3. Recall: tension pulls a joint away along the member; compression pushes toward it.
  4. Step 4. Since the arrow points away, label the member in tension.
  5. Step 5. Check the arrow refers to the member force on the joint, not the reaction at a support.
  6. Step 6. State the classification without inventing a numerical force.
Final answer: Tension

Balance a horizontal truss joint

Practice-style example: A left member pulls a joint 175 N left. Find the horizontal force required from the right member.

  1. Step 1. Draw 175 N left and an unknown rightward force.
  2. Step 2. Unknown: balancing horizontal member force F.
  3. Step 3. Use ΣFₓ = 0; choose right positive.
  4. Step 4. Write F − 175 = 0.
  5. Step 5. Solve F = 175 N right.
  6. Step 6. Check: +175 − 175 = 0; the joint is balanced horizontally.
Final answer: 175 N right

Resolve a diagonal force

Practice-style example: A diagonal member carries 250 N. Its vertical direction ratio is sin θ = 0.60.

  1. Step 1. Read member force 250 N and sin θ = 0.60.
  2. Step 2. Unknown: magnitude of vertical component Fᵧ.
  3. Step 3. Use Fᵧ = F sin θ when θ is measured from horizontal.
  4. Step 4. Substitute Fᵧ = 250 × 0.60.
  5. Step 5. Calculate Fᵧ = 150 N.
  6. Step 6. Check vertical component cannot exceed the total 250 N. Its up/down direction depends on the member's arrow.
Final answer: 150 N vertical component
Try it yourself — reveal the full solution

Question: A 180 N diagonal member force has sin θ = 0.75. Find vertical component magnitude.

Solution: Fᵧ = 180 × 0.75 = 135 N.

Lesson summary

An ideal truss is a pin-jointed framework whose straight two-force members carry axial tension or compression. Tension members pull away from joints; compression members push toward them. A stable truss commonly uses triangles because a pin-jointed rectangle can change shape without a diagonal brace. At each joint, horizontal and vertical force components must balance, so engineers use ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0. For a diagonal member at angle θ to the horizontal, force components have magnitudes F cos θ and F sin θ. A load path traces how forces travel through connected members and joints to the supports. These concepts prepare you to evaluate structural layouts in the Unit A5 bridge and beam investigation.
