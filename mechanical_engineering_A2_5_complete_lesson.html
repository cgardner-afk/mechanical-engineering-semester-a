Essential question: How can engineers test a design fairly and know whether a change actually improves performance?

SECTION 01

From Design Idea to Testable Prototype

A design sketch describes an intended solution, but engineers still need evidence that the solution behaves as expected. A prototype is an early model or version built to investigate a design. It might be a cardboard mock-up used to check fit, a computer simulation used to predict motion, or a functional device that can be measured under realistic conditions. The purpose is to learn before committing to full production.

Consider a school equipment cart that must roll easily across a workshop floor. The team has developed two wheel configurations: Design A uses smaller wheels, while Design B uses larger wheels. The team predicts that B will require less force to start moving, but a prediction is not proof. To evaluate the designs, students will measure starting push force in newtons (N) using a force gauge. Smaller force is preferred, provided every mandatory requirement is met.

Prototype testing connects to the earlier lessons: A2.1 introduced iterative design; A2.2 distinguished mandatory constraints from desirable criteria; A2.3 compared feasible alternatives; and A2.4 explained drawings and specifications. Here the focus shifts to controlled tests and reliable evidence that can support or challenge a design choice.

SECTION 02

What Makes an Engineering Test Fair?

A test is useful only when it answers a clear question. For the cart, the question is: Does wheel configuration change the starting force needed to move the same loaded cart on the same floor? The engineer must identify what will change, what will be measured, and what must stay constant.

The independent variable is the factor deliberately changed by the investigator. In our example, it is wheel configuration: A or B. The dependent variable is the measured response that may depend on that change. Here it is starting push force (N). Controlled variables are conditions kept the same so the comparison is meaningful: cart load, floor surface, measurement method, direction of push, and the procedure for starting from rest.

If Design A is tested carrying 10 kg but Design B is tested carrying 20 kg, wheel configuration is not the only thing that changed. A difference in starting force could be caused partly by load rather than wheel design. This additional changing influence is called a confounding variable. A fair test does not guarantee that all uncertainty disappears, but it reduces competing explanations for the measured difference.

SECTION 03

Plan the Experiment Before Collecting Data

A test plan documents the question, procedure, equipment, controlled conditions, and decision rules before data are collected. This reduces the temptation to change the procedure after seeing results. For the cart experiment, the team specifies that both designs will carry a 15 kg load on the same level floor. A force gauge will record the peak horizontal force required to initiate movement. The team will start each trial from rest, use the same push direction and measurement procedure, and perform three trials per design.

A trial is one repetition of a measurement procedure. Engineers repeat trials because real measurements vary. Wheel orientation, small surface irregularities, and the timing of the reading can cause slightly different force values. Repetition helps reveal whether a result is consistent or merely an unusual single reading. Three trials are enough for an introductory exercise, although professional testing may require many more trials and a statistically justified sample size.

For safety, engineers choose test loads and equipment appropriate to the prototype and use safeguards for moving carts. Students should not attempt physical load testing with unverified equipment. The interactive experiment later in this lesson is a simplified simulated dataset, not a substitute for a certified physical test.

SECTION 04

How to Calculate a Mean from Repeated Trials

When a measurement is repeated, the arithmetic mean gives a simple summary of the results. To calculate it, add all readings and divide by the number of readings. This does not erase variation or prove that the design meets every safety requirement; it provides one useful way to compare repeated measurements.

Mean = (sum of trial measurements) ÷ (number of trials)

Step 1 — List the measurements with units

Suppose Design A requires 34 N, 36 N, and 35 N in three trials. Design B requires 25 N, 27 N, and 26 N. Both designs were tested under the same stated conditions. Record each value, including N for newtons.

Step 2 — Add the readings for each design

For A, 34 + 36 + 35 = 105 N. For B, 25 + 27 + 26 = 78 N. Do not mix A and B measurements in one average because they represent different configurations.

Step 3 — Divide each sum by its number of trials

Each design has three trials. Design A's mean is 105 ÷ 3 = 35 N. Design B's mean is 78 ÷ 3 = 26 N. These are two mean starting forces, each based on three readings.

SECTION 05

Comparing Results and Measuring Improvement

After calculating means, engineers compare them to the test question. Because a lower starting force is desirable, Design B performs better in this simplified experiment: its mean is 26 N rather than 35 N. The absolute reduction is found by subtraction: 35 − 26 = 9 N. The result means the measured average starting force is 9 N lower for B under the tested conditions.

Engineers sometimes express the change as a percentage relative to the original design. This makes the difference easier to compare when projects use different starting values. It is important to name the reference value: here Design A is the original baseline.

Step 1 — Identify the original and new mean

The original mean is 35 N for A, and the new mean is 26 N for B.

Step 2 — Find the reduction

Subtract new from original: 35 − 26 = 9 N.

Step 3 — Divide by the original and multiply by 100%

Percent reduction = ((original − new) ÷ original) × 100%

Substitute: (9 ÷ 35) × 100% ≈ 25.7%. This describes the change in the measured mean, not a universal guarantee that B will always require 25.7% less force on every surface or load.

SECTION 06

Variation, Outliers, and Honest Conclusions

A mean is useful, but engineers must also look at the individual readings. Suppose three readings are 25 N, 26 N, and 50 N. Their mean is approximately 33.7 N, yet two trials cluster near 25 N and one is much higher. The high reading might reflect a real intermittent problem, a different starting condition, or a measurement error. The engineer should investigate rather than silently delete it.

A simple measure of spread is the range, calculated as the largest value minus the smallest value. For A's original readings (34, 36, 35 N), the range is 36 − 34 = 2 N. For B (25, 27, 26 N), it is 27 − 25 = 2 N. Both sets have the same range in this example. The range is not a complete statistical analysis, but it helps students notice variation that an average alone can hide.

Engineers distinguish repeatability from accuracy. Repeatable measurements cluster closely when the procedure is repeated. Accurate measurements are close to the true value. A poorly calibrated gauge could produce very consistent but systematically incorrect readings. Reliable conclusions depend on a sensible procedure, appropriate instruments, honest records, and enough evidence to address the design question.

SECTION 08

Why One-Variable-at-a-Time Testing Matters

When comparing two prototypes, a good starting strategy is to change one main factor at a time. If a team simultaneously changes wheel diameter, cart load, floor surface, and the way force is applied, it cannot easily tell which change produced the observed result. A controlled comparison holds other relevant conditions as consistent as practical while varying the factor of interest.

Consider three flawed comparisons. First, Design A is tested with a 10 kg load while B carries 20 kg; the heavier load could influence the force measurement. Second, one wheel design is tested on smooth tile and the other on rough concrete; surface texture is a competing explanation. Third, a fan with new blades runs at 1,200 revolutions per minute while the old fan runs at 800; airflow differences could be due to blade design, speed, or both. In each case, the investigator should match conditions or design a more advanced experiment that separately accounts for those factors.

One-variable-at-a-time testing is an introductory strategy, not the only legitimate experimental method. Engineers sometimes use factorial experiments to study multiple factors and their interactions systematically. What matters is that the experimental design allows valid interpretation rather than mixing uncontrolled changes together.

SECTION 09

Turning Data into a Defensible Recommendation

Engineering reports should distinguish observations from interpretations. An observation might state: “Across three trials, Design A's mean starting force was 35 N and Design B's was 26 N under the same 15 kg load and floor conditions.” An interpretation might state: “The lower measured mean suggests Design B is easier to start moving in this test.” A recommendation might be: “Advance B to further testing, provided it satisfies load capacity, cost, stability, and other mandatory requirements.”

The recommendation must acknowledge limitations. Three trials provide only a small dataset; testing one floor and one load does not establish performance in every environment. The engineer should record instrument details, date, conditions, raw readings, calculations, unusual events, and any deviations from the planned procedure. If a trial appears invalid, explain why before excluding it. This evidence supports the redesign and documentation work in Lesson A2.6.

SECTION 10

Glossary and Key Skills

Prototype: Early model or version used to investigate a design. Independent variable: Factor deliberately changed. Dependent variable: Response measured. Controlled variable: Condition intentionally kept consistent. Confounding variable: Additional changing influence that makes it difficult to attribute an observed effect to the intended factor. Trial: One repetition of a procedure. Mean: Sum of readings divided by their count. Range: Largest reading minus smallest. Repeatability: Closeness of repeated readings. Accuracy: Closeness to the true value. Baseline: Original condition used for comparison.

For the unit assessment and later engineering work, you should be able to identify variables in a scenario, spot unfair comparisons, explain why repeated trials matter, compute a mean and range, calculate the difference between designs, and explain what the evidence supports without claiming more than the data show.
