SECTION 01

Why Engineers Need Units

Mechanical engineers do not work with numbers alone. A measurement must identify what was measured, its numerical value, and its unit. The statement “the plate is 120” is incomplete: 120 millimeters describes a small component, while 120 meters describes something much larger. A unit makes the number meaningful and allows another person to interpret or reproduce a measurement.

Imagine an engineer preparing a drawing for a small equipment-cart bracket. The bracket is 120 mm long, has a mass of 0.45 kg, and is tested for 8 s. Length, mass, and time are different physical quantities. Their values cannot be compared or substituted simply because they are numbers. Engineering calculations require both correct arithmetic and correct units.

A physical quantity is a measurable property, such as length, mass, time, temperature, force, or speed. A unit is the agreed reference used to express that quantity. A measurement like 120 mm contains a number (120), a unit symbol (mm), and an implied quantity (length). Units are part of the engineering information, not decoration.

SECTION 02

The International System of Units (SI)

The International System of Units, abbreviated SI from its French name, provides a shared measurement language for science and engineering. It defines seven base units. Mechanical engineering most often begins with the meter (m) for length, the kilogram (kg) for mass, and the second (s) for time. The other SI base units are the kelvin (K) for thermodynamic temperature, ampere (A) for electric current, mole (mol) for amount of substance, and candela (cd) for luminous intensity.

These units are called base units because many other units can be expressed using combinations of them. The SI unit of mass is the kilogram, not the gram; the prefix kilo- is part of the SI base unit's name. Engineers may still use grams when appropriate. Temperature readings in everyday settings often use degrees Celsius (°C), which is accepted for use with SI, while kelvin is the SI base unit for thermodynamic temperature.

SECTION 03

Base Quantities and Derived Quantities

Some engineering quantities combine base quantities. These are called derived quantities. Area measures surface extent and uses square meters (m²). Volume measures three-dimensional space and uses cubic meters (m³). Speed compares distance with time and uses meters per second (m/s). A speed of 2 m/s means an object travels 2 meters each second if the speed remains constant.

Force is another derived quantity. Its SI unit is the newton (N). One newton is equivalent to one kilogram-meter per second squared (kg·m/s²). You will study why in Unit A4. For now, recognize that force is measured in newtons, not kilograms: kilograms measure mass. Energy is measured in joules (J), and power in watts (W). These named derived units will appear in later units.

SECTION 04

Metric Prefixes: Making Units Fit the Object

Engineers work at many scales, from tiny fasteners to large machines. SI prefixes make measurements easier to read by expressing multiples or fractions of a unit. The prefix is attached directly to the unit symbol: mm means millimeter, km means kilometer, and mg means milligram. A prefix changes the scale of the unit, not the physical quantity being measured.

For this course, learn five common prefixes. kilo- (k) means 1,000 times the unit. centi- (c) means one hundredth. milli- (m) means one thousandth. micro- (µ) means one millionth. mega- (M) means one million times the unit. The capital letter matters: m as a prefix means milli-, while M means mega-. Prefix symbols and unit symbols are case-sensitive.

A useful visual pattern is that each prefix represents a specific power of ten. The symbols are standardized, so 1 mm is always one thousandth of a meter. Do not confuse mm (millimeters) with m² (square meters): the first is length, while the second is area.

SECTION 05

Reading and Writing Measurements Correctly

Engineering notation must be clear. Write a space between a numerical value and most unit symbols: 120 mm, 0.45 kg, 8 s, and 25 °C. Unit symbols do not take a plural s: write 5 kg, not 5 kgs. The symbols are not ordinary abbreviations and generally do not take periods. Keep capitalization exact: N means newton, while n is the prefix nano-.

Measurements also need enough context. “Width: 120 mm” is clearer than “120” on a drawing. For a circular hole, “diameter: 12 mm” tells the machinist which dimension is intended. The quantity, value, and unit together support communication across design, manufacturing, and testing.

Mass is not weight. Mass describes how much matter an object has and is measured in kilograms. Weight is a gravitational force and is measured in newtons. In everyday speech people often say an object “weighs 5 kilograms,” but in engineering work a 5 kg value describes mass. This distinction will become essential when studying forces.

SECTION 06

Choosing an Appropriate Unit

Using a suitable unit makes a measurement readable. A tiny hole may be described in millimeters; the length of a classroom may be described in meters; the distance between cities may be described in kilometers. For mass, a small fastener may be expressed in grams, while a cart may be expressed in kilograms. For duration, short engineering tests often use seconds.

Consider a 120 mm bracket. Writing its length as 0.12 m is also correct, but 120 mm is convenient for a fabrication drawing. A 2.4 m beam is easier to read in meters than as 2,400 mm in a general description, although a technical drawing may still use millimeters for consistency. Unit choice depends on context, the drawing standard, and the precision required.

Be careful not to confuse the quantity with the unit. A student might say “meters” when asked what quantity a tape measure records, but the quantity is length and the unit might be meters, centimeters, or millimeters. Similarly, a stopwatch measures the quantity time using seconds as a common unit.

SECTION 07

Calculating with Prefixes: Three Connected Steps

This lesson introduces how a prefix relates to a base unit. Lesson A3.2 will develop full dimensional analysis and multi-step unit conversions. For now, follow a consistent three-step method when interpreting a prefixed measurement.

Step 1 — Identify the quantity, prefix, and base unit

Suppose a bracket length is 250 mm. The quantity is length, the prefix milli- means one thousandth, and the underlying unit is the meter.

Step 2 — Write the prefix relationship

Because 1 mm = 0.001 m, each millimeter is one thousandth of a meter. This is the relationship to apply.

Step 3 — Apply the relationship and keep the unit

250 mm × 0.001 m/mm = 0.250 m

The mm units cancel in the expression, leaving meters. The same bracket can be described as 250 mm or 0.250 m. Both values describe the same physical length. The three steps belong to one calculation: identify → write relationship → apply and label.

For a second example, a 3.5 km route uses kilo-, which means 1,000. Therefore 3.5 km × 1,000 m/km = 3,500 m. A prefix never changes length into mass or time; it changes only the size of the unit.

SECTION 09

Measurement Instruments and Limits

Choosing the right tool matters as much as choosing the right unit. A ruler can measure the length of a bracket, a tape measure can measure a larger structure, a balance measures mass, and a stopwatch measures elapsed time. For smaller dimensions, engineers may use calipers or micrometers. Instruments have different scales, ranges, and levels of resolution.

Resolution is the smallest increment an instrument can display or distinguish. A ruler marked every millimeter generally supports finer readings than one marked only every centimeter. However, more decimal places do not automatically make a measurement more accurate. Instrument condition, technique, and calibration also matter. The detailed study of precision, error, and uncertainty comes in Lesson A3.5.

For example, a drawing might specify a plate length of 120 mm. A student reading a ruler could report 120 mm, while a digital caliper might display 120.03 mm. The extra digits may be useful, but only if the instrument and procedure support them. Engineers should avoid claiming greater precision than the measurement process justifies.

SECTION 10

Common Mistakes and Engineering Checks

Before accepting a measurement, ask three questions. What physical quantity is being measured? Which unit expresses it? Does the scale make sense? A cart mass of 150 mm is meaningless because millimeters measure length. A small bolt 50 km long is implausible. A starting push force of 30 kg mixes a force quantity with a mass unit; force should be expressed in newtons.

Watch for similar-looking symbols. m by itself means meter; when placed before another unit symbol, m can be the prefix milli-, as in mm or mg. M means mega- as a prefix. kg means kilogram, while km means kilometer. These are not interchangeable. Always read the entire symbol in context.

Unit checking is a basic form of engineering quality control. It can catch a mistake before it reaches a drawing, a manufacturing order, or a safety calculation. Later lessons will show how units can also help check whether an equation is reasonable.
