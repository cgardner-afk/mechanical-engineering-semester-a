Section 01

A factory robot needs a reliable position

Start here: What is mechanical engineering?

Mechanical engineering is the work of designing, building, testing, and improving machines and moving systems. An engineer does more than calculate an answer: the engineer uses measurements to decide whether a machine will work reliably and safely. You do not need previous engineering experience for this lesson.

Picture the machine: Imagine a small motorized cart rolling left and right on a metal track inside a factory. The cart picks up a part, moves it to another station, and sometimes returns. In this lesson, we call that cart a robot carriage. A guide rail is the track that keeps it moving in a straight line. A controller is the computer that tells the motor when and where to move. A loading station is simply where the cart picks up its part.

The problem: Suppose the cart travels to a workstation and then comes partway back. How far did it travel altogether? Where did it end? Those questions sound similar, but they require different measurements. We will learn both, one step at a time.

In an automated manufacturing facility, a robot carriage travels along a straight guide rail between a loading station, a machining station, and an inspection station. The controller must know where the robot is, how far its wheels or drive belt have traveled, and where it ended relative to its starting point. Those are related but different quantities. A robot can travel a long distance and still return to exactly where it started.

Mechanical engineers use position measurements to plan machine travel, specify stroke lengths, avoid collisions with fixtures, estimate wear on moving components, and confirm that an automated system reaches the correct workstation. This lesson develops a one-dimensional motion model, then applies it to a factory transfer robot. The same reasoning appears in conveyor systems, linear actuators, machine-tool axes, elevators, and test rigs.

Section 02

Define the reference frame before calculating

Before the equation: How does a number line describe a machine?

Think of a number line from math class, placed directly underneath the track. Choose one spot as zero. That is our origin, or starting reference point. The cart may travel to the right or to the left of zero. We agree to call right/east positive (+) and left/west negative (−).

For example, +3 m means the cart is three meters to the right of zero. −3 m means three meters to the left. The minus sign is not a warning or a mistake; it tells us which side of zero the cart is on. The letter x is simply a short name for position. Engineers often use x for left-and-right motion.

Check your understanding: If the cart is at −5 m, is it five meters below the track? No. It is five meters to the left of the chosen zero point.

A reference frame identifies an origin and a positive direction. In our factory, the loading station is position 0 m, and positions increase toward the east. A location 6 m east of the loading station is written +6 m. A location 3 m west is −3 m. The sign identifies location relative to the origin, not how much ground the robot has traveled.

Engineers must specify the origin and positive direction because position and displacement depend on those choices. A machine drawing might define +x toward the motor, while a site plan might define east as positive. Neither convention is automatically correct; the design team must be consistent. For this unit's problems, motion occurs along one straight line unless the problem explicitly says otherwise.

Position: x = signed location measured from a defined origin, usually in meters (m).

Section 04

Distance: the total path traveled

Let's walk it before calculating it.

Imagine leaving your front door, walking 5 meters to the right, then walking 2 meters back toward the door. Your feet covered 5 meters on the first part and another 2 meters on the second part. A step still counts as travel even when you turn around. That is why distance adds every part of the route: 5 + 2 = 7 meters.

The vertical bars in an expression such as |−2| mean absolute value. Absolute value asks how big a number is without its direction sign. So |−2| = 2. We use it because walking 2 meters to the left still adds 2 meters to the total distance.

Try it: A cart moves 4 m right and 4 m left. Its distance is 4 + 4 = 8 m. Even though it ends where it began, it did not travel zero meters.

Distance is the total length of the path a machine travels, regardless of direction. For motion along a straight line with several segments, calculate the absolute length of each segment and add them. Because each segment length is nonnegative, total distance is nonnegative.

Total distance = |segment 1 change| + |segment 2 change| + …

Worked example 1: A transfer robot moves 9 m east from its loading station, then 4 m west to reach a part inspection point. It travels 9 + 4 = 13 m in total. A wheel-rotation counter that measures cumulative travel should register approximately 13 m, assuming negligible wheel slip.

Distance matters to mechanical engineers when estimating guide-rail wear, drive-system cycles, cable-carrier movement, lubrication intervals, and maintenance schedules. It is not interchangeable with the robot's net change in position.

Step-by-step worked method · Find total distance

  1. Step 1: Identify each movement: 9 m east, then 4 m west.
  2. Step 2: Distance ignores direction; use positive lengths.
  3. Step 3: Add both legs: 9 + 4 = 13 m.
  4. Step 4: Answer: 13 m traveled, even though the robot reverses.
Section 05

Displacement: the change in position

Distance and displacement answer different questions.

Distance asks, How much ground did you cover? Displacement asks, Where did you end compared with where you began? In the walking example, you went 5 m right and 2 m left, so you ended 3 m right of your starting point. Your distance was 7 m, but your displacement was +3 m.

In the formula Δx = xfinal − xinitial, the triangle-shaped symbol Δ (pronounced “delta”) means change in. So Δx means change in position. The little labels final and initial simply mean end and start.

Follow the steps: (1) Find the starting coordinate. (2) Find the ending coordinate. (3) Subtract start from end. (4) Interpret the sign as a direction. If you start at +2 m and finish at +6 m, then Δx = 6 − 2 = +4 m: you ended four meters to the right of where you started.

Displacement is the signed difference between the final position and the initial position. It describes where a machine ended relative to where it started, not the length of the path taken.

Δx = xfinal − xinitial

Worked example 2: The robot starts at 0 m, moves 9 m east, then 4 m west. Its final position is +5 m, so its displacement is +5 m (5 m east). The distance is 13 m, but the displacement is only 5 m east.

The sign is essential. If east is positive, a displacement of −5 m means 5 m west. Displacement can be zero even when distance is not zero. For a one-dimensional path, the magnitude of displacement can never exceed the total distance traveled.

Step-by-step worked method · Find net displacement

  1. Step 1: Choose east as positive.
  2. Step 2: Write the signed movements: +9 m and −4 m.
  3. Step 3: Add signed changes: +9 − 4 = +5 m.
  4. Step 4: Answer: +5 m, or 5 m east of the start.
Section 06

Engineering drawing: follow successive moves

In a machine travel diagram, arrows indicate successive movements. The arrows show what happened in order, not simply the starting and ending coordinates. Read the arrow directions, add the path lengths for distance, and combine signed movements for displacement.

Worked example 3: A service carriage moves 14 m east and 6 m west. Total distance = 14 + 6 = 20 m. Net displacement = +14 − 6 = +8 m. Do not subtract 6 from 14 when asked for distance, and do not add 14 and 6 when asked for displacement.

Step-by-step worked method · Read a multi-arrow motion drawing

  1. Step 1: Read first arrow: 14 m east; second arrow: 6 m west.
  2. Step 2: Distance: add path lengths 14 + 6 = 20 m.
  3. Step 3: Displacement: add signed changes +14 − 6 = +8 m.
  4. Step 4: State units and direction: distance 20 m; displacement 8 m east.
Section 07

Return trips and zero displacement

Consider an automated inspection robot that moves 11 m east to check a sensor, then travels 11 m west back to its charging station. Its distance is 22 m, but its displacement is 0 m. This is not a contradiction: the machine completed a trip but returned to its initial position.

Worked example 4: A linear actuator begins at +2 m, extends to +8 m, and retracts to +2 m. The outbound travel is 6 m and the return travel is 6 m. Total distance is 12 m. Final displacement is +2 − (+2) = 0 m. Engineers might use total travel to estimate service cycles, but use displacement to verify that the actuator returned to its home position.

A zero displacement does not imply that the robot never moved. It means only that the final and initial positions match.

Step-by-step worked method · Calculate a return trip

  1. Step 1: Read positions: start +2 m, reach +8 m, return to +2 m.
  2. Step 2: Outbound distance = |8 − 2| = 6 m; return distance = |2 − 8| = 6 m.
  3. Step 3: Total distance = 6 + 6 = 12 m.
  4. Step 4: Displacement = final − initial = 2 − 2 = 0 m.
Section 08

Signed coordinates: subtract final and initial

Why does subtracting a negative add?

If the cart starts at −4 m and ends at +3 m, first picture the number line. It travels 4 m to get from −4 to zero, then 3 m more to reach +3. That is 7 m to the right. The equation says the same thing: 3 − (−4) = 3 + 4 = +7 m. The double negative is not an engineering trick; it matches the actual distance between the two coordinates.

Guided question: A cart starts at −2 m and ends at +5 m. Count 2 m to reach zero and 5 m more to reach +5. Its displacement is +7 m.

Not every machine starts at the origin. A survey team might measure a carriage's location relative to a building reference line. In that case, use the initial and final coordinates directly.

Worked example 5: East is positive. A maintenance cart moves from xi = +12 m to xf = +5 m. Its displacement is 5 − 12 = −7 m, or 7 m west. The cart ends at a positive coordinate, but it moved west to get there.

Worked example 6: A robot moves from −4 m to +3 m. Its displacement is 3 − (−4) = +7 m. The double negative matters: crossing the origin from a negative coordinate to a positive coordinate produces a positive displacement.

For a single direct move without backtracking, distance is the absolute value of the displacement. For a multi-segment route with reversals, add the absolute changes for each segment to determine total distance.

Step-by-step worked method · Subtract signed coordinates

  1. Step 1: Use Δx = x_final − x_initial.
  2. Step 2: Example A: from +12 m to +5 m gives 5 − 12 = −7 m (west).
  3. Step 3: Example B: from −4 m to +3 m gives 3 − (−4) = +7 m (east).
  4. Step 4: Check direction from start to finish, not from the sign of the final coordinate.
Section 09

Design decisions: stroke, wear, and safe travel

Engineering words explained:

An actuator is a device that makes a machine move; a motor-driven sliding rod is one example. Its stroke is how far the moving part can extend or retract. A travel envelope is the complete range of places the moving part reaches, not just its final destination. A guard is a physical barrier that keeps people away from moving machinery.

Imagine a cart that begins at 0 m, reaches +8 m, and returns to +3 m. Its displacement is only +3 m, but a rail that ends at +3 m would prevent the cart from ever reaching the +8 m workstation. An engineer must check every position along the trip before selecting the rail length.

Mechanical engineers use motion quantities for different decisions. The required actuator stroke is related to the separation between the needed end positions. The travel envelope identifies the range of positions that moving parts may occupy, so engineers can place guards and avoid interference. The cumulative distance traveled helps estimate wear and maintenance demand.

Imagine a factory robot whose route is 0 m → +8 m → +3 m. Its total distance is 13 m, its displacement is +3 m, and its furthest position from the origin is +8 m. A design that provides only 3 m of rail travel would not work, even though the final displacement is 3 m. The machine must physically reach +8 m during its route. This is why a mechanical engineer must consider the complete path, not merely the final coordinate.

Engineering warning: A displacement calculation alone cannot establish clearance or safe travel. Designers also check intermediate positions, physical dimensions, stopping distances, and guarding.

Section 11

Engineering analysis of the motion trials

The simulator reports four quantities with different engineering uses. Final position is the robot's location relative to the factory origin. Total distance is the sum of the absolute movement lengths. Displacement is final position minus initial position. Travel envelope is the minimum and maximum position reached along the route; it helps identify the span the guide rail and guarding must accommodate.

Investigation example: Compare a route starting at 0 m with moves +6 m and −6 m to a route starting at +4 m with the same moves. Both routes have distance 12 m and displacement 0 m. However, their absolute position ranges differ: 0 to +6 m versus +4 to +10 m. Moving the origin-relative starting point does not change the route's net displacement, but it changes where the robot travels in the factory.

Limitation: This simulation assumes straight-line movement and exact commanded distances. A real machine may experience encoder errors, backlash, wheel slip, mechanical limits, and measurement uncertainty. Engineers validate actual motion with sensors and commissioning tests.

Section 12

Independent engineering practice

Solve each problem before opening its explanation. Include units and direction when appropriate.

1. A robot moves 7 m east, then 5 m west. Find total distance.

7 + 5 = 12 m.

2. For the same route, find displacement.

7 − 5 = +2 m (east).

3. A robot moves 15 m east, then 15 m west. Find distance and displacement.

Distance = 30 m; displacement = 0 m.

4. East is positive. A cart moves from +14 m to +6 m. Find displacement.

6 − 14 = −8 m (8 m west).

5. A carriage moves from −5 m to +4 m in one direct move. Find displacement and distance.

Displacement = 4 − (−5) = +9 m; direct-path distance = 9 m.

6. A robot follows 0 → +10 → +3 m. Find total distance, displacement, and furthest position.

Distance = 10 + 7 = 17 m; displacement = +3 m; furthest position = +10 m.

7. A robot follows +2 → −4 → +1 m. Find total distance.

First leg = 6 m, second = 5 m; total = 11 m.

8. For problem 7, find displacement.

Final minus initial = 1 − 2 = −1 m.

9. Can a robot's total distance be smaller than the magnitude of its displacement?

No. Total path length is at least as large as the straight-line net change.

10. Which quantity best describes the travel needed to check rail clearance: displacement alone or the complete position range?

The complete position range, because the robot may travel beyond its final position.

11. A robot starts at +3 m and moves +8 m then −2 m. Find its final position.

3 + 8 − 2 = +9 m.

12. If a robot's route is shifted 5 m east without changing its movements, what happens to displacement?

It stays the same; the initial and final coordinates both shift by 5 m.

Section 13

Vocabulary glossary

Expand each term to review its engineering meaning.

Position

Signed location of an object relative to a chosen origin and positive direction.

Reference frame

A system for describing an object's position and motion using a chosen origin and positive direction.

Origin

Reference position assigned coordinate zero.

Positive direction

Chosen direction represented by increasing coordinates.

Distance traveled

Total length of the route, independent of direction.

Displacement

The change in an object's position from start to finish, including direction. Calculate it as final position minus initial position: Δx = xfinal − xinitial.

Initial position

Coordinate at the beginning of the motion interval.

Final position

Coordinate at the end of the motion interval.

Motion segment

One successive portion of a route between two positions.

Linear actuator

A mechanical device that produces motion along a straight line.

Stroke

Distance an actuator travels between specified end positions.

Travel envelope

The complete range of positions a machine occupies during its movement.

Encoder

Sensor used to measure shaft rotation or position for motion control.

Cumulative travel

Accumulated distance moved over one or more motion segments.

Section 15

Lesson summary and engineering connection

Position is a signed coordinate relative to a chosen origin. Distance is the total path length; displacement is the signed difference between final and initial position. For one-dimensional motion, calculate total distance by adding the magnitudes of successive moves, and calculate displacement using Δx = xfinal − xinitial. A machine can return to its starting point with zero displacement after traveling a substantial distance.

Mechanical engineers use these quantities differently: displacement helps verify a move, cumulative distance informs wear and maintenance, and the complete travel envelope informs machine layout and guarding. In A7.2, you will add time to these motion measurements to distinguish average speed from average velocity in real mechanical systems.
