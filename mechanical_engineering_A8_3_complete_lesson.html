Section 01

Why lifting a load stores energy

Imagine an overhead crane lifting a steel component from a warehouse floor to a storage platform. The crane does work against gravity. Once the component is elevated, it has energy associated with its position, even if it is no longer moving. That stored energy is called gravitational potential energy (GPE).

Potential energy means energy associated with a condition or arrangement. Gravitational potential energy is associated with height in a gravitational field. A load on a high shelf can fall and transfer that stored energy into motion, sound, deformation, or heat. Engineers calculate GPE when designing hoists, elevators, ramps, cranes, and storage systems.

Words to know before we begin

A load is an object a machine carries or lifts. A hoist raises or lowers loads. A reference height is the level from which height is measured. Gravity is the attraction that pulls objects toward Earth. In this lesson, we will use a gravitational field strength of 9.8 newtons per kilogram (equivalently, an acceleration of 9.8 m/s²) near Earth's surface.

Section 02

Mass, weight, height, and gravity

Before using an energy equation, distinguish mass from weight. Mass describes how much matter is in an object. We measure mass in kilograms (kg), and moving an object to a higher shelf does not change its mass. Weight is the downward force gravity exerts on that mass. Weight is measured in newtons (N).

Near Earth's surface, weight is calculated with weight = mass × gravitational field strength, or Fg = mg. For a 20 kg box, its weight is 20 × 9.8 = 196 N. A crane lifting that box steadily must apply an upward force balancing its weight, ignoring other forces. The box's weight and its mass are not interchangeable numbers or units.

Height is the vertical change in position relative to the chosen reference level, measured in meters (m). If a platform is 3 m above the floor and we choose the floor as zero height, the load's height is 3 m. A ramp can be much longer than 3 m while reaching the same vertical height. For gravitational potential energy, the vertical height change matters, not the distance along the ramp.

Quick unit check

Mass: 20 kg. Weight: 196 N. Height above floor: 3 m. Energy: measured in J. Each unit describes a different physical quantity.

Section 03

The gravitational potential energy equation

For loads near Earth's surface, where gravity is approximately constant, calculate gravitational potential energy relative to a reference level using the following equation.

Gravitational potential energy = mass × gravity × height   →   GPE = mgh

The symbol m represents mass in kilograms, g is gravitational field strength (9.8 N/kg near Earth), and h is vertical height in meters. GPE is measured in joules. The calculation gives the energy relative to the chosen zero-height reference.

The units help explain the result: kg × N/kg × m = N·m, and one newton-meter equals one joule. If you already know an object's weight in newtons, you can calculate the change in gravitational potential energy as weight × vertical height change. This works because weight = mg.

Worked example 1 — Lift a machine part

A crane raises a 75 kg component by 4 m. Write the known values: m = 75 kg, g = 9.8 N/kg, and h = 4 m. Substitute: GPE = 75 × 9.8 × 4. Multiply 75 × 9.8 = 735 N; then multiply by 4 m. The increase in gravitational potential energy is 2,940 J.

Interpretation: The component has gained 2,940 J of gravitational potential energy relative to its original height. In an ideal, steady lift, the lifting force transfers this much energy to the load. A real motor may require additional input energy because of losses.

Section 04

A diagram of height and reference level

Look at the two paths in this diagram. A load can move straight up in a hoist or travel up a longer ramp. If it begins and ends at the same heights, the change in gravitational potential energy is the same for both paths. The path length is not the height used in GPE = mgh.

Suppose the load has mass 50 kg and both paths raise it by 3 m. The increase in GPE is 50 × 9.8 × 3 = 1,470 J for either route. A ramp can reduce the force required at a given moment by spreading the lifting work over a longer distance. It does not eliminate the energy needed to gain height. Friction on a real ramp may increase the total input energy required.

Section 05

Worked examples: compare masses and heights

Since GPE = mgh, potential energy is proportional to both mass and height when gravity is constant. Doubling mass while keeping height fixed doubles GPE. Doubling height while keeping mass fixed also doubles GPE. If both mass and height double, GPE increases by a factor of four.

Worked example 2 — A heavier load

Load A has mass 40 kg and is lifted 5 m. GPE = 40 × 9.8 × 5 = 1,960 J. Load B has mass 80 kg and is lifted to the same height. GPE = 80 × 9.8 × 5 = 3,920 J. Doubling mass doubles the stored energy.

Worked example 3 — A higher shelf

A 120 kg pallet is raised from floor level to a 2 m shelf. The gain is 120 × 9.8 × 2 = 2,352 J. Raising the same pallet to 6 m gives 120 × 9.8 × 6 = 7,056 J. Tripling height triples GPE.

When a load is lowered, its change in height is negative and its gravitational potential energy decreases. For example, lowering a 50 kg box by 2 m changes GPE by 50 × 9.8 × (−2) = −980 J. The negative sign describes a decrease in stored energy, not a negative mass or a negative amount of energy transferred.

Section 06

Rearrange the equation to find height

Engineers sometimes know how much gravitational potential energy a load gained and need to find how high it was lifted. We can rearrange GPE = mgh to solve for height. The key algebra idea is to undo multiplication by dividing both sides of an equation by the same nonzero quantity.

Algebra steps

Start: GPE = m × g × h.

Divide both sides by m × g: GPE ÷ (m × g) = (m × g × h) ÷ (m × g).

Simplify: h = GPE / (mg).

Check units: J ÷ N = (N·m) ÷ N = m, so the answer is a height in meters.

Worked example 4 — Find the lifting height

A 60 kg machine part gains 2,940 J of gravitational potential energy. How far upward did it move? Known values: GPE = 2,940 J, m = 60 kg, g = 9.8 N/kg. First calculate mg = 60 × 9.8 = 588 N. Then divide: h = 2,940 ÷ 588 = 5 m.

Verification: 60 × 9.8 × 5 = 2,940 J, so the result matches the original energy.

Write the denominator with parentheses when using a calculator: 2940 ÷ (60 × 9.8). Without parentheses, some calculators evaluate the operations in an unintended order.

Section 07

A ramp changes the force, not the height gained

A ramp is an inclined plane, a sloped surface used to move loads between elevations. In an ideal ramp with no friction, a longer, gentler ramp requires less force along the slope than a steep ramp, but the load travels farther. The total work against gravity remains equal to the gain in gravitational potential energy, mgh, for both routes.

Worked example 5 — Two ramp choices

A 90 kg crate must reach a loading dock 2 m above the ground. Route A is a short, steep ramp. Route B is a long, shallow ramp. The crate's increase in GPE on either route is 90 × 9.8 × 2 = 1,764 J. The two routes can require different pushing forces and different travel distances, but they produce the same gain in gravitational potential energy.

In the real world, rolling resistance and friction may cause extra energy losses. Therefore, the actual input work can differ between routes. The gain in gravitational potential energy still depends on the same mass, gravity, and vertical height difference. This distinction is important when interpreting exam questions about ramps and lifting.

Section 09

Common errors and a problem-solving guide

One frequent error is substituting weight in newtons for mass in kilograms in mgh. If the given value is already a weight, use weight × height rather than multiplying by gravity a second time. Another error is using ramp length instead of vertical height. A third is forgetting the 9.8 factor, or entering the height-rearrangement calculation without parentheses.

Choose the right method

Given mass and height? Use GPE = mgh. Given weight and height? Use GPE = weight × height. Given GPE and mass, asked for height? Use h = GPE/(mg). Given two paths to the same elevation? Their changes in GPE are the same if the load and gravitational field are the same. Given two heights? Calculate the vertical height change, not the total path distance.

In this unit, we use 9.8 N/kg unless a question supplies another gravitational field strength. Some introductory examples round gravity to 10 N/kg for estimates, but using 9.8 produces different numerical answers. Always follow the value specified in the problem.

Section 10

Independent practice with guided solutions

Work each problem on paper before expanding its solution. Show the equation, substituted values, and units. Explain your reasoning in comparison questions.

1. Find the GPE gained when a 40 kg load rises 5 m.

GPE = 40 × 9.8 × 5 = 1,960 J.

2. A 125 kg pallet rises 4 m. How much GPE does it gain?

GPE = 125 × 9.8 × 4 = 4,900 J.

3. A load weighs 490 N and rises 3 m. Calculate its gain in GPE.

GPE = weight × height = 490 × 3 = 1,470 J. Do not multiply by gravity again.

4. A 75 kg load gains 3,675 J. Find the vertical rise.

h = 3,675/(75 × 9.8) = 3,675/735 = 5 m.

5. A 50 kg load rises 2 m on a short ramp or 2 m on a long ramp. Compare the GPE changes.

Both are 50 × 9.8 × 2 = 980 J. Ramp length does not change the vertical height gain.

6. A load's mass doubles at the same height. What happens to GPE?

It doubles because GPE is proportional to mass.

7. A load's height triples with constant mass. What happens to GPE?

It triples because GPE is proportional to height.

8. A 100 kg crate moves from a 2 m shelf to a 7 m shelf. Calculate the increase in GPE.

Vertical height change = 7 − 2 = 5 m. ΔGPE = 100 × 9.8 × 5 = 4,900 J.

9. A 60 kg box is lowered by 4 m. What is its change in GPE?

ΔGPE = 60 × 9.8 × (−4) = −2,352 J. It loses 2,352 J of gravitational potential energy.

10. A 200 kg load rises 1.5 m. Calculate the GPE gain.

GPE = 200 × 9.8 × 1.5 = 2,940 J.

11. What is the difference between mass and weight?

Mass is measured in kilograms and describes the amount of matter. Weight is the gravitational force on that mass, measured in newtons; weight = mg.

12. Why might a long ramp require less pushing force but not less GPE gain?

The load reaches the same vertical height, so its GPE gain is mgh. An ideal longer ramp spreads the work over a greater distance, reducing force along the slope. Real friction can add input-energy losses.

Section 11

Glossary: gravitational-energy vocabulary

Click each term to reveal its definition. The vocabulary headings use the same aqua color and expandable design as the earlier lessons.

Gravitational potential energy (GPE)

Energy associated with an object's height in a gravitational field. Near Earth's surface, GPE = mgh relative to a chosen zero height.

Potential energy

Stored energy associated with an object's position or arrangement.

Mass

The amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms (kg).

Weight

The gravitational force on an object, measured in newtons (N). Near Earth, weight = mg.

Gravity (g)

The gravitational field strength, approximately 9.8 N/kg near Earth's surface, equivalent to 9.8 m/s² of gravitational acceleration.

Height (h)

Vertical distance above a chosen reference level, measured in meters (m).

Reference level

The level assigned zero height for calculating gravitational potential energy.

Joule (J)

The unit of energy and work. One joule equals one newton-meter.

Hoist

A machine that raises or lowers a load.

Load

The object or material being lifted or carried.

Inclined plane

A sloped surface, such as a ramp, used to move an object between heights.

Ramp length

Distance traveled along the slope, which is different from vertical rise.

Work

Energy transferred by a force acting through a displacement.

Energy loss

Energy transferred into other forms, such as thermal energy from friction, rather than the intended useful output.

Efficiency

The fraction of input energy converted into useful output energy. A later lesson will calculate it.

Section 12

Lesson summary and next steps

Gravitational potential energy is energy associated with height. Near Earth's surface, calculate it with GPE = mgh, where mass is in kilograms, gravity is approximately 9.8 N/kg, and vertical height is in meters. The answer is in joules. Mass and weight are different: mass is measured in kilograms; weight is a force measured in newtons.

Doubling mass or vertical height doubles the GPE gain when other quantities remain constant. If GPE and mass are known, solve for height with h = GPE/(mg). Different ramps or hoists that raise the same load through the same vertical height produce the same GPE increase, even when their path lengths differ.

In A8.4, you will combine gravitational potential energy with the kinetic energy from A8.2 to understand conservation of energy—how energy changes form as a machine or moving object changes height and speed.
