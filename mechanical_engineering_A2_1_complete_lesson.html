SECTION 01

Engineering Design Is a Process of Decisions

Engineering design is the process of developing a solution to a need while working within real limitations. A design might be a bicycle brake, a medical device, a folding ramp, or a factory conveyor. The engineer's task is not simply to imagine something that could work. The task is to produce a solution that satisfies stated requirements, can be manufactured or operated realistically, and is supported by evidence.

Consider a community center that needs a portable ramp for moving wheeled equipment onto a raised platform. A ramp must support its expected load, fit the available space, be manageable for staff, and stay within budget. A design that looks attractive but is too steep, too heavy, or too weak does not solve the problem. Engineers begin by understanding the need and defining what successful performance would look like.

The engineering design process provides a systematic way to make these decisions. It organizes the work, but it is not a rigid recipe. Engineers often revisit earlier decisions when measurements reveal new information. This repeated improvement is called iteration.

SECTION 02

Define the Need and Establish Requirements

A problem statement describes the need without prematurely prescribing a solution. “Build a steel ramp” already chooses a material and form. “Provide a safe, portable way to move equipment onto a raised platform” leaves room for alternatives. That difference matters because the best solution may use a different material, a hinged platform, or another lifting arrangement.

Engineers translate needs into criteria and constraints. Criteria describe desirable performance, such as low mass, ease of storage, or smooth operation. Constraints set mandatory limits, such as maximum cost, maximum dimensions, and minimum required load capacity. Whenever possible, requirements should be measurable: “support at least 150 kg under specified conditions” is more useful than “be strong.”

Engineering distinction: A solution that fails a mandatory safety requirement is not acceptable merely because it performs well on other criteria. In Lesson A2.2, you will practice evaluating designs against several simultaneous requirements.

Requirements also need context. “Support 150 kg” is incomplete if no one specifies how the load is distributed, whether it moves, or what safety conditions apply. Good engineers identify assumptions and document the conditions under which the requirement will be checked.

SECTION 03

Generate Alternatives Before Selecting One

After clarifying the problem, engineers develop several possible approaches. For the portable ramp, alternatives might include a solid aluminum panel, a folding composite panel, or a telescoping assembly. Early concepts are usually communicated with sketches, descriptions, and simple estimates rather than finished manufacturing drawings.

Generating alternatives before committing to a favorite design reduces the risk of overlooking a better approach. Engineers can compare options using the same criteria and constraints. They may estimate mass, cost, strength, storage dimensions, and ease of operation. These estimates do not prove a design will work; they help determine which concepts deserve closer investigation.

Brainstorming should initially encourage multiple plausible ideas. Evaluation comes next. A concept may be rejected because it violates a hard constraint, or it may be refined because one weakness can be corrected. A structured decision matrix, introduced in Lesson A2.3, makes the comparison more transparent.

SECTION 04

The Design Cycle Is Iterative

A simplified engineering design cycle moves from understanding the problem to generating ideas, choosing a promising concept, building a model or prototype, testing it, and revising the design. Real projects rarely follow this sequence exactly once. A test may reveal that the original requirement was incomplete, or a manufacturing problem may force the team to reconsider the geometry.

2. GeneratePossible concepts
3. SelectEvaluate alternatives
4. PrototypeBuild a testable version
5. TestCollect evidence
6. Revise ↺Improve and retest

Iteration is not a sign that engineers failed to plan. It is how engineers respond to information that could not be known confidently before testing. However, repeated changes without measurements are not productive iteration. Each revision should address a specific observation or hypothesis, and the next test should show whether that change helped.

SECTION 05

Prototypes, Models, and Tests

A prototype is a version of a design used to learn about its performance, construction, or usability. A prototype does not have to look like the final product. A cardboard model may reveal whether a folding mechanism fits in storage. A computer model may estimate deflection. A physical load test may reveal a weak connection. Each type of model answers different questions.

Testing should be planned around a specific requirement. If the requirement concerns maximum deflection under load, the engineer must define the load, where it is applied, how displacement is measured, and the acceptable limit. If the test conditions change between trials, the results may not support a fair comparison.

For example, imagine a prototype ramp that must deflect no more than 8 mm under a specified test load. The first prototype deflects 12 mm. The engineer should not describe it as “nearly good enough” and declare success. The evidence shows that the requirement was not met under those conditions. A redesign might increase stiffness by changing the section geometry, adding a support, or using a different material. Each option has consequences for cost, mass, and storage.

SECTION 06

Verification and Validation Are Different

Two important engineering questions sound similar but serve different purposes. Verification asks whether the design meets its specified requirements. Validation asks whether the resulting product actually meets the user's underlying need in its intended setting. A ramp could pass a laboratory load test and still be difficult for staff to deploy safely in a crowded room.

Suppose a team verifies that a portable ramp supports the required load and fits the specified storage dimensions. During a realistic user trial, however, staff struggle to unfold it without pinching their fingers. The design may have passed some verification checks but failed an important aspect of validation. The team should revisit the requirements and the design, not assume the product is acceptable because the original checklist was satisfied.

Remember: Verification = “Did we build it to the requirements?” Validation = “Did we solve the right problem for the user?” Both depend on evidence, and both can lead to redesign.

SECTION 07

Worked Example: Improve a Portable Ramp

An engineering team is testing a portable ramp with two measurable requirements: the ramp must weigh no more than 18 kg, and it must deflect no more than 8 mm under the specified load. The first prototype weighs 14 kg but deflects 12 mm. Its mass is acceptable, but its deflection is not. Because the design violates a mandatory requirement, it cannot be accepted as it stands.

B — Added supports17 kg7 mmMeets both limits
C — Heavy reinforcement21 kg5 mmFails mass limit

Prototype B is a promising improvement because it meets both stated limits. Prototype C is stiffer, but it exceeds the mass constraint. The “best” design is therefore not simply the one with the smallest deflection. Engineers must consider the entire set of requirements.

Notice also what the table does not establish. It does not prove that Prototype B is durable, safe under every possible load, affordable, or easy to deploy. Additional tests and documentation would be needed before making those claims. A careful engineering conclusion is limited to what the evidence actually demonstrates.

SECTION 08

Use Evidence to Decide What to Change

When a prototype misses a target, the next step is to diagnose the gap between expected and observed performance. An engineer should ask which requirement failed, what measurements are trustworthy, what factors might explain the result, and which change can be tested. Replacing the entire design without understanding the failure may introduce new problems while leaving the original cause unresolved.

Imagine a ramp that deflects too much. Adding material everywhere may reduce deflection, but it also increases mass and cost. Changing the support arrangement might improve stiffness more efficiently. A sound redesign proposal explains why the change is expected to help, what trade-offs it introduces, and how the next test will evaluate the result.

Engineers keep revision records: the version tested, the configuration, the test conditions, the measurements, and the reason for the next change. Without this documentation, a team can accidentally repeat unsuccessful ideas or compare measurements taken under different conditions. Lesson A2.6 will explore how records support responsible redesign.

SECTION 09

Design Decisions Require Honest Conclusions

Engineers communicate their findings in terms of evidence, not optimism. “The prototype passed the specified deflection and mass checks in these trials” is a defensible statement when the measurements support it. “The design is completely safe” is much broader and requires far more evidence. A successful test under one condition cannot establish performance under every condition.

Engineers also distinguish an observed improvement from a proven cause. If a prototype performs better after two changes were made at once, the team may not know which change produced the improvement. Controlled testing helps isolate the effects of individual changes, a topic developed in Lesson A2.5.

Throughout Unit A2, you will learn how to define requirements, compare alternatives, interpret technical drawings, design fair tests, and document improvements. Those skills form a connected process: engineering solutions become more credible when every important decision can be explained and checked.

REFERENCE

Engineering Vocabulary

Select a term to reveal its meaning.

Engineering design

Systematic development and improvement of a solution to a defined need.

Iteration

Repeating design, testing, and revision based on evidence.

Problem statement

Description of a need without unnecessarily selecting a solution.

Criterion

Measurable or assessable characteristic used to judge a design.

Constraint

Mandatory limit that a design must satisfy.

Requirement

Specific condition or performance target a design must meet.

Concept

Proposed approach to solving an engineering problem.

Alternative

One of several candidate design solutions.

Prototype

Early version built or modeled to investigate a design.

Model

Simplified representation used to predict or explore behavior.

Test condition

Specified circumstances under which performance is measured.

Deflection

Displacement of a component under load.

Verification

Checking whether stated specifications are met.

Validation

Checking whether the solution meets the actual user need.

Redesign

Modification of a design in response to evidence or changing requirements.

Trade-off

Gain in one characteristic accompanied by a cost in another.

Feasibility

Whether a proposed design can satisfy its requirements.

Revision record

Documentation of changes, reasons, and measured results.

Evidence

Observations and measurements supporting a conclusion.

Assumption

Condition taken as true for analysis and requiring clear documentation.
