Section 01

The engineering challenge: choose a lifting system

A mechanical engineer has been asked to choose a hoist for a warehouse. The hoist must raise a heavy load to a storage platform, complete the lift before a deadline, and avoid wasting unnecessary electrical energy. There may be several machines available, but the machine that lifts fastest is not necessarily the machine that uses energy most efficiently. A sound design recommendation must account for the task, the constraints, the calculations, and the trade-offs.

In this lesson, you will combine the skills from A8.1 through A8.5 to evaluate competing hoists. You will calculate useful lifting work, compare efficiency, determine useful output power and electrical input power, and justify a selection using evidence. The goal is not simply to choose a machine with the biggest number. The goal is to choose a machine that satisfies the job's requirements.

Engineering scenario

A warehouse needs to raise a 300 kg crate by 8 m. The lift must be finished in no more than 20 seconds. The engineer can choose from multiple hoists with different speeds and efficiencies. Which machine meets the deadline, and which qualifying machine uses the least electrical energy?

Section 02

Identify requirements, constraints, and trade-offs

A requirement is something the design must accomplish, such as lifting a particular load to a particular height. A constraint is a limit the design must obey, such as a maximum time, a motor's rated capacity, or a budget. A trade-off occurs when improving one feature may make another less desirable. For example, a faster hoist may have lower efficiency.

Before calculating, list what is known and what must be decided. In the warehouse example, the required load is 300 kg, the lift height is 8 m, and the maximum permitted lift time is 20 s. The hoist must have sufficient load capacity; the performance data in this lesson assume each listed candidate can safely handle the load. In real engineering, load rating, safety factors, braking, duty cycle, and manufacturer specifications must also be checked.

First decision: eliminate infeasible options

If a machine takes 24 seconds and the deadline is 20 seconds, it is not a valid choice even if it is highly efficient. Engineers first eliminate machines that fail mandatory constraints. They then compare the remaining machines against the design priorities.

Section 03

Calculate the useful energy for a shared lifting task

All machines that raise the same mass through the same vertical height give the load the same increase in gravitational potential energy. This is the useful output energy. Calculate it with Euseful = mgh, using mass in kilograms, gravitational field strength g = 9.8 N/kg, and vertical height in meters. The result is in joules.

Worked example 1 — Warehouse crate

Step 1: Identify m = 300 kg and h = 8 m. Step 2: Substitute into mgh: 300 × 9.8 × 8. Step 3: Calculate 300 × 9.8 = 2,940 N, then 2,940 × 8 = 23,520 J.

Every candidate hoist must deliver 23,520 J of useful energy to the crate. The machines may require different amounts of electrical input energy because their efficiencies differ.

Remember that ramp length or cable length is not the height in this equation. Use the actual vertical change in elevation.

Section 04

Rank efficiency using equal useful output

Efficiency is the proportion of input energy that becomes useful output: efficiency = useful output energy ÷ input energy. Multiply by 100 to express the result as a percentage. When the useful output is the same for all machines, the machine that requires less input energy is the more efficient machine.

HoistUseful energyInput energyEfficiency
A23,520 J29,400 J80%
B23,520 J26,133.33 J90%
C23,520 J33,600 J70%
Worked example 2 — Rank the machines

Hoist A: 23,520 ÷ 29,400 × 100 = 80%. Hoist B: 23,520 ÷ 26,133.33 × 100 ≈ 90%. Hoist C: 23,520 ÷ 33,600 × 100 = 70%. The efficiency ranking from highest to lowest is B, A, C. The input-energy ranking from least to greatest is also B, A, C because the useful output is identical.

Do not rank efficiency by input energy alone when machines perform different useful tasks. A fair efficiency comparison always considers both useful output and input.

Section 05

Compare useful output power and lifting time

Power tells us how quickly useful energy is delivered. For a lift, Puseful = Euseful/t = mgh/t. The useful energy remains 23,520 J in the warehouse scenario, but different lifting times produce different useful output powers.

Worked example 3 — Compare power

For A: 23,520 ÷ 12 = 1,960 W. For B: 23,520 ÷ 24 = 980 W. For C: 23,520 ÷ 16 = 1,470 W. Hoist A provides the greatest useful output power because it performs the same lift in the least time.

Hoist B is the most efficient of the three, but it fails the 20-second deadline. If the deadline is mandatory, B cannot be recommended even though it uses the least energy.

Section 06

Calculate electrical input power

Useful output power is the rate at which the crate gains gravitational potential energy. Electrical input power is the rate at which the motor receives energy. Since real hoists are less than 100% efficient, the electrical input power is higher than useful output power. Convert efficiency from a percentage to a decimal before dividing.

Input power = useful output power ÷ efficiency as a decimal
Worked example 4 — Hoist A

Hoist A delivers 1,960 W of useful power at 80% efficiency. Convert 80% to 0.80. Input power = 1,960 ÷ 0.80 = 2,450 W. Check using input energy: 29,400 J ÷ 12 s = 2,450 W.

Worked example 5 — Hoist C

Hoist C delivers 1,470 W of useful power at 70% efficiency. Convert 70% to 0.70. Input power = 1,470 ÷ 0.70 = 2,100 W. Check: 33,600 J ÷ 16 s = 2,100 W.

Hoist A has greater electrical input power than C because it delivers the lift faster. But A uses less total electrical energy for the lift (29,400 J compared with 33,600 J) because it is more efficient. This is an important distinction: power is a rate; energy is the total amount transferred.

Section 07

Make a defensible engineering recommendation

Return to the warehouse's 20-second deadline. Hoist B is eliminated because it takes 24 seconds. Hoists A and C both meet the deadline. Between them, A is faster (12 s versus 16 s), more efficient (80% versus 70%), and uses less input energy (29,400 J versus 33,600 J). Under the stated criteria, Hoist A is the strongest recommendation.

Model engineering justification

“I recommend Hoist A because it lifts the required 300 kg load through 8 m in 12 seconds, meeting the 20-second limit. Its useful output is 23,520 J. It operates at 80% efficiency, compared with 70% for the other qualifying machine, Hoist C. Hoist A uses 29,400 J of electrical input energy per lift, which is 4,200 J less than Hoist C. Although Hoist B is more efficient, it takes 24 seconds and therefore fails the required time constraint.”

A strong recommendation names the chosen option, verifies that it meets mandatory requirements, cites relevant calculations, and acknowledges trade-offs. If the requirements change—for example, if the deadline is extended to 25 seconds—the best choice may change too. With a 25-second limit and energy efficiency as the primary goal, Hoist B would become a strong candidate.

Section 09

A complete engineering selection workflow

Use this procedure when you see a design comparison on an assessment or in a real engineering task. First, read the constraints and reject machines that cannot meet them. Second, calculate the useful energy needed for the task using mgh. Third, calculate efficiency using useful energy divided by input energy, or find input energy by dividing useful energy by efficiency as a decimal. Fourth, calculate useful power by dividing useful energy by lift time. Fifth, calculate input power by dividing input energy by time. Finally, compare the qualifying options according to the stated priority, such as least energy used, shortest time, or lowest input-power requirement.

Worked example 6 — A changed design requirement

Suppose a hoist must deliver 12,000 J of useful lifting energy within 15 seconds. Hoist X needs 16,000 J of input energy and takes 12 s; Hoist Y needs 15,000 J and takes 18 s. Hoist X is 12,000/16,000 × 100 = 75% efficient and supplies 12,000/12 = 1,000 W of useful power. Hoist Y is 12,000/15,000 × 100 = 80% efficient and supplies about 667 W of useful power. Y is more efficient but fails the 15-second deadline. Choose X because it meets the mandatory constraint.

Cumulative walkthrough: from load to electrical input power

A 400 kg crate must rise 5 m in 10 s. The hoist is 80% efficient. The engineer needs to find the useful energy, useful power, electrical input energy, and electrical input power. These four answers have different meanings and units.

Step 1 — Useful energy: Euseful = mgh = 400 × 9.8 × 5 = 19,600 J. This is the increase in the crate's gravitational potential energy.

Step 2 — Useful output power: Puseful = Euseful/t = 19,600/10 = 1,960 W. This is the rate of useful lifting energy delivered.

Step 3 — Input energy: 80% = 0.80. Einput = Euseful/0.80 = 19,600/0.80 = 24,500 J. The motor needs more energy than the crate gains because the hoist is not perfectly efficient.

Step 4 — Electrical input power: Pinput = Einput/t = 24,500/10 = 2,450 W. Check by dividing useful power by efficiency: 1,960/0.80 = 2,450 W.

Step 5 — Energy transferred to other forms: 24,500 − 19,600 = 4,900 J. Some input energy becomes heat, sound, and other non-useful outputs.

Independent challenge: Repeat the process for a 200 kg crate lifted 6 m in 12 s at 75% efficiency.

Reveal the complete solution

Useful energy = 200 × 9.8 × 6 = 11,760 J; useful power = 11,760/12 = 980 W; input energy = 11,760/0.75 = 15,680 J; input power = 15,680/12 ≈ 1,306.7 W; energy transferred to other forms = 15,680 − 11,760 = 3,920 J.

Section 10

Independent practice with guided solutions

Attempt each problem before opening its solution. For numerical questions, write the formula, substitute the values, and label the units. For design questions, support your recommendation with evidence.

1. A 200 kg crate is lifted 6 m. Find useful lifting energy.

mgh = 200 × 9.8 × 6 = 11,760 J.

2. A hoist delivers 8,000 J useful energy and uses 10,000 J input. Find efficiency.

8,000/10,000 × 100 = 80%.

3. Three hoists perform the same task and use 9,000 J, 8,500 J, and 10,200 J of input. Rank their efficiency.

Because useful output is equal, lower input means higher efficiency. Second, first, third.

4. A machine transfers 6,000 J useful energy in 12 s. Find useful output power.

6,000/12 = 500 W.

5. A hoist uses 9,000 J input over 15 s. Find average electrical input power.

9,000/15 = 600 W.

6. A hoist needs 10,000 J useful energy at 80% efficiency. Find input energy.

10,000/0.80 = 12,500 J.

7. A hoist delivers 600 W useful power at 75% efficiency. Find input power.

600/0.75 = 800 W.

8. A 250 kg load rises 4 m in 10 s at 70% efficiency. Find electrical input power.

Useful energy = 250 × 9.8 × 4 = 9,800 J. Useful power = 980 W. Input power = 980/0.70 = 1,400 W.

9. Hoist A uses 12,000 J and takes 12 s; B uses 11,000 J and takes 19 s. Both deliver 9,000 J. Deadline is 15 s. Choose a hoist.

Only A meets the 15-second deadline. A is 75% efficient; B is about 81.8% efficient but is too slow.

10. Two hoists have equal useful energy but times of 10 s and 20 s. Compare useful power.

The 10-second hoist has twice the useful output power because the same energy is transferred in half the time.

11. A hoist delivers 5,000 J useful energy from 6,250 J input. How much energy goes to other forms?

6,250 − 5,000 = 1,250 J.

12. A hoist is 90% efficient but takes 22 s. Another is 80% efficient and takes 16 s. The deadline is 18 s. Which should you recommend if both can safely lift the load?

Choose the 80%-efficient, 16-second hoist because it meets the mandatory deadline. The 90%-efficient machine does not.

Section 11

Glossary: engineering design vocabulary

Click each aqua term to reveal its definition.

Engineering design

The process of developing and evaluating solutions to meet needs within constraints.

Requirement

A function or performance outcome a design must achieve.

Constraint

A limit or rule a design must satisfy, such as maximum time or load capacity.

Trade-off

A compromise between competing design qualities or goals.

Hoist

A machine that raises or lowers a load.

Load

The object or material a machine lifts or supports.

Useful output energy

The energy delivered to the intended task, such as increasing a load's GPE.

Input energy

The energy supplied to a machine to operate it.

Efficiency

Useful output divided by input, expressed as a ratio or percentage.

Useful output power

Useful energy delivered per second, measured in watts.

Electrical input power

Electrical energy supplied per second, measured in watts.

Gravitational potential energy

Energy associated with height; the change during a lift is mgh.

Watt (W)

A unit of power equal to one joule per second.

Joule (J)

A unit of energy and work.

Feasible design

A design that meets all mandatory requirements and constraints.

Design justification

An explanation of a design decision supported by relevant measurements, calculations, and constraints.

Section 12

Unit 8 synthesis and next steps

You can now analyze a lifting system from beginning to end. Calculate useful work or energy from the load and height, determine how quickly the machine performs the lift, evaluate how efficiently it converts input energy into useful output, and compare candidate machines under practical constraints. These are core mechanical engineering skills because design decisions require both accurate calculations and clear explanations.

Before completing the Unit 8 assessment, make sure you can distinguish energy (J) from power (W), useful output from electrical input, and a high-efficiency machine from a machine that simply operates quickly. A strong engineering recommendation always checks the constraints before declaring a winner.
