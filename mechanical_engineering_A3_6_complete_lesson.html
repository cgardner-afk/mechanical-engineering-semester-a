SECTION 01

The Purpose of an Engineering Experiment

An engineering experiment is a planned investigation designed to answer a specific question using measurements. Engineers test a component because a calculation or simulation alone cannot reveal every real-world influence. Friction, imperfect manufacturing, changing temperatures, instrument limitations, and human technique can all affect performance. A useful experiment produces evidence that another person could inspect and, ideally, reproduce.

Consider a small machine whose rubber vibration mounts seem to deform too much under load. An engineer might ask whether a thicker mount reduces deflection enough to satisfy the design limit. That is more useful than the vague question “Are these mounts good?” because it identifies a measurable outcome and a design decision.

An experiment is not successful only when the original idea is confirmed. If the data contradict the prediction, the experiment can still be valuable. The goal is to learn what the system actually does and make a justified engineering decision.

SECTION 02

Turn a Design Problem into a Testable Question

Begin with a design requirement, such as “The bracket must deflect no more than 2.0 mm under a 100 N load.” A testable question identifies what will change, what will be measured, and the conditions of the test. For example: “How does bracket thickness affect tip deflection under a constant 100 N load?”

A hypothesis is a prediction that can be checked against measurements. One possible hypothesis is: “Increasing bracket thickness will reduce tip deflection when material, length, and applied load are held constant.” A hypothesis is not a guarantee. It guides the test and helps define which observations matter.

Write a success criterion before collecting data. Otherwise, it is tempting to change the definition of success after seeing the results. A success criterion might be “Mean measured deflection is at most 2.0 mm at 100 N, with no visible permanent deformation.”

SECTION 03

Identify Variables and Keep the Test Fair

The independent variable is the quantity deliberately changed. In the bracket example, it is thickness. The dependent variable is the measured response: tip deflection. Controlled variables include material, bracket length, mounting method, load location, and measurement technique. If several conditions change at once, it becomes difficult to tell which one caused the result.

Real experiments also contain disturbances that are hard to eliminate completely. A sensor may warm up, a fixture may shift slightly, or the operator may read a scale from a different angle. Engineers reduce these effects through consistent procedures, calibration, and repeated trials. A control group or baseline design provides a reference for comparison when appropriate.

An experimental plan should specify the values of the independent variable, the number of trials at each value, and the order in which measurements will be collected. Record units with every quantity. A number such as “1.8” is not a complete engineering result unless readers know whether it means millimeters, newtons, or something else.

SECTION 04

Plan Equipment, Safety, and a Repeatable Procedure

A good procedure is detailed enough that a different student could perform the test in the same way. List the equipment and its measurement resolution. For a bracket-deflection test, the equipment might include a rigid mounting fixture, a set of brackets, calibrated masses or a force gauge, a ruler or dial indicator, and a recording sheet. Check that the measurement tool can distinguish changes small enough to matter.

Safety is part of experimental design. Identify pinch points, sharp edges, unstable loads, electrical hazards, and the possibility of sudden component failure. Never stand beneath a suspended load or exceed the rated capacity of the apparatus. In a classroom, use a teacher-approved low-force setup and secure components before applying load.

A repeatable procedure could be: (1) inspect and secure the fixture; (2) install the bracket; (3) zero the indicator; (4) apply the specified load slowly; (5) wait for the reading to stabilize; (6) record the deflection; (7) remove the load and inspect the part; and (8) repeat the trial. Changing the order or skipping a step may change the data.

SECTION 05

Build a Data Table Before Testing

Design the table before beginning the experiment. Include the independent variable, trial numbers, measured units, and room for observations. Record raw measurements rather than only averages. If one trial is unexpectedly high, the original values help you investigate what happened.

The following illustrative data compare three bracket thicknesses under the same load. Each thickness was tested three times. The deflection is smaller for thicker brackets, but individual readings vary slightly. That variation is expected in real measurement work and should not be hidden.

A table is a record of observations, not yet an explanation. After collecting data, calculate summaries and make a graph to look for patterns. Keep the raw table available so another engineer can verify the calculations.

SECTION 06

Calculate Means and Compare with a Requirement

A mean summarizes several trials: add the readings and divide by the number of readings. The range, found by subtracting the smallest reading from the largest, gives a simple indication of spread. Neither statistic tells the whole story, but together they help distinguish typical performance from variation.

For the 4 mm bracket, suppose the measured deflections are 2.4 mm, 2.5 mm, and 2.3 mm. The mean is 2.4 mm and the range is 0.2 mm. Because the design requirement is at most 2.0 mm, this bracket does not meet the criterion on the basis of its mean. A thicker bracket may perform better, but the final decision should also consider mass, cost, material availability, and the relevant safety factor.

A small difference near a limit deserves care. If the measured mean is 1.99 mm but the instrument's uncertainty is approximately ±0.10 mm, a simple pass/fail claim may be premature. Engineers report uncertainty and use appropriate acceptance rules rather than pretending every measurement is exact.

YOU ARE THE ENGINEER · WORKED CALCULATION

For a 4 mm bracket, the readings are 2.4, 2.5, and 2.3 mm. Find the mean and range.

  1. Step 1 — Identify the data: 2.4 mm, 2.5 mm, 2.3 mm; number of trials = 3.
  2. Step 2 — Calculate the mean: (2.4 + 2.5 + 2.3) ÷ 3 = 7.2 ÷ 3 = 2.4 mm.
  3. Step 3 — Calculate the range: 2.5 − 2.3 = 0.2 mm.
  4. Step 4 — Compare with the requirement: 2.4 mm is greater than the 2.0 mm limit, so this design fails the mean-deflection criterion.
SECTION 07

Use Graphs to Interpret Experimental Results

Graph the independent variable on the horizontal axis and the dependent variable on the vertical axis. For bracket thickness and deflection, a scatter plot shows each trial, while a second series can display the mean at each thickness. The plotted pattern makes it easier to see whether deflection generally decreases as thickness increases.

A trend does not prove that the relationship will continue indefinitely. The test only provides direct evidence for the thicknesses and loads actually examined. Predicting a value between tested thicknesses is interpolation; predicting beyond the tested range is extrapolation, which usually requires greater caution.

Look for clusters, outliers, and changes in spread. If one result differs greatly from the others, investigate whether it reflects an equipment problem, a recording error, or genuine behavior. Do not delete an inconvenient point simply to make a graph look cleaner.

SECTION 08

Distinguish Error, Uncertainty, and Experimental Limitations

Measurement error is the difference between a measured value and a reference value when that reference is known. Uncertainty describes the doubt associated with a measurement or estimate. Random variation makes repeated readings scatter; systematic error pushes measurements consistently in one direction. A mis-zeroed dial indicator, for example, can make every deflection appear too large or too small.

A limitation is a condition that restricts what the results can support. Testing only one material, one load, and three specimens cannot establish how every bracket will behave under all conditions. A strong conclusion openly states such limits rather than overstating confidence.

Improve the experiment by changing one meaningful feature of the procedure: use a more precise instrument, increase the number of trials, test additional load levels, or verify fixture alignment. The best improvement depends on the weakness revealed by the original data.

SECTION 09

Write a Conclusion That Uses Evidence

An engineering conclusion answers the original question and connects a claim to numerical evidence. A useful structure is claim, evidence, reasoning, and limitation. For example: “Increasing thickness reduced mean bracket deflection in our test. Mean deflection fell from 2.4 mm at 4 mm thickness to 1.4 mm at 6 mm thickness under a constant 100 N load. This supports selecting the thicker bracket for further evaluation because it meets the 2.0 mm mean-deflection target. However, only three trials per thickness were performed.”

Avoid statements such as “The experiment worked” or “The graph proves thicker is always better.” Those statements do not explain the measured result or the limits of the test. An engineering recommendation should say what to do next and why.

Experiments often lead to another cycle of design, test, and revision. If the 6 mm bracket passes deflection requirements but is too heavy, an engineer may investigate a different geometry or material. The evidence helps narrow the next design choice.

SECTION 11

Independent Practice

Work each problem on paper before opening the explanation. In your response, identify the experimental variable or calculation, include units, and explain what the result means for an engineering decision.

1. Identify the variables

You test whether changing a spring's wire diameter affects its extension under the same load.

Worked answer: Independent: wire diameter. Dependent: extension. Controls: applied load, material, coil geometry, temperature, and measurement method.

2. Write a testable question

A team wants to know whether different fan speeds affect motor temperature.

Worked answer: How does fan speed (rpm) affect motor temperature (°C) after ten minutes at a constant motor load?

3. Calculate a mean

Three deflection readings are 1.6, 1.8, and 1.7 mm.

Worked answer: Mean = (1.6 + 1.8 + 1.7) ÷ 3 = 5.1 ÷ 3 = 1.7 mm.

4. Calculate a range

The readings are 1.6, 1.8, and 1.7 mm.

Worked answer: Range = maximum − minimum = 1.8 − 1.6 = 0.2 mm.

5. Compare with a limit

A design requires deflection ≤ 2.0 mm. Three trials give 1.9, 2.1, and 2.0 mm.

Worked answer: Mean = 2.0 mm, which meets the mean criterion. However, one trial exceeds the limit, so acceptance depends on whether the requirement applies to the mean or to every individual result.

6. Spot a systematic error

A dial indicator reads +0.20 mm when the bracket has no load.

Worked answer: The instrument has a zero offset. Re-zero or calibrate it before collecting data; otherwise all measurements may be biased high.

7. Improve repeatability

Two students measure the same bracket using different mounting positions.

Worked answer: Standardize the mounting location and written procedure. Otherwise the fixture position becomes an uncontrolled variable.

8. Write a cautious conclusion

Three samples of a new mount deform less than three samples of the original mount.

Worked answer: The new mount showed lower deflection in this small test, but more samples and additional operating conditions are needed before generalizing the result.

SECTION 12

Lesson Glossary

These terms describe the process of planning, conducting, and evaluating an engineering experiment. Open each term to review its meaning.

Experiment

A planned test used to answer a question with measurements.

Testable question

A specific question that can be investigated with observations.

Hypothesis

A prediction that can be evaluated using evidence.

Independent variable

The factor deliberately changed by the experimenter.

Dependent variable

The measured response.

Controlled variable

A condition held consistent to make a comparison fair.

Baseline

A reference design or condition used for comparison.

Trial

One repeated run or measurement under defined conditions.

Raw data

Measurements recorded before averaging or interpretation.

Mean

Sum of readings divided by their number.

Range

Highest reading minus lowest reading.

Measurement uncertainty

An estimate of doubt associated with a measurement.

Random variation

Unpredictable scatter among repeated readings.

Systematic error

A consistent bias in a measurement process.

Calibration

Checking or adjusting an instrument against a known reference.

Outlier

An observation unusually far from the other measurements.

Interpolation

Estimating within a tested range.

Extrapolation

Predicting beyond the tested range.

Success criterion

A measurable requirement set before the test.

Repeatability

Consistency when a test is repeated under similar conditions.

Limitation

A constraint on what the experiment can establish.

Evidence-based conclusion

A claim supported by measured results and qualified by limitations.

UNIT TEST + FINAL EXAM SKILLS

Identify Variables in an Unfamiliar Experiment

On an engineering assessment, you may encounter a test you have never seen before. Do not rely on memorizing which quantity was independent in the bracket activity. Instead ask three questions: What did the experimenter deliberately change? What response was measured? What conditions should remain the same? These identify the independent, dependent, and controlled variables.

Worked example — cooling a motor:
  1. Question: How does fan speed affect motor temperature after ten minutes of operation?
  2. Independent variable: Fan speed, because the experimenter selects different settings.
  3. Dependent variable: Motor temperature after ten minutes, because it is measured.
  4. Controlled variables: Motor load, room conditions, sensor placement, and the ten-minute operating period.
  5. Why it matters: If motor load changes at the same time as fan speed, the effect of fan speed becomes difficult to isolate.
Second example — beam material:
  1. Question: How does beam material affect bending under a fixed load?
  2. Independent variable: Material type.
  3. Dependent variable: Measured deflection.
  4. Controls: Beam length, cross-section, support spacing, load, and measurement location.

Independent practice: Students test how wheel diameter affects the distance a toy car rolls after one motor rotation. Identify the independent variable, dependent variable, and at least two controls.

Show worked solution

Independent: wheel diameter. Dependent: distance traveled. Controls: same car, surface, motor rotation count, starting procedure, and wheel material where possible. The test needs these controls so a change in distance can be associated with wheel diameter.

APPLY WHAT YOU LEARNED

Additional Practice: Variables in a Ramp Experiment

Before analyzing an experiment, identify what is intentionally changed and what is measured. Imagine testing how the angle of a ramp affects a cart's speed at the bottom. The independent variable is ramp angle because the experimenter chooses it. The dependent variable is bottom speed because it is measured in response. A controlled variable could be the cart's mass, wheel condition, starting procedure, or measurement method.

Worked experimental plan:
  1. Question: How does ramp angle affect the cart's speed at the bottom?
  2. Prediction: A steeper ramp will produce a higher bottom speed under otherwise comparable conditions.
  3. Change: Set the ramp to 10°, 15°, and 20°.
  4. Measure: Record bottom speed in m/s for each trial.
  5. Control: Use the same cart, release method, track surface, and speed sensor. Keep the relevant starting geometry consistent or document it carefully.
  6. Repeat: Collect several trials per angle and calculate the mean and range.
  7. Interpret: Compare the measured results, report any variation, and avoid claiming the trend must continue beyond tested angles.

Try it: A student says, “Speed is the independent variable because speed changes.” Explain the mistake.

Show reasoning

Something can change without being the independent variable. The independent variable is what the experimenter deliberately adjusts: ramp angle. Bottom speed is the measured dependent variable.
