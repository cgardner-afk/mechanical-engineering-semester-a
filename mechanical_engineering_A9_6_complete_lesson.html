Section 01

The final engineering design review

At the end of an engineering project, a team must do more than announce that its machine works. It must defend its design using requirements, calculations, test results, and a clear explanation of trade-offs. This is called a design review. A strong review lets another engineer understand what the system must do, what evidence supports the decision, and what limitations remain.

Our final Semester A challenge is to recommend a warehouse lifting-and-transport system. The system must move a loaded cart, raise a payload, support the load safely, and satisfy budget and performance constraints. You will practice the review process and then revisit the central formulas and reasoning skills from the semester.

Design review question

Which design should the customer approve, and what evidence supports that choice? Your answer must distinguish calculated predictions from measured test results. Neither a large motor rating nor a low price is enough on its own.

Section 02

Start with requirements, not preferences

A requirement describes what the design must achieve. A constraint limits how it may achieve that goal. A preference helps rank designs that already meet mandatory requirements. Engineering teams should write measurable acceptance criteria before choosing a solution.

CategoryDesign briefVerification method
Required performanceLift 200 kg through 3 m within 6 sRated-load timed lift
Electrical constraintAverage input power ≤ 1,500 WPower calculation and measurement
Structural constraintYield-based FoS ≥ 2.0Stress and material-strength check
Structural constraintDeflection ≤ 4 mmLoaded deflection test
Budget constraintTotal cost ≤ $2,400Itemized cost estimate
PreferenceLowest feasible massCompare passing candidates

“Make it powerful” is too vague to verify. “Raise 200 kg by 3 m in 6 seconds” can be tested directly. A design review should identify each requirement and show the corresponding evidence, rather than offering a general claim that the machine is safe or efficient.

Section 03

Check energy, power, and efficiency

To raise a payload vertically, the useful energy is E = mgh. Average useful lifting power is Puseful = mgh/t. When the overall efficiency is η, average electrical input power is Pinput = mgh/(tη). Use g = 9.8 N/kg and convert percentage efficiency to a decimal before calculating.

Worked example 1 — Lift requirement

A lift raises 200 kg through 3 m in 6 s at 80% efficiency. Useful energy = 200 × 9.8 × 3 = 5,880 J. Average useful power = 5,880/6 = 980 W. Electrical input power = 980/0.80 = 1,225 W. This meets the 1,500 W input-power limit in the simplified average-power model.

If the deadline changes to 3 seconds with the same payload, height, and efficiency, required average electrical power doubles to 2,450 W. That exceeds the available supply. The relationship is important: with energy and efficiency fixed, power is inversely proportional to allowed time.

Do not confuse useful mechanical power with electrical input power. If efficiency is below 100%, electrical input must exceed useful output for the same interval.

Section 04

Check cart forces and acceleration

A cart's motor applies a driving force, while friction and other resistance oppose motion. For a simplified straight-line problem, Fnet = Fdrive − Fresist. Newton's second law gives Fnet = ma. If a problem asks for the maximum total moving mass at a required acceleration, rearrange to mmax = (Fdrive − Fresist)/arequired.

Worked example 2 — Maximum mass

A cart has 950 N driving force and 150 N resistance. Required acceleration is 2.0 m/s². Net force = 950 − 150 = 800 N. Maximum total moving mass = 800/2.0 = 400 kg. If the empty cart has mass 125 kg, maximum payload is 275 kg.

The mass in F = ma is the total moving mass unless the problem states otherwise. A design review should say whether its capacity includes the empty vehicle.

Section 05

Check stress, safety factors, and deflection

A safe-looking structure is not enough. Engineers check relevant failure modes. For uniform axial loading, average stress is σ = F/A. When F is in newtons and A is in mm², stress is in N/mm², numerically equal to MPa. A simplified yield-based factor of safety is FoS = yield strength / working stress.

Worked example 3 — Tension member

A lifting member carries 10,000 N in axial tension over 200 mm². Average stress = 10,000/200 = 50 MPa. If yield strength is 150 MPa, the yield-based factor of safety is 150/50 = 3.0. It passes a minimum requirement of 2.0 for that failure mode.

Also check deflection, the amount a member bends or moves under load. A beam can be strong enough to avoid yielding yet deflect too far. If a beam deflects 5 mm under a 4 mm limit, it fails the stiffness requirement even if its FoS is high.

These calculations do not prove complete structural safety. Real reviews may also require buckling, fatigue, connection strength, dynamic loads, and appropriate codes and standards.

Section 06

Check motion, momentum, and stopping

Moving equipment creates additional hazards. Kinetic energy is KE = ½mv², and momentum is p = mv. When a cart stops, its momentum changes to zero. Average net stopping force magnitude can be estimated using Favg = |Δp|/Δt. A longer stopping time reduces average force for the same momentum change.

Worked example 4 — Impact protection

A 100 kg cart moves at 4 m/s. Its momentum magnitude is 100 × 4 = 400 kg·m/s. If it stops in 0.5 s, average net force magnitude is 400/0.5 = 800 N. If a protective device increases stopping time to 1.0 s, average force falls to 400 N. The peak force can still differ from the average.

For constant braking deceleration magnitude a, stopping distance is d = v²/(2a). Doubling initial speed quadruples the idealized stopping distance when deceleration stays constant. In an engineering review, check both distance and force constraints; shortening a stop can increase force.

Section 07

Read an energy-flow diagram

Mechanical systems transfer energy between forms. A motor may take in electrical energy and deliver useful mechanical energy, with the remainder transferred to heating, vibration, or other outputs. Efficiency = useful output / total input × 100% when the two quantities refer to the same operating interval.

Worked example 5 — Efficiency from a diagram

A system receives 1,000 J of electrical energy and delivers 750 J of useful mechanical energy. Efficiency = 750/1,000 × 100% = 75%. The other 250 J is not destroyed; it leaves as non-useful energy transfers. If a different system provides 800 J useful output from 1,000 J input, its efficiency is 80%.

Section 08

Test evidence is stronger than an unsupported claim

A calculation predicts performance under stated assumptions. A test measures how a prototype behaves. A defensible recommendation uses both when appropriate. For example, the claim “This hoist can safely raise the rated load” should be supported by verified load-path and strength calculations, component ratings, and a controlled rated-load test—not just by a photo of the machine lifting something once.

ClaimRelevant evidenceEvidence that is insufficient alone
Meets lifting deadlineRated-load time measurementsMotor brand
Meets power limitMeasured electrical input under specified conditionsUseful output power only
Meets strength requirementLoad, stress, capacity, and FoS calculationPaint color or appearance
Meets deflection limitMeasured displacement under specified loadWeight of the frame alone
Improvement caused the changeControlled before/after comparisonChanging several variables at once

When testing an improvement, change one variable at a time where practical, or use a planned experimental design that can separate effects. Document before-and-after drawings, operating conditions, and measured results. Without that record, it is difficult to explain why performance changed.

Section 09

Final design review: compare three candidates

The following table contains illustrative test results for three warehouse-hoist concepts. The requirements are lift time ≤ 6 s, electrical input ≤ 1,500 W, FoS ≥ 2.0, deflection ≤ 4 mm, and total cost ≤ $2,400. Among feasible designs, the customer prefers the lowest mass.

Worked example 6 — Defend the recommendation

Concept B is the only candidate that passes every mandatory requirement. A fails the factor-of-safety minimum despite being cheaper and lighter. C exceeds the electrical input limit despite its shorter lift time and greater structural capacity. B completes the lift in 5.9 s, uses 1,450 W, has FoS 2.4, deflects 3 mm, and costs $2,250. These results support selecting B for further engineering validation. They do not by themselves certify the machine for operation.

A complete recommendation states the chosen design, compares it with alternatives, identifies its remaining risks, and specifies any additional verification required before approval.

Section 12

Independent cumulative practice with guided solutions

Attempt each question on paper before opening the explanation. These are original practice problems combining ideas from the semester.

1. A 120 kg load rises 5 m. How much potential energy does it gain?

PE = 120 × 9.8 × 5 = 5,880 J.

2. The lift in problem 1 takes 6 s. Find average useful power.

5,880/6 = 980 W.

3. If the lift is 70% efficient, find average electrical input power.

980/0.70 = 1,400 W.

4. A cart has 900 N driving force and 180 N resistance. Find net force.

900 − 180 = 720 N forward.

5. The cart in problem 4 must accelerate at 1.5 m/s². Find maximum total mass.

m = 720/1.5 = 480 kg.

6. A 250 N force acts perpendicular to a lever 0.4 m from its pivot. Find torque magnitude.

τ = Fd = 250 × 0.4 = 100 N·m.

7. A rod carries 8,000 N over 160 mm². Find average axial stress.

8,000/160 = 50 MPa.

8. Yield strength is 200 MPa and working stress is 50 MPa. Find FoS.

200/50 = 4.0.

9. A 100 kg cart moves at 6 m/s. Find kinetic energy.

KE = ½ × 100 × 36 = 1,800 J.

10. The cart in problem 9 stops in 2 s. Find average net force magnitude.

Initial momentum = 100 × 6 = 600 kg·m/s. Force = 600/2 = 300 N.

11. A machine receives 2,000 J and produces 1,500 J useful output. Find efficiency.

1,500/2,000 × 100% = 75%.

12. A beam passes strength checks but deflects 6 mm under a 4 mm limit. What should the review conclude?

Fail. The design violates a mandatory deflection requirement despite sufficient strength.

Section 13

Vocabulary for the final review

Select an aqua term to reveal its definition.

Design review

A structured evaluation of whether a proposed design satisfies its requirements and is supported by evidence.

Requirement

A condition a design must achieve.

Constraint

A mandatory limit on a design or its operation.

Preference

A desirable feature used to compare designs that already satisfy requirements.

Verification

Checking whether a design meets specified requirements.

Validation

Checking whether a design serves its intended real-world use and needs.

Prototype

A preliminary version used to test a design.

Iteration

A cycle of testing, revising, and retesting.

Factor of safety

Capacity divided by demand for a defined failure mode.

Deflection

Displacement of a structure under load.

Efficiency

Useful energy or power output divided by corresponding input.

Load path

The route through which forces transfer to structural supports.

Net force

The vector sum of forces acting on an object.

Momentum

Mass multiplied by velocity.

Impulse

Change in momentum resulting from net force over time.

Trade-off

A situation in which improving one characteristic can worsen another.

Feasible design

A design that satisfies every mandatory criterion.

Evidence-based recommendation

A design choice justified by relevant calculations, measurements, and comparisons.

Section 14

Final engineering reflection

Imagine you are presenting your recommendation to a team of engineers who have never seen your design. State the problem, list the mandatory requirements, show the calculations and test data, explain why competing designs were rejected, and identify remaining uncertainties. Use the following structure: claim → numerical evidence → comparison → limitation → next verification step.

The central lesson of Semester A is that mechanical engineering is not just applying formulas. It is using physics, mathematics, materials knowledge, testing, and careful reasoning to make decisions that can be explained and defended. A good engineer does not hide a failed test or assume that the largest number is always best. A good engineer asks whether the evidence shows that the complete system meets the actual design requirements.
