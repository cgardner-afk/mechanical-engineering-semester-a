SECTION 01

How a beam transfers loads to its supports

Walk across a small footbridge and your weight is carried by the bridge deck, then transferred through the supporting structure into the ground. A warehouse shelf, a floor joist, and a machine frame all perform a similar job: they carry loads and transfer forces to supports. Mechanical engineers must calculate how much force each support carries so that the structure can be designed safely.

In this lesson, we study a simply supported beam resting on two supports. We will ignore the beam's own weight unless a problem says otherwise, and treat downward loads as concentrated point forces. By combining the force and moment equilibrium equations from Lesson A5.3, you will find the upward support reactions at the left and right ends. You will then explore what happens when loads move along the beam or when several loads act together.

SECTION 02

Recognize supports, loads, and reactions

A simply supported beam typically has a pin support at one end and a roller support at the other. The pin can resist horizontal and vertical movement but allows rotation. The roller can resist movement perpendicular to its supporting surface but allows horizontal movement and rotation. On a horizontal beam with only vertical loads, both supports supply upward vertical reactions, which we call RA at the left and RB at the right.

A point load is a force modeled as acting at one specific location. In reality, loads often spread across a small area, but the point-load model is useful when the contact area is small relative to the beam length. The span L is the distance between the supports. Load positions x are measured from the left support unless the diagram states otherwise.

SECTION 03

Use both force and moment equilibrium

A stationary beam must satisfy ΣFy = 0 and ΣM = 0. With vertical loads only, the sum of upward support reactions equals the sum of downward loads. This gives one equation, but it is not enough by itself to determine two unknown reactions. A moment equation provides the second relationship.

RA + RB = ΣPdownward

Choose the left support A as the pivot for the moment equation. The unknown RA has no moment about A because it acts directly through the pivot. The right reaction RB acts at distance L and produces a counterclockwise moment. A downward point load P at distance x from A produces a clockwise moment. Therefore, for one point load:

RBL − Px = 0
RB = Px/L   and   RA = P − RB

This method is more reliable than guessing that supports always share the load equally. Equal sharing occurs for a single centered load on a simply supported beam, but not for an off-center load. After calculating, check that both reactions add to the downward load and that the moments cancel.

SECTION 04

Worked example: a load at midspan

When a single point load acts halfway between two supports, the geometry is symmetric. The two reactions are equal. This result follows from the equilibrium equations, not from a separate rule that applies to every beam.

Worked example 1 · Centered load

A simply supported beam spans 5 m. A 500 N downward load acts at its center, 2.5 m from the left support. Ignore the beam's own weight. Find both support reactions.

  1. Take moments about A: RB(5) − 500(2.5) = 0.
  2. Solve: RB = 1,250/5 = 250 N upward.
  3. Use vertical force balance: RA + 250 − 500 = 0.
  4. Solve: RA = 250 N upward.
  5. Check: 250 + 250 = 500 N, matching the downward load.

If the span were longer but the load remained centered, the reactions would still split the point load equally in this ideal model. The span length matters when loads are not centered or when the beam's own weight and other effects must be included.

SECTION 05

Worked example: an off-center load

When a point load moves closer to one support, that support carries a larger share of the load. The total upward reaction remains equal to the load, but the distribution changes. To determine the shares, take moments about one support and then use vertical force balance.

Worked example 2 · Load nearer the left support

A 360 N downward load acts 2 m from the left end of a 6 m simply supported beam. Find the reactions.

  1. Take moments about A: RB(6) − 360(2) = 0.
  2. RB = 720/6 = 120 N upward.
  3. Use vertical force balance: RA = 360 − 120 = 240 N upward.
  4. Check: 240 + 120 = 360 N. Also, 120(6) = 360(2) = 720 N·m.

The load is closer to the left support, so the left reaction is greater. This is a useful reasonableness check before accepting the arithmetic.

Worked example 3 · Load nearer the right support

A 450 N load acts 6 m from the left support of a beam with an 8 m span. RB = (450 × 6)/8 = 337.5 N upward. RA = 450 − 337.5 = 112.5 N upward. The right support now carries the greater share because the load is nearer to it.

SECTION 06

Find an unknown reaction using force balance

Some engineering problems give the total downward load and one support reaction. You do not need to take moments again to find the other reaction, provided there are no other vertical forces. Use ΣFy = 0 and subtract the known reaction from the total load.

Worked example 4 · Known right reaction

A beam carries 680 N of total downward load. The right support reaction is 260 N upward. Find the left reaction.

RA + 260 − 680 = 0, so RA = 680 − 260 = 420 N upward. Check: 420 + 260 = 680 N.

Remember that a force-balance equation determines only the total upward force. If neither reaction is known, the locations of the loads are needed to calculate how that force is distributed between the supports.

SECTION 07

Worked example: multiple point loads

Several loads may act on the same beam. Each load contributes its own moment about the chosen support. The right reaction times the full span must balance the sum of the clockwise load moments. Once the right reaction is known, the left reaction follows from the total downward force.

RB = (P₁x₁ + P₂x₂ + …)/L

Worked example 5 · Two loads on a beam

A 6 m beam carries a 120 N downward load at 2 m from the left and a 240 N downward load at 4 m. Find the reactions.

  1. Total clockwise moment = 240 + 960 = 1,200 N·m.
  2. Moment equilibrium: RB(6) = 1,200, so RB = 200 N upward.
  3. Total downward load = 120 + 240 = 360 N.
  4. Vertical force balance: RA = 360 − 200 = 160 N upward.
  5. Check: 160 + 200 = 360 N and 200 × 6 = 1,200 N·m.

Worked example 6 · Three separate loads

An 8 m beam carries 100 N at x = 1 m, 200 N at x = 4 m, and 100 N at x = 7 m. The sum of moments about A is 100(1) + 200(4) + 100(7) = 1,600 N·m. Therefore RB = 1,600/8 = 200 N upward. The total load is 400 N, so RA = 200 N upward. The overall arrangement is symmetric even though it contains three loads.

SECTION 08

What happens when a load moves?

A single point load of constant magnitude P moves along a simply supported beam of span L. Its right support reaction is RB = Px/L, where x is its distance from the left support. As the load moves to the right, x increases, so the right reaction increases. Because RA + RB = P, the left reaction decreases by the same amount. The reverse happens when the load moves toward the left.

Worked example 7 · A moving maintenance cart

A 600 N maintenance cart travels along a 10 m simply supported platform. At x = 2 m, the right reaction is 600(2)/10 = 120 N, and the left reaction is 480 N. At the center, x = 5 m, both reactions are 300 N. At x = 8 m, the right reaction is 480 N and the left reaction is 120 N.

The total reaction stays at 600 N in every case, but the support carrying the greater share changes as the cart moves.

This trend is especially important for bridge and crane design. A support must withstand the largest reaction it might experience, not merely the reaction when a load is centered. Lesson A5.6 will use this idea to compare design options.

SECTION 10

Engineering checks and limits of the model

Support reaction calculations are a first step in evaluating a beam. If a support can safely carry only 400 N, a configuration with a calculated reaction of 470 N at that support does not meet the requirement, even if the beam itself is strong enough. Engineers must consider the most demanding load position and check both supports.

Real beams also have weight, and real loads can be spread over a length rather than concentrated at a point. A uniformly distributed load can be replaced by an equivalent total force acting at its center for the purpose of calculating support reactions. For example, a uniform load of 100 N/m over a 4 m span has a total downward force of 400 N acting at the midpoint; its reactions are 200 N each in the ideal simply supported model. This does not mean that all bending and deflection effects are identical to a single point load.

In later engineering courses, you may calculate shear force, bending moment along the beam, deflection, and stress. For this lesson, concentrate on identifying the correct forces and distances, solving both equilibrium equations, and checking whether each support's reaction is reasonable.

SECTION 11

Common errors in support reaction problems

Do not automatically divide every load equally between the two supports. That shortcut is correct for a single centered load, but it fails for off-center and multiple loads. Another frequent mistake is measuring the load distance from the wrong support. If you take moments about the left support, every load's distance must be measured from the left support.

Students also sometimes find one reaction from the moment equation and forget to calculate the other from vertical force balance. Always finish with both reactions and include their directions and units. A useful final check is RA + RB = total downward load. A second check is that moments about either support add to zero.

INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Independent practice and worked solutions

Use the equilibrium equations for each problem. Ignore beam weight and assume only vertical point loads unless otherwise stated.

A · Centered load

A 7 m beam supports a 700 N downward load at midspan. Find both reactions.

Show worked solution

The load is centered, so RA = RB = 700/2 = 350 N upward.

B · Off-center load

A 500 N load acts 3 m from the left end of a 10 m simply supported beam. Find both reactions.

Show worked solution

RB = (500 × 3)/10 = 150 N upward. RA = 500 − 150 = 350 N upward.

C · Given one reaction

A beam carries a total downward load of 850 N. Its right reaction is 320 N upward. Find its left reaction.

Show worked solution

RA = 850 − 320 = 530 N upward.

D · Two loads

A 9 m beam carries 180 N at x = 3 m and 270 N at x = 6 m. Find the right reaction.

Show worked solution

RB(9) = 180(3) + 270(6) = 540 + 1,620 = 2,160 N·m. RB = 240 N upward. The left reaction is 450 − 240 = 210 N upward.

E · Three loads

An 8 m beam carries 80 N at x = 1 m, 120 N at x = 3 m, and 200 N at x = 7 m. Find both reactions.

Show worked solution

RB(8) = 80(1) + 120(3) + 200(7) = 1,840 N·m. RB = 230 N upward. Total load = 400 N, so RA = 170 N upward.

F · Moving load

A 480 N point load moves from the center of a 12 m beam to a point 9 m from the left support. What happens to the right reaction?

Show worked solution

At center, RB = 480(6)/12 = 240 N. At x = 9 m, RB = 480(9)/12 = 360 N. The right reaction increases by 120 N, while the left reaction decreases by 120 N.

G · Support capacity

A 600 N point load acts 7 m from the left end of an 8 m beam. The right support has a 500 N capacity. Does the arrangement meet the limit?

Show worked solution

RB = 600(7)/8 = 525 N, which exceeds 500 N. The arrangement does not meet the right-support capacity limit.

H · Uniform load extension

A 6 m simply supported beam carries a uniform downward load of 80 N/m along its entire span. Find the total load and the reactions.

Show worked solution

Total load = 80 × 6 = 480 N. The equivalent resultant acts at midspan, so each reaction is 240 N upward.

VOCABULARY & GLOSSARY

Vocabulary and key concepts

Simply supported beam

A beam supported so that it can rotate at its supports, commonly modeled with a pin at one end and a roller at the other.

Support reaction

A force supplied by a support to balance applied loads.

Pin support

A support that resists translation in two directions but allows rotation in an ideal planar model.

Roller support

A support that resists translation normal to its supporting surface but allows motion along the surface and rotation.

Point load

A force modeled as acting at a particular position on a beam.

Span

The distance between beam supports.

Midspan

The midpoint between supports.

Vertical force equilibrium

The requirement that the sum of upward and downward forces equals zero.

QUESTION 1 OF 15

Lesson summary

A simply supported beam transfers downward loads into upward support reactions. For a weightless beam with vertical point loads, the reactions must sum to the total downward load, and the moments about either support must balance. For a single load P located x from the left support on a span L, the right reaction is RB = Px/L and the left reaction is RA = P − RB. For multiple loads, add their moments about one support before solving. A centered load is shared equally, while an off-center load places a greater reaction on the nearer support. As a load moves toward one support, that support's reaction increases and the other decreases. These principles allow engineers to evaluate support capacity and prepare for more complex structural analysis.
