CURRICULUM ALIGNMENT

Semester objective A-O4: Analyze forces and motion using Newton's laws, vectors, and free-body diagrams.

Unit essential question: How do forces determine the motion or equilibrium of mechanical systems?

Learning target: Represent forces as vectors and calculate horizontal and vertical components of angled forces.

Objectives: Distinguish scalar and vector quantities; interpret arrow direction and magnitude; add one-dimensional forces; resolve angled forces into x and y components; use component signs; and calculate the magnitude and direction of a resultant force.

SECTION 01

Forces do not always act straight ahead

Imagine a rescue team pulling a stuck vehicle with a cable. The cable runs at an angle, not straight along the road. Part of the pull moves the vehicle forward, while another part pulls it sideways. An engineer needs to understand both parts before deciding how the vehicle will move or whether the cable is positioned safely.

In Lesson A4.2, you used Newton's second law to connect net force, mass, and acceleration. Now you will learn to work with force vectors: forces that have both size and direction. You will draw force arrows, combine forces acting in the same or opposite directions, and break angled forces into horizontal and vertical parts.

These skills prepare you to analyze ramps, lifting cables, cranes, and other systems where forces act in more than one direction. In the next lesson, you will use them to build free-body diagrams.

SECTION 02

What makes a force a vector?

A scalar tells you only how much of something there is. Mass is a scalar: saying that a toolbox has a mass of 12 kg is enough to describe its mass. A vector includes both a magnitude (size) and a direction. Force is a vector. Saying that a cable pulls with 100 N is incomplete unless you also know which way it pulls.

Engineers often draw vectors as arrows. The arrow points in the direction of the force, and its length represents the magnitude when the drawing is made to scale. For example, a long arrow might represent 100 N, while an arrow half as long represents 50 N.

In calculations, we often use a positive or negative sign to show direction along a single line. For example, we can choose right as positive and left as negative. A negative answer then means the resulting force points left; it does not mean the force has a negative physical size.

SECTION 03

Adding forces along one line

When two forces act in the same direction, their magnitudes add. If two workers push a cart with 40 N and 60 N to the right, the combined force is 100 N right. When forces act in opposite directions, subtract their magnitudes and keep the direction of the larger force.

Worked example 1 · Opposing forces

  1. Choose directions: right is positive; left is negative.
  2. Identify forces: +85 N and −30 N.
  3. Add signed values: Fnet,x = 85 + (−30).
  4. Calculate: 55 N right.

If the forces have the same size but opposite directions, they balance. The net force along that line is zero. However, a zero horizontal net force does not tell you whether the vertical forces are balanced; each direction must be considered.

Check your thinking

A machine is pulled 120 N left and 45 N right. Find the horizontal net force.

Show explanation

Choose right as positive: +45 − 120 = −75 N. The net force is 75 N left.

SECTION 04

Horizontal and vertical directions

Engineers describe directions using two perpendicular axes. The x-axis is horizontal, and the y-axis is vertical. We usually choose right as +x, left as −x, up as +y, and down as −y. These signs are a choice, but once you choose them, use them consistently.

An angled force can affect both directions at once. Instead of treating it as one difficult arrow, we can describe it as two simpler forces called components. The horizontal component, Fx, shows how much of the force acts left or right. The vertical component, Fy, shows how much acts up or down.

SECTION 05

Using sine and cosine to find components

If you know a force's magnitude and its angle measured from the positive horizontal axis, you can use the trigonometry skills from Unit A3 to find its components. Think of the force arrow as the long side, or hypotenuse, of a right triangle. The horizontal side is next to the angle, and the vertical side is across from it.

Fx = F cos(θ)    Fy = F sin(θ)

Here, F is the magnitude of the angled force and θ is the angle measured from the positive x-axis. Cosine finds the horizontal component and sine finds the vertical component for this angle convention. These formulas give the correct signs automatically if your calculator uses the correct angle and the angle is measured from +x. For introductory first-quadrant examples, both components are positive.

Worked example 2 · A 100 N cable at 30°

  1. Given: F = 100 N and θ = 30° above the horizontal.
  2. Horizontal: Fx = 100 cos(30°) = 100(0.866) ≈ 86.6 N right.
  3. Vertical: Fy = 100 sin(30°) = 100(0.500) = 50.0 N up.
  4. Check: the two components combine to describe the original 100 N force.
Calculator tip: Use degree mode, not radian mode, when the problem gives an angle in degrees. For a 30° angle, sin(30°) should equal 0.5.

If the angle is measured from the vertical axis instead, the roles of sine and cosine switch. Always look at where the angle is drawn before choosing a formula.

SECTION 06

Understanding signs and directions

Components can point left, right, up, or down. With right and up chosen as positive, a component pointing left is negative and a component pointing down is negative. The sign tells you direction; the magnitude is the absolute value.

For example, a 50 N cable pulls at 30° above the leftward horizontal. Its horizontal component is 50 cos(30°) ≈ 44.3 N left, so Fx = −44.3 N. Its vertical component is 50 sin(30°) = 25.0 N up, so Fy = +25.0 N. This is the same as measuring an angle of 150° counterclockwise from the positive x-axis.

Forces that act straight along one axis have zero component along the other axis. A 40 N force straight upward has Fx = 0 N and Fy = +40 N.

SECTION 07

Combining several forces in two directions

When several forces act on one object, add their horizontal components to find the total horizontal force, ΣFx. Then add their vertical components to find the total vertical force, ΣFy. The Greek letter Σ (sigma) means “sum of.” This method lets engineers combine forces that point in different directions without guessing.

ΣFx = F1x + F2x + …
ΣFy = F1y + F2y + …

Worked example 3 · Two angled pulls

Two cables pull a ring. Cable A pulls with 100 N at 30° above the rightward horizontal. Cable B pulls with 80 N at 30° above the leftward horizontal.

  1. Cable A: FAx = +100 cos(30°) ≈ +86.6 N; FAy = +100 sin(30°) = +50.0 N.
  2. Cable B: FBx = −80 cos(30°) ≈ −69.3 N; FBy = +80 sin(30°) = +40.0 N.
  3. Add horizontal: ΣFx = 86.6 − 69.3 = +17.3 N.
  4. Add vertical: ΣFy = 50.0 + 40.0 = +90.0 N.

The combined force has a small rightward component and a much larger upward component. It points mostly upward and slightly right.

To find the magnitude of the combined force, use the Pythagorean theorem: FR = √[(ΣFx)² + (ΣFy)²]. In the example, FR ≈ √(17.3² + 90²) ≈ 91.6 N. This is called the resultant force. To find its angle above +x, use θ = tan−1(90/17.3) ≈ 79.1°. Because both components are positive, the resultant points up and right.

Remember: The magnitude of the resultant is not generally the sum of the original force magnitudes. Direction matters.
SECTION 08

How engineers use force components

Force components are especially useful for cables, ramps, and cranes. A cable attached at an angle may pull an object forward while also lifting some of its weight. A rope pulling a sled upward at an angle has a horizontal component that helps move the sled and a vertical component that reduces how strongly the sled presses against the ground.

Consider a technician pulling a heavy equipment case with a 120 N rope at 40° above the horizontal. The horizontal component is 120 cos(40°) ≈ 91.9 N forward. The vertical component is 120 sin(40°) ≈ 77.1 N upward. These values do not mean the case automatically accelerates forward or rises. Friction, gravity, and other forces must also be considered.

In engineering, components help separate one complicated situation into two simpler questions: What is happening horizontally? What is happening vertically? That is exactly the approach used in free-body diagrams in Lesson A4.4.

SECTION 10

Independent engineering practice

Work through these problems before checking the solutions. Sketch a force arrow whenever the direction is difficult to picture. Round trig answers to one decimal place unless the problem asks otherwise.

Problem A · Direction and net force

A 65 N force acts right and a 95 N force acts left. Find the horizontal net force.

Show solution

ΣFx = +65 − 95 = −30 N, so the result is 30 N left.

Problem B · Horizontal and vertical components

A cable pulls with 80 N at 60° above the positive horizontal. Find Fx and Fy.

Show solution

Fx = 80 cos(60°) = 40.0 N right. Fy = 80 sin(60°) ≈ 69.3 N up.

Problem C · A force pointing up and left

A 70 N force points 45° above the leftward horizontal. Find its signed components.

Show solution

Fx = −70 cos(45°) ≈ −49.5 N. Fy = +70 sin(45°) ≈ +49.5 N.

Problem D · Resultant magnitude

The combined components on a machine are ΣFx = +30 N and ΣFy = +40 N. Find the resultant magnitude and general direction.

Show solution

FR = √(30² + 40²) = 50 N. It points up and right; the angle is tan−1(40/30) ≈ 53.1° above +x.

Problem E · Explain the engineering meaning

Why does an engineer need both horizontal and vertical components when analyzing an angled lifting cable?

Show explanation

The horizontal component pulls sideways, while the vertical component pulls upward. Each affects the load differently, and both must be considered along with other forces to predict motion or equilibrium.

VOCABULARY & GLOSSARY

Vocabulary and glossary

Scalar

A quantity with magnitude only, such as mass.

Vector

A quantity with magnitude and direction, such as force.

Magnitude

The size of a vector, regardless of its direction.

Component

The part of a vector acting along a chosen axis.

Horizontal component (Fx)

The part of a force that acts left or right.

Vertical component (Fy)

The part of a force that acts up or down.

Resultant force

The single force equivalent to the combined forces acting on an object.

Net force

The vector sum of all external forces on an object.

Σ (sigma)

The mathematical symbol for a sum.

Lesson summary

Forces are vectors, so direction matters. In one dimension, combine signed forces to find net force. For angled forces, break the arrow into horizontal and vertical components using cosine and sine when the angle is measured from the horizontal. Add components separately, then use the Pythagorean theorem if you need the resultant magnitude. These steps will help you construct and analyze free-body diagrams in Lesson A4.4.
