Semester objective A-O4: Analyze forces and motion using Newton's laws, vectors, and free-body diagrams.

Unit essential question: How do forces determine the motion or equilibrium of mechanical systems?

Learning target: Represent forces as vectors and calculate horizontal and vertical components of angled forces.

Objectives: Distinguish scalar and vector quantities; interpret arrow direction and magnitude; add one-dimensional forces; resolve angled forces into x and y components; use component signs; and calculate the magnitude and direction of a resultant force.

LESSON A4.4

Why engineers draw free-body diagrams

A free-body diagram (FBD) is a simple drawing that shows every external force acting on one chosen object. Engineers use a box or dot to represent the object and labeled arrows to represent forces. This helps them see whether forces balance or cause acceleration. Imagine a heavy motor on a platform: gravity pulls down, while the platform pushes up. Before calculating, the engineer must identify both forces.

LESSON A4.4

Identify the object and its forces

Start by choosing the object you want to study. If you choose a crate, draw only forces acting on that crate. Common forces include weight (gravity pulling down), normal force (a surface pushing perpendicular to itself), tension (a rope pulling), friction (resistance to sliding or attempted sliding), and applied pushes or pulls. Velocity and acceleration are not forces.

LESSON A4.4

The five-step free-body diagram method

First, choose one object. Second, replace it with a simple shape. Third, identify everything interacting with it. Fourth, draw and label an arrow for each external force. Fifth, choose x and y axes and calculate force sums. Use ΣFx = max and ΣFy = may. When acceleration is zero along an axis, the net force along that axis is zero.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A crate resting on a level floor

A 20 kg crate rests on a level floor. Its weight is 20 × 9.8 = 196 N downward. The floor pushes upward with a normal force of 196 N, so the vertical forces balance and acceleration is zero.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A crate being pushed

A 25 kg crate is pushed 120 N right. Friction acts 45 N left. Weight acts down and the floor's normal force acts up. The horizontal forces are unbalanced, while the vertical forces balance.

Find acceleration

  1. Horizontal net force = 120 − 45 = 75 N right.
  2. Newton's second law: a = Fnet/m = 75/25.
  3. Acceleration = 3.0 m/s² right.

Always use net force in Newton's second law, not just the applied push.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A motor hanging from a cable

A vertical cable pulls upward on a 15 kg motor. Gravity pulls downward. If the motor hangs still, tension and weight balance.

Calculate cable tension

  1. Weight = 15 × 9.8 = 147 N down.
  2. Vertical balance: T − W = 0.
  3. T = 147 N upward.

If the motor accelerates upward at 2 m/s², then T − 147 = 15 × 2, giving T = 177 N.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A case pulled by an angled rope

A worker pulls a 10 kg case with a 50 N rope at 30° above horizontal. The rope pulls right and up. Gravity pulls down, and the floor pushes up.

Find the normal force

  1. Weight = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N down.
  2. Rope's horizontal component = 50 cos(30°) ≈ 43.3 N right.
  3. Rope's upward component = 50 sin(30°) = 25 N up.
  4. Vertical balance: N + 25 − 98 = 0.
  5. N = 73 N up.

We cannot find horizontal acceleration unless we know friction. The normal force is smaller than weight because the rope also pulls upward.

ENGINEERING APPLICATION

Common FBD mistakes

Students sometimes draw a force in the direction of motion just because an object is moving. But velocity is not a force. A box can slide right while friction points left and slows it down. Only actual interactions belong on the diagram.

Another mistake is putting both forces from a Newton's third-law pair on the same diagram. If a crate pushes down on a floor, that force acts on the floor, not the crate. A crate's FBD includes the floor's upward normal force instead.

Finally, do not assume the normal force always equals weight. An angled rope can pull upward and reduce the normal force. On a ramp, the normal force is perpendicular to the slope, not straight up.

SECTION 10

Independent engineering practice

Work through these problems before checking the solutions. Sketch a force arrow whenever the direction is difficult to picture. Round trig answers to one decimal place unless the problem asks otherwise.

Problem A · Direction and net force

A 65 N force acts right and a 95 N force acts left. Find the horizontal net force.

Show solution

ΣFx = +65 − 95 = −30 N, so the result is 30 N left.

Problem B · Horizontal and vertical components

A cable pulls with 80 N at 60° above the positive horizontal. Find Fx and Fy.

Show solution

Fx = 80 cos(60°) = 40.0 N right. Fy = 80 sin(60°) ≈ 69.3 N up.

Problem C · A force pointing up and left

A 70 N force points 45° above the leftward horizontal. Find its signed components.

Show solution

Fx = −70 cos(45°) ≈ −49.5 N. Fy = +70 sin(45°) ≈ +49.5 N.

Problem D · Resultant magnitude

The combined components on a machine are ΣFx = +30 N and ΣFy = +40 N. Find the resultant magnitude and general direction.

Show solution

FR = √(30² + 40²) = 50 N. It points up and right; the angle is tan−1(40/30) ≈ 53.1° above +x.

Problem E · Explain the engineering meaning

Why does an engineer need both horizontal and vertical components when analyzing an angled lifting cable?

Show explanation

The horizontal component pulls sideways, while the vertical component pulls upward. Each affects the load differently, and both must be considered along with other forces to predict motion or equilibrium.

VOCABULARY & GLOSSARY

Vocabulary and glossary

Scalar

A quantity with magnitude only, such as mass.

Vector

A quantity with magnitude and direction, such as force.

Magnitude

The size of a vector, regardless of its direction.

Component

The part of a vector acting along a chosen axis.

Horizontal component (Fx)

The part of a force that acts left or right.

Vertical component (Fy)

The part of a force that acts up or down.

Resultant force

The single force equivalent to the combined forces acting on an object.

Net force

The vector sum of all external forces on an object.

Σ (sigma)

The mathematical symbol for a sum.

QUESTION 1 OF 10

Lesson summary

Forces are vectors, so direction matters. In one dimension, combine signed forces to find net force. For angled forces, break the arrow into horizontal and vertical components using cosine and sine when the angle is measured from the horizontal. Add components separately, then use the Pythagorean theorem if you need the resultant magnitude. These steps will help you construct and analyze free-body diagrams in Lesson A4.4.
