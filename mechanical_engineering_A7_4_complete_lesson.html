Section 01

A collision is an engineering problem

Imagine two shopping carts rolling toward one another. When they hit, each cart's motion changes. Now imagine the same event inside a factory, where automated carts carry heavy machine parts. An unexpected collision could damage the equipment, spill a load, or injure a worker. Mechanical engineers need ways to predict what happens when moving objects interact.

This lesson introduces momentum, a measurement that combines an object's mass and velocity. We will use it to predict the motion of carts after a collision. We will also explain what our calculations do not tell us, because designing a real machine requires more than getting the arithmetic right.

Words to know before we begin

A cart is a wheeled platform. A test track is a straight path used for experiments. A collision occurs when two objects strike one another and exert forces during contact. A payload is the material a machine carries. A motion sensor measures where something is or how fast it moves.

Section 02

Review mass, velocity, and direction

Mass describes how much matter an object contains. We measure it in kilograms, abbreviated kg. A loaded cart generally has more mass than the same cart when empty. Mass is not the same as weight: weight is a force caused by gravity, while mass is the quantity we use in the momentum equation.

Velocity describes both how fast something moves and which direction it moves. It is measured in meters per second, or m/s. A cart moving east at 4 m/s has velocity +4 m/s if we choose east as positive. A cart moving west at the same speed has velocity −4 m/s. The negative sign describes direction, not a smaller amount of motion.

Before solving a collision problem, draw a simple sketch and choose a positive direction. This prevents the most common mistake: adding two velocities that actually point in opposite directions.

Section 03

What momentum means

Think about trying to stop a rolling empty shopping cart. Now imagine stopping a cart carrying several heavy boxes at the same speed. The loaded cart is harder to bring to rest. Next, imagine pushing the same loaded cart much faster. It becomes harder to stop again. Momentum captures both observations: it increases with mass and with speed.

In engineering, linear momentum is the product of mass and signed velocity. The symbol p stands for momentum, m for mass, and v for velocity.

Momentum = mass × velocity   →   p = mv

The unit is kg·m/s, read “kilogram meters per second.” It comes from multiplying kilograms by meters per second. Momentum is not measured in newtons (N), because newtons measure force. A cart at rest has velocity zero, so its momentum is zero even if it is very heavy.

One-sentence check:

If two carts move at the same speed in the same direction, the more massive cart has more momentum. If two equally massive carts move in opposite directions at the same speed, their momenta have equal magnitudes but opposite signs.

Section 04

Calculate momentum one step at a time

Worked example 1: A 500 kg test vehicle travels east at 6 m/s. What is its momentum? Step 1: Choose east as positive. Step 2: Record mass m = 500 kg and velocity v = +6 m/s. Step 3: Multiply p = (500)(+6) = +3,000 kg·m/s. Step 4: Explain the sign: the momentum points east.

Worked example 2: A 750 kg factory vehicle travels west at 4 m/s. East is positive. Its velocity is −4 m/s, so p = (750)(−4) = −3,000 kg·m/s. The negative result means westward momentum; its magnitude is 3,000 kg·m/s.

Worked example 3: A 20 kg cart is stationary. Its momentum is (20)(0) = 0 kg·m/s. It still has mass, but it has no momentum while it is at rest.

Common error: Don't add mass and velocity. They represent different measurements and must be multiplied. Don't replace kg·m/s with N; those units describe different physical quantities.

Section 05

What happens during a collision?

When two carts collide, they push on each other for a short period. One cart may slow down, speed up, stop, or reverse. Sometimes the carts bounce apart. Sometimes they become attached and continue moving as a single object. These are different collision outcomes, and the engineer must identify which one the problem describes.

For the collision calculations in this lesson, we will use a nearly frictionless horizontal track. “Frictionless” is an idealized way of saying that outside forces from the track are small enough to ignore during the brief collision. A system means the collection of objects we are studying—in our case, both carts together.

When the total external impulse on that two-cart system is negligible, total linear momentum is conserved. This means that the signed momentum of both carts added together before the collision equals their signed momentum added together afterward. Individual cart momenta can change; it is the combined total that stays the same.

Total momentum before = total momentum after

This is a model of what happens during the short impact, not a claim that friction and outside forces never exist. A real engineering test must check whether the approximation is reasonable.

Section 06

Two carts stick together: the basic equation

When two carts stick together, engineers call the event a perfectly inelastic collision. The phrase sounds complicated, but the important part is simple: after the impact, the two carts move together at one shared final velocity.

Before impact, Cart 1 has momentum m₁v₁ and Cart 2 has momentum m₂v₂. The small numbers 1 and 2 tell us which cart we mean. After impact, the combined mass is m₁ + m₂, and both carts share velocity vf.

m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁ + m₂)vf

To find the final velocity, divide both sides by the combined mass:

vf = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂) ÷ (m₁ + m₂)

Read the formula in words: Add the signed momenta of both carts before impact. Then divide by their combined mass. The answer is the signed velocity of the joined carts. This is not the same as simply averaging their two speeds, because a heavier cart contributes more momentum.

Section 07

Worked collision: one cart is stationary

Worked example 4: A 6 kg cart moves east at 4 m/s. It hits a stationary 2 kg cart, and they stick together. What happens next?

Step 1: Choose east as positive. Step 2: Write m₁ = 6 kg, v₁ = +4 m/s, m₂ = 2 kg, v₂ = 0 m/s. Step 3: Find total momentum before: (6)(4) + (2)(0) = +24 kg·m/s. Step 4: Add masses: 6 + 2 = 8 kg. Step 5: Divide: vf = 24 ÷ 8 = +3 m/s.

Engineering interpretation: The joined carts continue east, but at a lower speed than the original moving cart. The original momentum is now shared by a larger total mass. This model helps predict the motion immediately after the impact.

Section 08

Worked collision: both carts move in the same direction

Worked example 5: A 4 kg cart moves east at 5 m/s and catches up with a 2 kg cart moving east at 2 m/s. They latch together. Both velocities are positive because both carts move east.

Step 1: First cart's momentum = (4)(5) = +20 kg·m/s. Step 2: Second cart's momentum = (2)(2) = +4 kg·m/s. Step 3: Add = +24 kg·m/s. Step 4: Combined mass = 4 + 2 = 6 kg. Step 5: Final velocity = 24 ÷ 6 = +4 m/s.

The joined carts move east at a speed between the two original speeds. Notice that we did not simply average 5 and 2. The 4 kg cart has more mass, so it contributes more momentum.

Section 09

Worked collision: opposite directions

Worked example 6: A 5 kg cart moves east at 4 m/s. A 3 kg cart moves west at 2 m/s. They collide and stick. Which way do they move afterward?

Step 1: Choose east as positive. Step 2: Write velocities with signs: +4 m/s and −2 m/s. Step 3: Total momentum before = (5)(+4) + (3)(−2) = 20 − 6 = +14 kg·m/s. Step 4: Combined mass = 5 + 3 = 8 kg. Step 5: Final velocity = 14 ÷ 8 = +1.75 m/s.

The joined carts move east because the total momentum before impact was positive. If the total momentum had been negative, the joined carts would have moved west. If it had been exactly zero, the joined carts would have been stationary immediately after impact.

Why the signs matter:

If you incorrectly changed −2 m/s to +2 m/s, you would predict a larger eastward final velocity. That would be the wrong physical situation. Always decide which direction is positive before multiplying.

Section 10

Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy may not be

You may remember that energy describes a system's capacity to cause change. The kinetic energy of an object is the energy it has because it moves. In a collision where carts stick together, some of the initial kinetic energy is transformed into other forms, including sound, heat, and deformation of the carts.

Worked example 7: Return to the 6 kg cart traveling at 4 m/s that hits a stationary 2 kg cart. Its initial kinetic energy is ½(6)(4²) = 48 joules. After impact, the 8 kg joined carts travel at 3 m/s, so kinetic energy is ½(8)(3²) = 36 joules. The carts' kinetic energy decreased by 12 joules, even though total momentum remained 24 kg·m/s before and after.

That does not mean energy vanished. It changed form. In the idealized two-cart collision, momentum is conserved because the external impulse is negligible. Kinetic energy is not generally conserved when carts stick together. Engineers care about the lost kinetic energy because it can be associated with deformation and damage, but this simplified calculation alone cannot predict exact damage.

Section 12

Engineering decisions: what the model can and cannot do

Mechanical engineers use collision models when developing bumpers, couplings, transfer systems, and equipment that may experience impact. A coupling joins moving parts; a bumper is a component designed to absorb or manage contact. Knowing the shared velocity after two carts latch together helps engineers estimate where the combined load will travel next.

However, a predicted final velocity is not a complete safety assessment. The momentum equation does not tell us the exact peak force, how long the impact lasts, or how much a component will bend. Engineers must also consider impact duration, material deformation, structural strength, and guards that keep people away from moving equipment. We will study stopping force and impulse in the next lesson.

Another important limitation is that our calculation assumes external impulse is negligible during the collision. If a cart hits a fixed wall or strong brakes act during impact, the two-cart system is not isolated in the same way. We must include the outside interaction rather than blindly applying the two-cart equation.

Section 13

Independent practice with guided solutions

Work each problem before opening its solution. Write the direction convention, the given masses and velocities, the equation, and the answer with units. The first questions build confidence; later questions combine more than one idea.

1. A 40 kg cart moves east at 3 m/s. Find its momentum.

Choose east positive. p = mv = 40 × (+3) = +120 kg·m/s.

2. A 25 kg cart moves west at 6 m/s. East is positive. Find signed momentum.

v = −6 m/s. p = 25 × (−6) = −150 kg·m/s.

3. A 10 kg cart is stopped. Find momentum.

p = 10 × 0 = 0 kg·m/s.

4. Which has greater momentum magnitude: 3 kg at 8 m/s or 6 kg at 3 m/s?

First: 3×8 = 24 kg·m/s. Second: 6×3 = 18 kg·m/s. The 3 kg cart has greater momentum magnitude.

5. A 5 kg cart at +6 m/s hits a stationary 5 kg cart. They stick. Find final velocity.

Total p = 5×6 + 5×0 = 30 kg·m/s. Total mass = 10 kg. vf = 30/10 = +3 m/s.

6. A 3 kg cart at +8 m/s sticks to a stationary 1 kg cart. Find final velocity.

p = 3×8 = 24 kg·m/s. Total mass = 4 kg. vf = +6 m/s.

7. A 4 kg cart at +5 m/s sticks to a 2 kg cart at +2 m/s. Find final velocity.

p = 20 + 4 = 24 kg·m/s. Mass = 6 kg. vf = +4 m/s.

8. A 6 kg cart at +4 m/s sticks to a 2 kg cart at −4 m/s. Find final velocity.

p = 24 − 8 = 16 kg·m/s. Mass = 8 kg. vf = +2 m/s.

9. A 3 kg cart at +2 m/s sticks to a 6 kg cart at −4 m/s. Find final velocity.

p = 6 − 24 = −18 kg·m/s. Mass = 9 kg. vf = −2 m/s, meaning west.

10. A 4 kg cart at +3 m/s and a 6 kg cart at −2 m/s stick together. Find final velocity.

p = 12 − 12 = 0. Total mass = 10 kg. vf = 0 m/s.

11. A 9 kg cart at +4 m/s hits a stationary 3 kg cart. They stick. Verify momentum after impact.

Initial p = 9×4 = 36 kg·m/s. Final velocity = 36/12 = 3 m/s. Final p = 12×3 = 36 kg·m/s.

12. Two equal-mass carts collide and stick; one moves +6 m/s and the other is stationary. Predict final velocity without full arithmetic.

The moving cart's momentum is shared by twice the mass, so final velocity is +3 m/s.

13. Explain why kinetic energy can decrease when carts stick even if momentum is conserved.

Momentum is conserved for the isolated two-cart system, but some kinetic energy is converted into deformation, sound, heat, and other forms.

14. A 7 kg cart moves west at 2 m/s. Is its momentum negative if west is positive?

No. If west is chosen as positive, its velocity is +2 m/s and its momentum is +14 kg·m/s. Signs depend on the chosen axis.

15. A 2 kg cart at +5 m/s hits a stationary 8 kg cart and sticks. What is final velocity?

p = 2×5 = 10 kg·m/s. Mass = 10 kg. vf = +1 m/s.

16. A 6 kg cart at +3 m/s and 3 kg cart at −2 m/s stick. Which direction do they move?

Total p = 18 − 6 = +12 kg·m/s, so the joined carts move east (v = 12/9 ≈ +1.33 m/s).

Section 14

Glossary: collision engineering vocabulary

Use these definitions whenever you encounter an unfamiliar term.

Mass

Amount of matter in an object; measured in kilograms (kg).

Velocity

Speed together with direction; measured in meters per second (m/s).

Momentum

Mass multiplied by signed velocity; p = mv, measured in kg·m/s.

Linear momentum

Momentum associated with movement along a line or direction.

Collision

Interaction in which objects exert forces on one another during contact.

System

The objects selected for analysis, such as both carts together.

External force

A force acting on the chosen system from something outside it.

External impulse

The effect of an external force acting over time, changing system momentum.

Conservation of momentum

Total momentum stays constant when net external impulse is negligible.

Perfectly inelastic collision

A collision in which objects stick together and share a final velocity.

Signed velocity

Velocity with a positive or negative sign to represent direction.

Combined mass

The sum of the masses of objects moving together after collision.

Kinetic energy

Energy of motion; KE = ½mv², measured in joules (J).

Deformation

Change in shape of an object caused by forces.

Bumper

Part designed to manage contact or absorb energy during impact.

Nearly frictionless track

A track whose frictional effect is small enough to ignore for the modeled interval.

Payload

Load carried by a vehicle or machine.

Motion sensor

Device that measures motion-related quantities such as position or velocity.

Section 16

Lesson summary and next steps

Momentum combines mass and velocity: p = mv. Because velocity includes direction, momentum does too. When two carts collide on a nearly frictionless track and external impulse is negligible, the total momentum of the two-cart system stays the same. If the carts stick together, their shared velocity is the total signed momentum before impact divided by the combined mass.

The engineering value of this model is that it predicts the motion of coupled carts after impact. But predicting final velocity is only the beginning of impact design. In A7.5, we will learn about impulse and average stopping force—including why extending the time of a collision can reduce the average force on a vehicle or passenger.
