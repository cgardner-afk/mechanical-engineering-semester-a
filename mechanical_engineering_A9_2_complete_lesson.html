Section 01

A design is more than a motor

Suppose a powered cart must move a heavy load across a warehouse floor and then lift it onto a platform. A designer cannot simply choose the motor with the largest advertised power. The cart must accelerate at an acceptable rate, overcome resistance, stay within the machine's force and power capabilities, and operate with an appropriate margin of safety. In this lesson you will use calculations to screen designs before recommending one.

The main idea is that engineering requirements become mathematical checks. A motor may deliver enough force but not enough power at operating speed. A machine may satisfy the calculated minimum but lack adequate capacity for uncertainty or real-world operating conditions. Each question must be answered separately.

Design brief

A powered cart must accelerate from rest while carrying a load. Its motor supplies a maximum forward driving force of 900 N. Rolling and mechanical resistance oppose the motion with 180 N. The required acceleration is at least 1.2 m/s². What is the greatest total moving mass the cart can accelerate at that rate?

Section 02

Forces on a powered cart

A force is a push or pull, measured in newtons (N). The motor provides a forward driving force. Friction, rolling resistance, and other opposing effects can be combined into a resistive force when a problem provides one total value. Because these forces point in opposite directions, subtract resistance from driving force to obtain the net force.

Choose the forward direction as positive. Then Fnet = Fdrive − Fresist. If driving force is greater, the net force points forward and the cart accelerates forward. If the two are equal, net force is zero and the cart has zero acceleration. If resistance exceeds driving force, net force points backward relative to the chosen positive direction.

Worked example 1 — Find net force

Forward driving force = 900 N; backward resistance = 180 N. Subtract: Fnet = 900 − 180 = 720 N forward. The vertical normal force and weight balance on a level floor in this simplified example; they do not contribute to the horizontal net force.

Section 03

Newton's second law and maximum mass

Newton's second law connects net force, mass, and acceleration: Fnet = ma. Here Fnet is in newtons, m is total moving mass in kilograms, and a is acceleration in meters per second squared (m/s²). Acceleration describes how quickly velocity changes, not how fast the cart is already moving.

To solve for mass, divide both sides of Fnet = ma by a: m = Fnet/a. In a design problem with a required minimum acceleration, this gives the maximum total moving mass that can achieve that acceleration under the stated force conditions. Total moving mass includes the cart itself as well as the payload unless the problem explicitly states otherwise.

Worked example 2 — Maximum total moving mass

The motor supplies 900 N forward and resistance is 180 N backward. Required acceleration is 1.2 m/s². First calculate net force: 900 − 180 = 720 N. Next rearrange Newton's law: m = 720/1.2 = 600 kg. This is the greatest total mass for which the simplified model predicts acceleration of at least 1.2 m/s². If the empty cart weighs 140 kg, the maximum payload is 600 − 140 = 460 kg.

Check the logic: increasing total mass while keeping net force fixed reduces acceleration. If the cart and payload together exceed 600 kg, the acceleration falls below the 1.2 m/s² requirement.

Step-by-step worked method · Find maximum moving mass

  1. Step 1: Read drive force 900 N, resistance 180 N, required acceleration 1.2 m/s².
  2. Step 2: Calculate net force: 900 − 180 = 720 N.
  3. Step 3: Rearrange F_net = ma to m_max = F_net/a.
  4. Step 4: Calculate 720/1.2 = 600 kg total moving mass.
  5. Step 5: Subtract empty-cart mass if asked for payload capacity; check operating assumptions.
Section 04

Avoid three common force-calculation errors

First, do not use the driving force as though it were the net force. A 900 N drive opposed by 180 N of resistance produces 720 N net, not 900 N. Second, do not confuse the mass of the payload with the total moving mass. If a question asks for payload capacity, subtract the empty vehicle mass after calculating the total allowable mass. Third, do not confuse acceleration with speed: m/s² and m/s are different units.

Worked example 3 — Does this design meet the requirement?

A cart has a total moving mass of 450 kg, driving force of 800 N, and resistance of 170 N. The required acceleration is 1.5 m/s². Net force = 800 − 170 = 630 N. Actual acceleration = 630/450 = 1.4 m/s². Since 1.4 is less than 1.5, the design fails the acceleration requirement. A motor upgrade, reduced mass, or reduced resistance could improve performance.

A useful way to check your answer is to calculate the required net force directly: Frequired = ma. In this example, 450 × 1.5 = 675 N is required, but only 630 N is available.

Section 05

From force to power: what a motor must deliver

Force alone does not describe everything a motor must do. Power measures the rate of energy transfer or work, measured in watts (W). If a constant force acts in the direction of motion at speed v, the instantaneous mechanical power associated with that force is P = Fv, where F is in newtons and v is in meters per second. This follows from work W = Fd and average speed v = d/t: P = W/t = Fd/t = Fv for constant aligned force and speed over the interval.

Use the appropriate force for the power you are calculating. Motor mechanical output power at the wheels uses the driving force. Net power contributing to changing kinetic energy uses net force, with the difference accounting for energy transferred against resistance. These are not interchangeable. Motor electrical input power may be larger still because the drive system has losses.

Worked example 4 — Motor output power at a specified speed

A cart motor delivers a driving force of 700 N while the cart moves at 2.5 m/s. Mechanical power delivered in the direction of motion is P = Fv = 700 × 2.5 = 1,750 W. If the drive system is 80% efficient, estimated electrical input power is 1,750/0.80 = 2,187.5 W. This calculation assumes the given force and efficiency apply at that operating point.

Real motors have torque-speed characteristics, so a motor's ability to provide a force at one speed does not guarantee that it can provide the same force at every speed. A real design must check its rated operating curve.

Step-by-step worked method · Find motor mechanical output power

  1. Step 1: Read driving force F = 700 N and speed v = 2.5 m/s.
  2. Step 2: Choose P_mechanical = Fv for aligned force and velocity.
  3. Step 3: Calculate 700 × 2.5 = 1,750 W.
  4. Step 4: Check units N·m/s = W.
  5. Step 5: Do not confuse motor mechanical output power with net-force power or electrical input power.
Section 06

Lifting motors: energy, time, and efficiency

A lifting system must give a load gravitational potential energy. Near Earth's surface, the useful lifting energy is E = mgh, where g = 9.8 N/kg and h is vertical height. Average useful power is Puseful = mgh/t. If efficiency is η as a decimal, electrical input power is Pinput = mgh/(tη).

Worked example 5 — Motor rating and deadline

A hoist raises 200 kg by 4 m in 10 seconds at 80% efficiency. Useful energy = 200 × 9.8 × 4 = 7,840 J. Useful output power = 7,840/10 = 784 W. Convert 80% to 0.80. Average electrical input power = 784/0.80 = 980 W.

Suppose the deadline shortens to 5 seconds, with the same load, height, and efficiency. Input power becomes 7,840/(5 × 0.80) = 1,960 W. Halving the time doubles the required average power. A motor rated for only 1,200 W of continuous electrical input would not satisfy the 5-second average input-power demand in this simplified model.

A motor rating is a stated capacity under defined conditions. In practice, engineers distinguish continuous and peak ratings, duty cycle, thermal limits, and starting torque. An average power calculation is an initial screening tool, not complete proof of suitability.

Step-by-step worked method · Find lifting input power and test a deadline

  1. Step 1: Read m = 200 kg, h = 4 m, η = 0.80.
  2. Step 2: Calculate E_useful = 200 × 9.8 × 4 = 7,840 J.
  3. Step 3: At 10 s: P_useful = 784 W and P_input = 784/0.80 = 980 W.
  4. Step 4: At 5 s: P_input = 7,840/(5 × 0.80) = 1,960 W.
  5. Step 5: Compare 1,960 W with a 1,200 W continuous input rating: the shorter deadline fails this simplified power screen.
Section 07

Safety margins and factor of safety

Engineers rarely design safety-critical equipment to operate exactly at a failure threshold. A margin provides room for uncertainty in loads, manufacturing, wear, and operating conditions. A factor of safety (FoS) is commonly expressed as failure capacity divided by expected working load for the same kind of quantity. For a component with a 4,000 N failure load and a 1,000 N working load, FoS = 4,000/1,000 = 4. This is a ratio, so it has no unit.

Do not mix incompatible quantities. If the problem provides a material's yield strength, the comparison is between allowable stress and working stress, not between stress and electrical power. A motor's rated power divided by demanded power is a capacity ratio, but it is not automatically the structural factor of safety of the whole machine. The required safety factor depends on the design, standard, and failure mode.

Worked example 6 — Check a structural load limit

A support component has a specified failure load of 6,000 N and the expected working load is 2,000 N. FoS = 6,000/2,000 = 3. If the design brief requires a factor of safety of at least 2.5 for that failure mode, the component meets that numerical criterion. It does not prove that other parts of the machine are safe.

Step-by-step worked method · Calculate factor of safety

  1. Step 1: Read specified component capacity and the matching working load.
  2. Step 2: Use FoS = failure capacity / working load for the same failure mode.
  3. Step 3: Substitute the lesson's force values; keep both in newtons.
  4. Step 4: Compare calculated FoS with the stated minimum.
  5. Step 5: Do not treat a motor power-capacity ratio as the whole machine's structural FoS.
Section 08

Screening motor options under multiple constraints

A good design must meet every mandatory requirement. Suppose a lift must raise a 250 kg load by 4 m in no more than 8 s. The lift's efficiency is 75%, and a motor must supply at least the calculated average electrical input power. Useful energy = 250 × 9.8 × 4 = 9,800 J. Useful power at the deadline = 9,800/8 = 1,225 W. Required electrical input power = 1,225/0.75 ≈ 1,633.3 W.

Motors B and C pass the initial power screen, while A does not. It would be incorrect to conclude that C is automatically the best choice. A designer must consider efficiency at operating load, cost, dimensions, duty cycle, and safety requirements. The smallest acceptable rating might reduce cost, but only after all other constraints are checked.

Step-by-step worked method · Screen motors under hard constraints

  1. Step 1: Calculate E = 250 × 9.8 × 4 = 9,800 J.
  2. Step 2: At 8 s, P_useful = 9,800/8 = 1,225 W.
  3. Step 3: Convert 75% to 0.75; P_input = 1,225/0.75 ≈ 1,633.3 W.
  4. Step 4: Compare A = 1,200 W (fail), B = 1,800 W (pass), C = 2,400 W (pass).
  5. Step 5: Check other mandatory criteria before selecting between B and C.

Section 10

Engineering decision guide and diagram interpretation

When a question provides arrows and labels, read the directions before choosing a formula. Horizontal forces in opposite directions must be combined with signs. If the problem asks for maximum mass at a target acceleration, first find net force and then divide by the acceleration. If it asks for payload mass, subtract the empty cart mass. If it asks for mechanical power at speed, use the driving force times the speed. If it asks for a hoist's average electrical input power, use the lifting-energy formula with time and efficiency.

Worked example 7 — An unfamiliar force diagram

A cart diagram shows a 1,050 N arrow to the right, a 250 N arrow to the left, and a minimum acceleration of 2.0 m/s². The maximum total mass is (1,050 − 250)/2.0 = 400 kg. If the cart itself has a mass of 125 kg, maximum payload is 400 − 125 = 275 kg. The diagram's arrow lengths do not replace the printed numerical values.

Check the units: newtons divided by m/s² give kilograms. Joules divided by seconds give watts. A factor of safety is a ratio and has no units. Unit checks help catch formula mistakes.

Section 11

Independent practice with worked solutions

Solve each problem on paper before opening its explanation. The solutions show the reasoning as well as the final value.

1. A motor drives forward with 750 N against 150 N of resistance. Find net force.

750 − 150 = 600 N forward.

2. A 300 kg cart has 600 N net force. Find acceleration.

a = F/m = 600/300 = 2.0 m/s².

3. A 900 N drive faces 180 N resistance. Required acceleration is 1.2 m/s². Find maximum total mass.

Net force = 720 N; m = 720/1.2 = 600 kg.

4. If the empty cart in problem 3 has mass 140 kg, find maximum payload.

600 − 140 = 460 kg.

5. A cart has mass 400 kg, driving force 950 N, and resistance 150 N. Find acceleration.

Net force = 800 N; a = 800/400 = 2.0 m/s².

6. A motor provides 600 N forward force at 3 m/s. Find mechanical output power.

P = Fv = 600 × 3 = 1,800 W.

7. A 200 kg load rises 5 m in 10 s. Find useful power.

E = 200 × 9.8 × 5 = 9,800 J; useful power = 9,800/10 = 980 W.

8. The lift in problem 7 is 70% efficient. Find electrical input power.

980/0.70 = 1,400 W.

9. The lift in problem 8 must finish in 5 s instead of 10 s. Find electrical input power.

9,800/(5 × 0.70) = 2,800 W.

10. A support fails at 8,000 N and carries 2,000 N working load. Find its factor of safety.

8,000/2,000 = 4.

11. A 500 kg cart must accelerate at 1.5 m/s² with 200 N resistance. What driving force is required?

Required net force = 500 × 1.5 = 750 N. Drive = 750 + 200 = 950 N.

12. A 1,500 W-rated input motor is compared with a calculated 1,800 W average electrical demand. Does it pass this power screen?

No. The estimated demand exceeds the stated input-power rating. Other checks are also needed for a complete design.

Section 12

Glossary: motor, force, and safety vocabulary

Select each aqua term to reveal its definition.

Driving force

The force a motor or propulsion system applies in the intended direction of travel.

Resistive force

A force that opposes motion, such as friction or rolling resistance.

Net force

The vector sum of all forces acting on an object.

Acceleration

The rate of change of velocity, measured in m/s².

Mass

A measure of inertia, expressed in kilograms.

Payload

The carried load, excluding the mass of the empty vehicle when specified.

Newton's second law

Net force equals mass times acceleration: F = ma.

Newton (N)

The SI unit of force; 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

Power

The rate at which energy is transferred or work is done.

Watt (W)

The unit of power, equal to one joule per second.

Mechanical output power

Power delivered mechanically by a motor or drive to its load.

Electrical input power

The rate of electrical energy supplied to a motor.

Efficiency

Useful output divided by input, expressed as a fraction or percentage.

Motor rating

A specified capacity of a motor under stated operating conditions.

Factor of safety

A ratio comparing a defined failure capacity with the corresponding expected working demand.

Design constraint

A mandatory limit that a proposed system must satisfy.

Section 13

Lesson summary and next steps

Engineering calculations help turn requirements into pass-or-fail checks. On a level cart, subtract resistance from driving force to obtain net force, then use Newton's second law to calculate acceleration or maximum mass. For mechanical power at a specified speed, multiply driving force by speed. For a lifting motor, use mgh, time, and efficiency to estimate electrical input power. Evaluate each mandatory requirement separately and do not treat one successful calculation as proof of overall safety.

In A9.3 you will use similar decision-making skills to compare materials and structures, including stress, strength, stability, and factors of safety.
