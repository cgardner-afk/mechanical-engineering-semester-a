Section 01

The material decision is a system decision

A lifting machine has a motor, but it also needs a frame, a beam, fasteners, and supports that safely carry the load. A powerful motor cannot compensate for a weak support. Mechanical engineers compare both material choices and structural configurations because a successful design must meet strength, stiffness, weight, cost, and manufacturing requirements simultaneously.

Imagine a warehouse lift with a horizontal support beam. One design uses a lightweight aluminum member, another uses a heavier steel member, and a third changes the beam geometry. The strongest material is not automatically the best design: a structure can be strong enough yet bend too much, be too expensive, or make the whole machine too heavy.

Design brief

A lifting frame must support a 400 kg load. The designer requires a minimum factor of safety of 2.0 against the specified member failure mode, limits frame mass to 90 kg, and sets a material budget of $1,200. The design team must choose a candidate that meets all three mandatory requirements before considering preferences such as lower weight.

Section 02

Load paths: where the force travels

A load path is the route by which an applied force travels through structural members to supports and ultimately the ground. A suspended load pulls downward on the hook, which transfers force to a beam or cable, then to columns and foundations. If any essential component along that route is inadequate, the system may fail even if other parts are strong.

At rest, a mass m produces a downward weight F = mg, where g ≈ 9.8 N/kg near Earth. A 400 kg load has a weight of 400 × 9.8 = 3,920 N. That is the basic static vertical load in our simplified design. Starting, stopping, swinging, and unexpected impacts can create additional forces, so real equipment requires more complete analysis.

In a symmetrical, centrally loaded frame with two identical supports, an idealized static model predicts each support carries half the vertical load: 3,920/2 = 1,960 N. That division is valid only under the stated symmetry and loading assumptions.

Section 03

Tension, compression, bending, and shear

Tension pulls a member apart, as in a loaded cable. Compression pushes a member together, as in a vertical column. Bending curves a member, often placing one side in tension and the other in compression. Shear tends to slide adjacent sections past each other, as when a pin transmits a sideways force.

Type of loadingTypical componentQuestion to ask
TensionLifting cableCan it withstand the pulling force?
CompressionSupport columnWill it crush or buckle?
BendingHorizontal beamWill it break or deflect too much?
ShearConnection pinCan it resist the transverse force?

A single component can experience more than one type of loading. A beam under a suspended load typically experiences both bending and internal shear. Engineers analyze the relevant failure modes rather than assuming that one number describes the entire structure.

Section 04

Stress: force spread over an area

Stress describes force per unit area. For a member carrying a uniform axial tension or compression load, the average normal stress is σ = F/A. Here σ (the Greek letter sigma) is stress, F is axial force in newtons, and A is cross-sectional area in square meters. The unit N/m² is a pascal (Pa). Because typical engineering stresses are large, they are often reported in megapascals (MPa), where 1 MPa = 1,000,000 Pa.

Worked example 1 — Axial stress

A straight metal rod carries 12,000 N of tension. Its cross-sectional area is 200 mm². First convert area: 200 mm² = 200 × 10⁻⁶ m² = 0.0002 m². Then σ = 12,000/0.0002 = 60,000,000 Pa = 60 MPa.

Remember that area is squared. Since 1 mm = 0.001 m, 1 mm² = (0.001 m)² = 0.000001 m². A common shortcut is that 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa. Thus 12,000 N / 200 mm² also gives 60 MPa.

This equation applies to average axial stress. It is not a complete formula for bending stress or buckling in a long slender column.

Step-by-step worked method · Calculate average axial stress

  1. Step 1: Read force 12,000 N and cross-sectional area 200 mm².
  2. Step 2: Choose σ = F/A for average axial loading.
  3. Step 3: Calculate 12,000/200 = 60 N/mm².
  4. Step 4: Convert directly: 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa, so σ = 60 MPa.
  5. Step 5: Check allowable stress separately; this is not a bending or buckling check.
Section 05

Strength, stiffness, and ductility

Strength describes a material's ability to resist failure under loading. Stiffness describes resistance to elastic deformation—how much a component bends or stretches under a load. A material or structure may be strong but relatively flexible. Ductility describes the ability to deform plastically before breaking. These are different properties and matter in different ways.

For many metals, yield strength is the stress at which permanent deformation begins. Ultimate tensile strength is the maximum engineering tensile stress reached in a standard tensile test. Engineers choose an appropriate limit for the relevant failure mode; yield strength and ultimate strength should not be treated as interchangeable.

Worked example 2 — Yield check

A rod experiences 60 MPa average tensile stress. Its specified yield strength is 180 MPa. A simplified yield-based factor of safety is 180/60 = 3.0. If the project requires at least 2.5 for this failure mode, it passes this numerical check. The calculation does not establish its resistance to fatigue, corrosion, connection failure, or other hazards.

Section 06

Factor of safety and allowable stress

A factor of safety (FoS) compares a specified capacity with the expected demand for the same failure mode. For an axial yield check, FoS = yield strength / working stress. If a design brief specifies the required FoS, you can rearrange to find the allowable stress = yield strength / required FoS. The working stress must not exceed this allowable value in the simplified model.

Worked example 3 — Minimum area

A tension rod must carry 18,000 N. The material has a yield strength of 150 MPa, and the required yield-based FoS is 3. Allowable stress = 150/3 = 50 MPa, or 50 N/mm². From σ = F/A, rearrange to A = F/σ. Minimum area = 18,000/50 = 360 mm². An actual design must select an available size and verify connections and other failure modes.

Notice that the factor of safety is not an extra force to subtract. It changes the permitted stress or required capacity. If a candidate's calculated FoS is below the requirement, it fails that requirement regardless of whether it is the cheapest option.

Step-by-step worked method · Size a rod using allowable stress

  1. Step 1: Read F = 18,000 N, yield strength = 150 MPa, required FoS = 3.
  2. Step 2: Find allowable stress = 150/3 = 50 MPa = 50 N/mm².
  3. Step 3: Rearrange σ = F/A to A_min = F/σ_allow.
  4. Step 4: Calculate A_min = 18,000/50 = 360 mm².
  5. Step 5: Choose a real section at least that large and verify other failure modes.
Section 07

Why beam shape matters

For beams, geometry affects stiffness and bending performance. Moving material farther from a beam's neutral axis often increases resistance to bending without increasing mass in the same proportion. This helps explain the widespread use of I-shaped beams and hollow rectangular sections. The correct choice depends on load direction, lateral stability, manufacturing, and connection details.

Deflection is the displacement of a loaded member from its original shape. A beam may have enough strength to avoid breaking but still deflect too far for machinery alignment or safe operation. For a particular beam configuration, deflection depends on material stiffness, beam geometry, support locations, and loading. A larger cross-sectional area alone does not always mean lower bending deflection.

For this introductory design comparison, you will use provided measured deflection values rather than calculating beam deflection from advanced formulas. A complete beam analysis requires additional geometry and material data.

Step-by-step worked method · Diagnose excessive beam deflection

  1. Step 1: Read the allowable deflection and the measured deflection under the specified load.
  2. Step 2: If measured deflection exceeds the limit, the stiffness criterion fails even if yield FoS passes.
  3. Step 3: Consider changing section depth, stiffness, or support arrangement while preserving other constraints.
  4. Step 4: Change one design variable at a time for a controlled comparison.
  5. Step 5: Retest deflection, mass, strength, and connections before recommending the revision.
Section 08

Compare candidates against all constraints

A design decision should separate mandatory constraints from preferences. A candidate that fails a mandatory strength or deflection requirement should not win simply because it costs less. First eliminate failing options. Then compare feasible options using the project priorities.

Worked example 4 — Three structural candidates

A frame must carry the required load with FoS ≥ 2.0, deflection ≤ 5 mm, frame mass ≤ 90 kg, and cost ≤ $1,200.

OptionFoSDeflectionMassCostResult
A: light section1.84 mm55 kg$850Fails FoS
B: reinforced section2.63 mm82 kg$1,050Passes all
C: heavy section3.52 mm110 kg$1,150Fails mass

Only Option B satisfies all four requirements. Option A is lighter and cheaper but fails the safety requirement. Option C is stronger and stiffer but exceeds the mass limit. This is an example of a design trade-off: improving one performance measure can worsen another.

Step-by-step worked method · Screen structural candidates

  1. Step 1: List requirements: FoS ≥ 2.0, deflection ≤ 5 mm, mass ≤ 90 kg, cost ≤ $1,200.
  2. Step 2: A has FoS 1.8: reject despite its low mass and price.
  3. Step 3: B has FoS 2.6, deflection 3 mm, mass 82 kg, cost $1,050: passes all.
  4. Step 4: C has mass 110 kg: reject despite stronger/stiffer performance.
  5. Step 5: Choose B as the only feasible option; preferences cannot override hard constraints.

Section 10

How to justify a recommendation

Strong engineering conclusions refer to evidence. Instead of writing “Option B is best because it looks sturdy,” explain which constraints it satisfies and what trade-offs remain. A recommendation should include the requirement, numerical evidence, comparison, and any important limitation.

Model recommendation

“I recommend Option B because its factor of safety of 2.6 exceeds the minimum 2.0, its 3 mm deflection is below the 5 mm limit, its 82 kg mass is below the 90 kg cap, and its $1,050 cost is within the $1,200 budget. Option A fails the safety requirement, and Option C exceeds the mass limit. The provided data do not address fatigue, joint design, or dynamic loads, so those must be evaluated before the frame is approved for construction.”

This structure of reasoning is valuable for both the Unit 9 assessment and the cumulative semester examination. If a problem gives a table, read every column; the largest strength value is not necessarily the best overall design.

Section 11

Independent practice and guided solutions

Work each problem before opening the solution.

1. Find the weight of a 250 kg suspended load.

F = mg = 250 × 9.8 = 2,450 N.

2. A 3,000 N axial force acts over 100 mm². Find average stress in MPa.

3,000/100 = 30 N/mm² = 30 MPa.

3. A rod has 240 MPa yield strength and 80 MPa working stress. Find yield-based FoS.

240/80 = 3.

4. A project requires FoS ≥ 2.5. A candidate has FoS 2.2. Does it pass?

No. 2.2 is less than 2.5.

5. Yield strength is 200 MPa and required FoS is 4. Find allowable stress.

200/4 = 50 MPa.

6. An axial member carries 10,000 N with allowable stress 50 N/mm². Find minimum area.

A = F/σ = 10,000/50 = 200 mm².

7. A symmetrical beam supports a central 4,000 N static load with two identical supports. Find each reaction.

4,000/2 = 2,000 N upward at each support, under the stated assumptions.

8. Name the main loading type in a suspended cable.

Tension, because the load pulls along the cable.

9. Name a possible failure mode of a long slender compression column.

Buckling, or sideways instability under compression.

10. A beam meets strength requirements but bends 8 mm when the limit is 5 mm. Does it pass?

No. It fails the stiffness/deflection requirement.

11. A candidate meets safety and deflection limits but costs $1,300 when the budget is $1,200. Is it feasible?

No. A mandatory budget constraint is still a constraint.

12. Why might an I-section outperform a solid bar of similar mass in bending?

Its shape places more material farther from the neutral axis, which can increase bending stiffness for the relevant orientation.

Section 13

Lesson summary

Structural design requires more than choosing a strong material. Identify the load path and the loading types, calculate relevant stresses, compare working demands with allowable limits, and check stiffness, mass, and cost. A factor of safety applies to a defined failure mode; it does not guarantee that the entire machine is safe. Eliminate candidates that fail any mandatory requirement, then justify the preferred feasible design with numerical evidence.

In the next lesson, you will connect structural decisions to motion, energy, and operating hazards across the full mechanical system.

UNIT TEST + SEMESTER FINAL · TARGETED PRACTICE

Exam readiness: stability, density, and structural selection

Engineering exams often give several possible designs and ask for the lightest option that meets every mandatory requirement. The correct sequence is to calculate the working demand, reject any unsafe candidate, and compare the remaining options. The three activities below provide extra practice with the specific structural reasoning needed for the Unit 9 test and semester final.

1. Why a triangle resists sway

A rectangle connected with pin-like joints can change into a parallelogram when pushed sideways without changing the lengths of its four members. This movement is called racking. A diagonal brace divides the rectangle into triangles. Because three fixed-length sides determine a triangle's shape, a properly connected triangulated frame resists that same racking motion. The brace must have adequate capacity and sound connections; simply drawing a diagonal does not guarantee a safe structure.

Decision example · Lightweight but unstable

Frame R weighs 14 kg but sways under a lateral test. Frame T weighs 18 kg and stays within the allowable displacement because it is properly triangulated. If stability is mandatory, Frame R is not feasible, even though it is lighter. Frame T is preferable among these two options, assuming it meets the remaining constraints.

2. Select a material by stress, yield strength, and density

For a tension member, average axial stress is σ = F/A. The minimum yield strength required for a specified yield-based factor of safety is Sy,min = FoSrequired × σ. Compare density only after excluding materials that fail the required yield strength. When geometry and volume are the same, lower density means lower mass because m = ρV.

Worked example · Lowest-density safe material

A tie bar carries 18,000 N across a 120 mm² section. Required FoS against yielding is 2.0. Working stress = 18,000/120 = 150 MPa. Minimum yield strength = 2.0 × 150 = 300 MPa.

Try it independently

Practice A · A 24,000 N load acts on 160 mm². Required FoS is 2.5. Find the minimum yield strength.

Working stress = 24,000/160 = 150 MPa. Required yield strength = 2.5 × 150 = 375 MPa.

Practice B · Candidate materials have (density, yield strength): X (2.8, 300 MPa), Y (4.5, 420 MPa), Z (7.7, 600 MPa). Use Practice A's requirement. Which has the lowest density while meeting strength?

X fails because 300 MPa is below 375 MPa. Y and Z pass. Y has the lowest density among passing candidates.

Practice C · A rectangular frame is 20% lighter than a triangulated alternative, but it sways beyond the permitted limit. Which should be chosen?

The rectangular frame fails a mandatory stability constraint. Select the triangulated frame if it meets the other requirements, or redesign and retest.

Practice D · A beam has FoS 2.6, costs $95, and weighs 8 kg. The constraints are FoS ≥ 2 and cost ≤ $90. Is it eligible for lowest-mass selection?

No. Its $95 cost exceeds the $90 limit, regardless of its favorable mass and safety factor.
