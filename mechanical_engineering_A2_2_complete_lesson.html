Learning target

Distinguish criteria from constraints, translate goals into measurable requirements, screen candidates against simultaneous limits, and justify trade-offs without compromising mandatory safety requirements.

Essential question: How can engineers tell whether a design is acceptable—and which acceptable design is best?

SECTION 01

The Problem: A Good Design Must Meet More Than One Goal

Imagine that a school needs a portable equipment cart. The cart must carry a heavy box, fit through narrow doors, and be affordable to build. Staff would also prefer a cart that is easy to push, light enough to store, and quiet in the hallway. These are not all the same kind of requirement. Some describe conditions the cart absolutely must satisfy; others describe qualities that make an acceptable cart better.

Engineers cannot select a design simply because it performs well in one area. A cart that rolls effortlessly but cannot fit through the doorway is unusable. A lightweight lifting device that fails a required safety test is unacceptable even if it is inexpensive. The design team needs a method for identifying mandatory limits, describing desired performance, measuring results, and comparing alternatives only after determining which are feasible.

This lesson develops that method. You will learn to distinguish criteria from constraints, write requirements that can be tested, screen candidate designs against multiple limits, and make defensible trade-offs without sacrificing safety.

SECTION 02

Criteria and Constraints: Two Different Questions

A design criterion describes a quality used to judge how well a design meets a need. Examples include low operating effort, low energy consumption, durability, quiet operation, and ease of maintenance. A criterion may be expressed as a goal to maximize or minimize. For the equipment cart, reducing the force needed to push it is a criterion: among acceptable carts, lower push force is desirable.

A constraint is a limit or condition that a design must satisfy to be acceptable. Constraints may come from safety requirements, physical dimensions, budget, regulations, available materials, or operating conditions. If the cart must fit through a doorway that is 760 mm wide, its overall width must be less than the available clearance. If the project has a $150 maximum manufacturing cost, a $170 cart violates the budget constraint even if it performs beautifully.

The difference is often expressed by the words used in a requirement. Statements such as “must,” “shall,” “at least,” and “no more than” frequently signal a mandatory limit. Statements such as “prefer,” “minimize,” and “as quiet as possible” often signal a criterion to optimize. Wording alone is not enough, however: engineers must ask the stakeholder whether a particular target is mandatory. A minimum carrying capacity may be written as a performance criterion in a general discussion, but when the team establishes it as a required threshold, it functions as a hard acceptance limit.

Notice that a design can satisfy every constraint without being the best available choice. Passing the required limits establishes feasibility; criteria help distinguish among feasible solutions.

SECTION 03

Writing Requirements That Engineers Can Actually Test

Words such as strong, comfortable, safe, and quiet are useful when discussing a problem, but they are too vague to verify on their own. Different people may interpret them differently. Engineers turn these wishes into measurable requirements by specifying a quantity, an acceptable threshold, and the conditions under which the measurement is made.

Consider the request “The cart should be easy to push.” A more testable version is: “With a 150 kg load on a level concrete floor, the horizontal force required to start the cart moving shall not exceed 55 N.” This sentence identifies the quantity (starting force), the threshold (55 newtons), and the test condition (specified load and floor). An engineer can now select a force gauge, carry out a repeatable test, and determine whether the cart passes.

Similarly, “The fan should be quiet” might become “At the required airflow setting, the fan shall produce no more than 48 dBA measured 1 m from the unit under the specified test conditions.” The exact number and measurement procedure must come from the project's needs, not from guessing. A requirement for a lifting bracket might specify the supported load and acceptable deformation at a particular test condition. Numbers without units or test conditions can be misleading; 55 could mean newtons, pounds-force, or something else entirely.

Some requirements are evaluated by inspection rather than by an instrument. A folded ramp might have to fit inside a specified storage envelope, verified by measuring its dimensions. A guard might need to prevent access to moving gears, verified by an appropriate safety inspection or test. In every case, the requirement should make it possible for different evaluators to reach the same decision using the same evidence.

SECTION 04

Feasibility: Every Mandatory Limit Counts

A design is feasible only if it meets all mandatory requirements under the relevant conditions. Engineers cannot average a failed requirement together with several successful ones. If a cart meets the load requirement but is too wide, it is not feasible for the intended doorway. If a lift meets the budget and size limits but fails a safety test, it is not an acceptable design.

Suppose three cart concepts must carry at least 180 kg, measure no more than 720 mm wide, and cost no more than $150. Candidate A supports 200 kg, measures 700 mm wide, and costs $140; it passes all three. Candidate B supports 240 kg and costs only $125, but measures 745 mm wide; it fails the width constraint. Candidate C fits the doorway and costs $130, but supports only 165 kg; it fails the load constraint. The extra capacity of B and the low price of C do not compensate for their failed limits.

CandidateCapacity (≥180 kg)Width (≤720 mm)Cost (≤$150)Feasible?

Engineers often create a compliance table like this before comparing preferences. The first decision is whether a candidate qualifies at all. Only then does it make sense to rank feasible candidates according to criteria such as mass, efficiency, comfort, or maintenance cost.

SECTION 06

Trade-Offs: Choosing Among Designs That Qualify

A trade-off occurs when improving one characteristic makes another characteristic worse, or when competing goals cannot all be optimized at the same time. Increasing the thickness of a bracket may improve stiffness but add mass and cost. Choosing a larger motor may increase available torque but consume more energy. Engineers investigate these relationships because the best solution is rarely the one with the highest value in every category.

Imagine two carts that both meet every mandatory requirement. Cart D costs $135 and requires 28 N to start moving; Cart E costs $148 and requires only 18 N. If ease of pushing is the project's highest priority and both are within budget, Cart E is preferable even though it costs more. If the project instead prioritizes keeping money available for replacement parts, Cart D may be preferable. The decision depends on the stated priorities, not on a universal rule that cheaper or lighter is always better.

Trade-off decisions should be supported by comparable measurements. Comparing one cart's push force on a smooth floor with another's force on carpet would be unfair. Engineers also distinguish a preference from a hard constraint: if Cart E costs $155 when the limit is $150, it must be rejected or redesigned, regardless of how easy it is to push. A formal weighted decision matrix can help compare multiple criteria; you will study that tool in Lesson A2.3. Here the essential principle is to screen for feasibility first and compare priorities second.

SECTION 07

Safety Requirements Are Not Optional Trade-Offs

Safety deserves special attention because an attractive performance benefit does not justify ignoring a mandatory safety limit. Consider a lightweight step ladder that meets its mass goal but fails a required load test. The ladder cannot be approved simply because customers value low weight. The team must investigate the failure, revise the design, and verify that the safety requirement is met. The same reasoning applies to a stroller with an unreliable wheel lock or a lifting hook that exceeds an allowable stress limit.

Engineers sometimes use the word optimize to describe finding the best balance of competing objectives. Optimization happens within the feasible design space. The design team may seek the lightest safe ladder, the quietest compliant fan, or the least expensive lifting mechanism that meets all required loads. A failed safety requirement is a reason to redesign or reject a candidate, not a negative point that can be offset by lower cost.

Responsible engineers document the results that support a decision, including failed tests and uncertainty. If a prototype has not been tested under the required conditions, the team should not claim that it has passed. This connects design requirements with the verification and professional responsibility ideas you encountered in earlier lessons.

SECTION 08

Worked Decision: Selecting a Portable Equipment Cart

A facilities team asks for a cart that can carry at least 180 kg, fit through a 720 mm opening, and be built for $150 or less. The team also wants a cart that is easy to push. After preliminary testing under comparable conditions, it has three candidates. Cart R has a capacity of 190 kg, a width of 700 mm, a cost of $135, and a starting push force of 32 N. Cart S has a capacity of 210 kg, a width of 710 mm, a cost of $148, and a starting push force of 20 N. Cart T has a capacity of 230 kg, a width of 735 mm, a cost of $125, and a starting push force of 16 N.

Step 1 — List requirements

First, separate mandatory limits from desired qualities. The cart must support at least 180 kg, be no wider than 720 mm, and cost no more than $150. These three thresholds are constraints: a candidate must meet all of them to qualify. The team also wants to minimize the force needed to start the cart moving. That is a criterion used to compare candidates that qualify. Making this distinction before examining the results prevents the team from treating an attractive feature as compensation for a failed limit.

Step 2 — Measure designs

Next, gather comparable evidence for each requirement. The team records load capacity in kilograms, overall width in millimeters, build cost in dollars, and starting push force in newtons. Push-force tests should use the same load and floor conditions for all three carts. Otherwise, a lower measured force might reflect an easier test rather than a better design. The values below represent the team's preliminary measurements and cost estimates.

R190 kg700 mm$13532 N
S210 kg710 mm$14820 N
T230 kg735 mm$12516 N

Step 3 — Screen constraints

Now compare each candidate against every mandatory threshold. R and S both meet the capacity, width, and cost limits. T meets the capacity and cost limits but is 15 mm wider than allowed, so it is not feasible. Its excellent 16 N push-force result does not erase the width failure. T must be redesigned or rejected before it can compete with the feasible designs.

Step 4 — Compare criteria

Only after the constraint screening does the team compare the feasible candidates using its stated priorities. Both R and S qualify, so the team can now ask which one better meets its preference for easy pushing. S requires 20 N to start moving, compared with 32 N for R. The difference is 32 − 20 = 12 N: S needs 12 N less starting force. However, S costs $148 rather than $135, so choosing it adds $148 − $135 = $13 to the build cost. This is a genuine trade-off: S is easier to push but more expensive.

If ease of pushing is the highest-priority criterion, S is the better choice because it meets every constraint and performs better on that criterion. If minimizing cost is the higher priority, R may be preferable. Engineers should not pretend that one design is universally best when the decision depends on project priorities. They should explain which criterion was prioritized, compare the relevant measurements, and acknowledge the disadvantages of the selected design. In this case, with ease of pushing explicitly prioritized, the team recommends Cart S.

SECTION 09

Glossary and Key Ideas

Criterion: A characteristic used to evaluate or rank a design, such as ease of pushing or energy efficiency.

Constraint: A mandatory limit or condition a design must satisfy.

Requirement: A documented statement describing what a design must do or what limits it must meet.

Specification: A precise statement of a required property or performance target, often including units and test conditions.

Threshold: A boundary value used to determine whether a requirement is satisfied.

Feasibility: Whether a candidate meets all mandatory requirements.

Trade-off: A situation in which improving one design characteristic affects another.

Verification: Checking, using evidence, whether a design meets its stated requirements.

Optimization: Seeking the best performance on selected criteria while respecting constraints.

Safety margin: Additional capacity or separation from a limit intended to account for variation and uncertainty; its adequacy depends on the application.

Before moving to the next lesson, remember the decision sequence: write measurable requirements → test or estimate performance → screen every mandatory limit → compare feasible designs according to priorities → document the reasoning.
