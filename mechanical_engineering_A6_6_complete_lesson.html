Unit A6 integrated learning goals

Apply engineering material properties, axial normal stress, strain and elongation, yield-based safety factors, pin shear stress, and design constraints to a mechanical assembly. Support a recommendation with calculations and an investigation log.

Scope: The challenge provides integrated practice for Unit A6 concepts. It is not a verified item-by-item mapping to the graded assessment or semester final.

Section 01

Your role: mechanical design engineer

A manufacturer is developing a compact lifting fixture. A straight tie link pulls on a guided carriage, and a transverse pin connects the link to a bracket. The assembly must carry its working load without yielding, stretching too far, or exceeding the available budget. A cheaper design may fail a stress check; a stronger material may still permit too much movement if the member is long and narrow.

You are the mechanical engineer responsible for a preliminary design recommendation. You must examine both the tie link and the connection pin. Your decision must be supported by calculations, recorded comparisons, and a clear explanation of what the simplified analysis does not establish. This lesson integrates the material-property and loading concepts from A6.1 through A6.5.

Section 02

Define the design brief and constraints

The fictional assembly carries a steady axial working force of 12,000 N. The link's unsupported original length is 1,000 mm. The maximum acceptable elastic elongation is 0.30 mm. The design requires a minimum yield-based factor of safety of 2.5 for the link, and a minimum shear-based factor of safety of 2.0 for the connection pin. The initial project budget is $85 for the modeled link-and-pin package.

These are classroom design requirements, not a real lifting-equipment specification. The link is treated as straight, uniform, and axially loaded. The pin is treated as an idealized single-shear connection unless the design brief explicitly changes that assumption. In an actual lifting fixture, bending, fatigue, pin bearing, plate tear-out, connection geometry, fabrication quality, inspection, and applicable standards must also be evaluated.

Design acceptance: A candidate must pass every specified check. Passing stress alone does not compensate for excessive extension, an inadequate safety factor, or a budget violation.
Section 03

Translate the problem into a load path

A load path describes how forces travel through an assembly. In this fixture, the carriage applies force to the link; the link transmits axial tension to its end connection; the pin transfers force into the bracket; and the bracket transfers it to the machine frame. Each element must be checked for the loading it actually experiences.

The tie link's axial stress and elastic extension are calculated using its cross-sectional area. The pin's average shear stress uses the pin's circular cross-sectional area and the number of idealized shear planes. Although both components carry the same transmitted force in this simplified model, their stress formulas and failure criteria differ.

Section 04

Check 1: axial stress and yielding

From A6.2, the link's average axial stress is σ = F/A. For a specified yield strength Sy, the calculated yield-based factor of safety is nactual = Sy/σ. To satisfy a required factor nreq, the working stress must not exceed σallow = Sy/nreq.

Worked example 1: A candidate steel link has area 200 mm² and specified yield strength 300 MPa. Its working stress is 12,000/200 = 60 MPa. Its yield-based factor is 300/60 = 5.0. Because 5.0 exceeds the required 2.5, it passes this simplified yield check. That result says nothing yet about extension or pin performance.

Section 05

Check 2: elastic strain and elongation

From A6.3, the engineering strain in the initial linear elastic region is ε = σ/E, and the predicted extension of a uniform axially loaded link is ΔL = FL/(AE). Use N, mm, mm², and MPa consistently so the result is in millimeters.

Worked example 2: The 200 mm² steel link carries 12,000 N, has length 1,000 mm, and has E = 200,000 MPa. The strain is 60/200,000 = 0.00030. Its extension is 0.00030 × 1,000 = 0.30 mm. It meets the specified 0.30 mm limit exactly in the simplified model.

Suppose the link instead had the same area and length but E = 70,000 MPa. Its predicted extension would be 12,000 × 1,000/(200 × 70,000) ≈ 0.857 mm. A material may satisfy a strength requirement yet fail the movement requirement. Engineers must check both.

Section 06

Check 3: shear stress in the connection pin

From A6.5, the pin's circular area is Apin = πd²/4. In an idealized single-shear joint, the average shear stress is τ = F/Apin. In an idealized equally shared double-shear joint, τ = F/(2Apin). The pin's simplified shear factor is nshear = Sshear/τ, where Sshear is the specified reference shear strength for this teaching problem.

Worked example 3: A 16 mm diameter pin in single shear carries 12,000 N. Its area is π(16²)/4 ≈ 201.06 mm². Its average shear stress is approximately 59.7 MPa. If the reference shear strength is 180 MPa, its calculated shear factor is 180/59.7 ≈ 3.02, exceeding the required 2.0.

Do not confuse the link's tensile yield strength with the pin's reference shear strength. They are different criteria and may use different material data.

Section 07

Check 4: derive minimum dimensions

Before choosing a candidate, calculate the minimum link area required by each independent criterion. For yield-based strength, Astrength ≥ Fnreq/Sy. For axial extension, Astiffness ≥ FL/(EΔLmax). The link must satisfy the larger of these two minimum areas.

Worked example 4: For the 12,000 N steel link, n = 2.5, Sy = 300 MPa, E = 200,000 MPa, L = 1,000 mm, and ΔLmax = 0.30 mm. The strength requirement gives 12,000 × 2.5/300 = 100 mm². The stiffness requirement gives 12,000 × 1,000/(200,000 × 0.30) = 200 mm². Therefore stiffness governs this preliminary link sizing.

For a single-shear pin, the theoretical minimum diameter is dmin = √(4Fnshear,req/(πSshear)). With F = 12,000 N, n = 2.0, and reference shear strength = 180 MPa, dmin ≈ 13.03 mm. Select an available diameter at or above this minimum, then check the rest of the joint.

Section 08

Compare material properties rather than labels

Engineers cannot conclude that one material is the best choice simply because it is steel, aluminum, or a polymer. A design comparison requires specific material properties, dimensions, and operating conditions. A material with a high elastic modulus may reduce deflection but increase weight; a high-strength material may permit a smaller cross section but still have inadequate stiffness. The cheapest material per kilogram may not produce the least expensive finished component.

In the interactive investigation, the three link-and-pin packages use illustrative material properties, estimated package masses, and costs. These values are not supplier data. Their purpose is to let students practice transparent engineering decisions. The selected material concept for the link and pin is specified separately for each package, and each package is evaluated under the same working load and design constraints.

Section 10

Worked example 5: screen all three candidates

Use the baseline requirements: F = 12,000 N, L = 1,000 mm, ΔLmax = 0.30 mm, required link yield factor 2.5, required pin shear factor 2.0, and budget $85.

Candidate A: Link stress = 12,000/150 = 80 MPa; link yield factor = 300/80 = 3.75; extension = 12,000 × 1,000/(150 × 200,000) = 0.40 mm. Its 12 mm pin has shear stress ≈ 106.1 MPa and shear factor ≈ 1.70. It fails the extension and pin shear-factor requirements despite meeting the link yield requirement and budget.

Candidate B: Link stress = 12,000/250 = 48 MPa; link yield factor = 6.25; extension = 0.24 mm. Its 16 mm pin has shear stress ≈ 59.7 MPa and shear factor ≈ 3.02. It meets all stated classroom requirements at $72.

Candidate C: Link stress = 12,000/600 = 20 MPa; link yield factor = 12.0; extension = 12,000 × 1,000/(600 × 70,000) ≈ 0.286 mm. Its 16 mm pin has the same simplified shear result as B. It also meets the baseline requirements at $82.

Engineering recommendation: If the objective is the lowest cost among feasible packages, B is preferred. If lower estimated mass is also valuable, C deserves consideration. The choice depends on which constraints and priorities the design brief specifies.

Section 12

Write an evidence-based recommendation

A professional design recommendation should identify the chosen candidate, report the relevant calculations, explain why rejected candidates failed, and identify the model's limitations. For example: “Package B is recommended for the stated baseline load because its calculated link yield factor is 6.25, its predicted extension is 0.24 mm, its idealized pin shear factor is approximately 3.02, and its estimated $72 cost is below the $85 budget. Package A fails the extension and pin checks. This recommendation is preliminary and requires bearing, fatigue, connection geometry, and applicable standards review before manufacture.”

Write your own recommendation after running the laboratory. Include the conditions you tested, a comparison of at least two packages, and one design change that would improve an unacceptable candidate. A design decision without numerical evidence is incomplete.

Section 13

Common engineering errors and how to avoid them

Mixing units: Use N, mm, mm², and MPa consistently. A modulus listed in GPa must be multiplied by 1,000 to obtain MPa. Confusing material properties: Young's modulus describes elastic stiffness, while yield strength is used for a specified plastic-deformation criterion. Neither is the same as pin shear strength.

Checking only one component: A passing link does not imply that its pin passes. Checking only one failure mode: A link can pass a yield-based factor while failing the movement tolerance. Rounding dimensions down: A theoretical minimum area or diameter must not be rounded to a smaller available part. Claiming a safety certification: A simple average-stress model omits real-world failure modes and does not establish fitness for lifting service.

Section 14

Independent engineering practice

Solve each problem before opening the explanation. Use equations, numerical substitution, units, and an engineering conclusion.

1. A link carries 15,000 N with area 250 mm². Find average axial stress.

σ = 15,000/250 = 60 MPa.

2. A link has yield strength 300 MPa and working stress 60 MPa. Find its yield factor.

n = 300/60 = 5.0.

3. A 1,200 mm steel link has F = 10,000 N, A = 200 mm², E = 200,000 MPa. Find elongation.

ΔL = 10,000 × 1,200/(200 × 200,000) = 0.30 mm.

4. A 12 mm pin carries 8,000 N in single shear. Find average shear stress.

A = π(12²)/4 ≈ 113.10 mm²; τ ≈ 70.7 MPa.

5. For problem 4, find ideal double-shear stress.

τ = 8,000/(2 × 113.10) ≈ 35.4 MPa.

6. A material has Sᵧ = 240 MPa and required yield factor 3. Find allowable normal stress.

σallow = 240/3 = 80 MPa.

7. A link carries 16,000 N, L = 1,000 mm, E = 200,000 MPa, and max extension 0.40 mm. Find minimum stiffness area.

Amin = FL/(EΔLmax) = 16,000 × 1,000/(200,000 × 0.40) = 200 mm².

8. A component passes strength but fails the movement tolerance. Can it be selected?

No. The candidate fails the design brief because all required constraints must be satisfied.

9. A design meets all mechanical limits but exceeds the budget. What should the engineer do?

Reject it under the current brief or formally revise the constraints with approval; do not silently ignore cost.

10. A pin passes an average shear check. Name two additional real-world checks.

Examples include bearing, pin bending, fatigue, plate tear-out, fit, wear, and applicable safety standards.

11. Candidate B is cheaper than C but heavier. Which is the best design?

It depends on the specified objective. Both must first satisfy all constraints; then compare cost and mass according to the brief.

12. Why might doubling link area not halve pin stress?

Link area and pin diameter are separate design dimensions. Increasing link area alone does not change the pin's shear area.

VOCABULARY GLOSSARY

Vocabulary glossary

Select a term to expand its definition.

Load path

Route through which forces are transferred across components and into supports.

Normal stress

Axial force divided by the relevant cross-sectional area, σ = F/A.

Engineering strain

Change in length divided by original length, ε = ΔL/L₀.

Young's modulus

Elastic stress-to-strain ratio in the initial linear region, E = σ/ε.

Elastic elongation

Predicted recoverable length change, ΔL = FL/(AE) for a uniform axially loaded member.

Yield strength

Stress associated with a specified onset of plastic deformation.

Factor of safety

Ratio of specified strength to calculated stress for a particular failure mode.

Allowable stress

Stress limit obtained from an applicable design criterion or specified directly.

Direct shear

Loading that tends to slide one portion of a component relative to another along a plane.

Single shear

Idealized joint with one primary shear plane.

Double shear

Idealized joint with two primary shear planes.

Governing constraint

The most restrictive design requirement for a candidate.

Feasible design

Candidate meeting all specified design constraints.

Design trade-off

Choice between competing objectives such as cost, mass, stiffness, and manufacturability.

Design verification

Process of checking whether a design meets specified requirements using appropriate evidence.

Model limitation

Condition or failure mode not represented by a simplified calculation.

A mechanical engineer needs a lightweight tensile support carrying 15,000 N. Its area is 150 mm², so the average working stress is 100 MPa. The specified minimum yield-based factor is 2.0, so a candidate must have yield strength of at least 200 MPa. Only after screening for this requirement should the engineer choose the lowest-density feasible material.

Candidate A is the lightest by density but fails the safety requirement. Candidates B, C, and D pass. These are fictional teaching-property sets, not specifications for procurement.

Why is choosing the lowest density before checking strength incorrect?

The lightest candidate can be mechanically unacceptable. Feasibility screening must precede optimization.

If the area were doubled while the force stayed constant, what would change?

Working stress would fall from 100 MPa to 50 MPa, increasing all yield-based safety factors. Mass would also increase for the same length and material, so strength and weight must be evaluated together.

Worked example: From the first linear points, E = Δσ/Δε = (200 − 100) MPa/(0.0010 − 0.0005) = 200,000 MPa = 200 GPa. The 420 MPa maximum recorded engineering stress is the ultimate tensile strength for this illustrative test. The onset of departure from linearity is not automatically a precise 0.2%-offset yield strength; determining a specified yield strength may require additional data and the correct offset procedure.

Which two data points should be used to estimate initial Young's modulus?

Choose two points within the initial linear elastic region, such as (0.0005, 100 MPa) and (0.0010, 200 MPa).

Why is the largest stress not necessarily the yield strength?

The maximum engineering stress is UTS. Yielding occurs earlier and is defined by an appropriate material/test criterion.

What does the decrease from 420 MPa to 350 MPa imply?

After UTS, the measured engineering stress has declined, which may reflect necking in a ductile tensile specimen; the table alone does not prove fracture has already occurred.

Controlled-variable investigation: three machine tie rods

Mechanical engineers test one design variable at a time to identify cause and effect. A fixture holds three straight rods made from the same steel, each 1,000 mm long and each loaded axially by 12,000 N. The independent variable is cross-sectional area; material, force, length, and test method remain constant. The measured outcomes are average axial stress and predicted elastic elongation. Assume E = 200,000 MPa and a uniform axial load.

TrialAreaForceStress σ = F/AElongation ΔL = FL/(AE)
A100 mm²12,000 N120 MPa0.60 mm

1. Identify the independent, dependent, and controlled variables.

Independent: rod area. Dependent: stress and extension. Controlled: force, material/modulus, original length, and loading arrangement.

2. Predict what happens when area doubles from 100 to 200 mm².

Both average stress and predicted extension are halved, from 120 to 60 MPa and from 0.60 to 0.30 mm.

3. A classmate increases force and area in the same trial. Can the change in stress be attributed only to area?

No. More than one independent variable changed, so this is not a controlled comparison. Hold force fixed to isolate area.

4. How would you investigate the effect of material stiffness instead?

Keep force, area, length, and loading conditions constant, change only material modulus E, and compare extension. Stress F/A would remain unchanged under the stated assumptions.

Engineering application: Controlled comparisons help designers decide whether to increase link area, change materials, or modify geometry to meet a precision movement limit.

Section 17

Unit A6 engineering summary

The engineering design process connects material properties, loading, geometry, and practical constraints. Average normal stress is σ = F/A; engineering strain is ε = ΔL/L₀; in the linear elastic region, σ = Eε; and axial extension for a uniform member is ΔL = FL/(AE). Factors of safety compare working demand with specified strength criteria. Direct shear in an idealized pin is τ = F/(mA), where m is the number of equally loaded shear planes.

Students should now be able to explain why a part may pass one requirement yet fail another, distinguish strength from stiffness, interpret yielding and fracture risks, and support a material-selection decision with calculations. The Unit A6 assessment will require the same habits: identify the correct physical model, use consistent units, calculate carefully, and justify the engineering conclusion.
