SECTION 01

Why Documentation and Redesign Matter

An engineering design is not finished simply because a prototype works once. Teams must be able to explain what they built, how it was tested, what the results showed, and why the next version is an improvement. Documentation is the organized record of those decisions and observations. Redesign is the process of changing a design in response to evidence, requirements, or newly discovered problems.

Return to the equipment cart used in Lesson A2.5. The original cart required an average starting push force of 35 N in three controlled trials. A revised wheel configuration averaged 26 N under the same test conditions. This looks promising, but the team must also check cost, width, load capacity, and stability before recommending the revision. A lower force alone cannot prove the entire cart is better.

Engineers need clear records because projects involve other people: technicians must build the correct version, managers must understand the trade-offs, testers must reproduce the procedure, and future engineers must know why changes were made. Without records, a team may repeat failed experiments or accidentally manufacture an outdated design.

SECTION 02

What Belongs in an Engineering Design Record?

A useful design record is dated, specific, traceable, and honest. It identifies the design version, the reason for a change, the drawing or specification that was updated, the test conditions, the raw measurements, the calculations, and the conclusion. A short statement such as “New wheels work better” is weak documentation because it does not define “better” or show supporting evidence.

Consider a stronger entry: “Revision B, October 15. Replaced the original wheel configuration. Both versions were tested on the same level floor with a 15 kg load using the same force gauge and procedure. Revision A readings: 34, 36, 35 N. Revision B readings: 25, 27, 26 N. Mean force fell from 35 N to 26 N. Width, load capacity, cost, and stability still require verification.” This record allows another engineer to understand the claim and its limits.

A revision identifier such as A, B, or C helps teams distinguish versions. A change log summarizes what changed and why. Drawings and specifications should carry matching revision information so a technician does not accidentally build Revision A while reading instructions intended for Revision B.

SECTION 03

Intellectual Property in Engineering

Engineers often develop original devices, drawings, software, product names, and manufacturing methods. Intellectual property (IP) refers to certain legally protected creations and identifying information. Different kinds of protection apply to different things; they are not interchangeable.

A patent can protect a qualifying invention for a limited period, subject to legal requirements. For example, a genuinely novel and nonobvious mechanical locking mechanism might be a candidate for patent protection. A patent does not automatically arise just because someone invents a device: applications, examination, jurisdiction, and legal requirements matter. A trademark identifies the commercial source of goods or services, such as a distinctive brand name or logo used for a line of carts. It does not protect the cart's mechanical function. Copyright protects original expression fixed in a tangible form, such as original written instructions, illustrations, and certain software code; it generally does not protect the underlying mechanical idea or method itself.

A trade secret can protect valuable confidential business information when reasonable steps are taken to keep it secret. A private manufacturing process might qualify, whereas information publicly disclosed without protection generally cannot remain secret. These categories are introduced here as engineering literacy, not as legal advice; specific protection depends on facts and applicable law.

SECTION 04

Ethics, Ownership, and Accurate Reporting

Technical honesty is an engineering responsibility. A team must not invent test readings, conceal a failed trial without explanation, copy another person's work without permission, or claim that a prototype is safe when required testing has not been completed. The correct approach is to preserve raw data, explain unusual measurements, cite outside sources, and state uncertainty plainly.

Students sometimes assume that anything found online can be reused freely. That is not true. A diagram or code sample may be copyrighted, licensed under specific conditions, or subject to other restrictions. Teams should record where reference material came from, check the license or permission, and distinguish their own contributions from outside work. Similarly, employment agreements, school policies, and contracts may affect who owns work created during a project; being the person who drew a design does not always settle legal ownership.

Engineering communication should never exaggerate the evidence. If the redesigned cart has only been tested on a smooth floor with a 15 kg load, the report should say exactly that. It should not claim the cart performs better on rough surfaces or under every load. A clear statement of what remains untested is a strength of the report, not a weakness.

SECTION 05

Calculate Performance Changes Step by Step

Engineers quantify improvements rather than relying on vague phrases. We will compare the cart's original average starting force of 35 N with the redesigned average of 26 N. Because lower starting force is desirable, a reduction represents improved performance on this particular measure.

Step 1 — Identify the baseline and redesigned values

Write the values with units: original = 35 N and redesigned = 26 N. The original is the baseline against which the change will be measured.

Step 2 — Calculate the absolute change

Reduction = original value − redesigned value

Substitute: 35 − 26 = 9 N. The revised cart requires 9 N less starting force on average in this controlled comparison.

Step 3 — Calculate percentage reduction relative to the baseline

Percent reduction = (reduction ÷ original value) × 100%

Substitute: (9 ÷ 35) × 100% ≈ 25.7%. The denominator is the original 35 N, not the redesigned 26 N. The result describes the observed mean reduction under the tested conditions; it is not a guarantee of performance in every situation.

SECTION 06

Redesign Is More Than a Better Number

Improving one measure can make another worse. Larger wheels might reduce starting force but increase the cart's width or cost. An engineer must first check every mandatory constraint and then compare the desirable criteria among the feasible options. A design that exceeds the maximum allowed width is not acceptable just because its wheels roll easily.

For this instructional project, suppose the cart must carry at least 180 kg, fit within a maximum width of 720 mm, and cost no more than $150 to build. Starting force is a performance criterion to minimize among designs that meet all three constraints. These are fictional classroom requirements, not safety ratings for a real cart.

Consider three revisions. Revision A has a 35 N starting force, 190 kg rated load capacity, 700 mm width, and $135 cost. Revision B has 26 N, 210 kg, 710 mm, and $148. Revision C has 20 N, 230 kg, 735 mm, and $140. Revision C has the lowest starting force, but its 735 mm width exceeds the 720 mm maximum. It must be rejected or redesigned. A and B satisfy the stated limits; B has the lower starting force, although it costs $13 more than A.

SECTION 07

A Defensible Redesign Decision

The team can now make a recommendation using the same sequence introduced in earlier lessons. First, document the alternatives and their measurements. The table records each revision's starting force, load capacity, width, and cost. Second, screen the mandatory constraints. A and B pass, while C fails the width limit. Third, compare the feasible options. B reduces starting force by 9 N relative to A, a reduction of approximately 25.7%, while increasing cost by $13. Fourth, state the recommendation and remaining questions. The team can recommend B for further evaluation if lower starting force justifies its additional cost.

The phrase for further evaluation matters. The listed load capacity values are assumed specifications in this fictional exercise, and the test data address starting force only. Before real deployment, the team would still need to verify stability, braking, durability, manufacturing quality, and other applicable safety requirements. An engineering recommendation is strongest when it clearly separates what has been measured from what still needs verification.

SECTION 09

Write a Revision Record That Someone Else Can Use

A good record tells the next engineer exactly what changed and what the evidence means. For Revision B, begin with a dated identifier and the design change: larger wheels replace the original wheel configuration. Include the test procedure: same 15 kg load, same floor, same gauge, and three trials for each design. Preserve the individual force readings rather than only the averages. Calculate the mean reduction of 9 N and the percentage reduction of approximately 25.7%.

Next, record the trade-offs: Revision B meets the classroom width, capacity, and cost limits, but costs $13 more than Revision A. End with the decision and limitations: recommend B for additional testing, including stability, braking, and durability. If a drawing is updated, assign the correct revision letter and ensure that the change log and drawing match. The goal is not to make the report sound impressive; it is to make the design history understandable and verifiable.

SECTION 10

Unit A2 Synthesis and Glossary

Unit A2 has followed a complete design-and-communication process. In A2.1, you learned that engineers define problems, generate ideas, select concepts, prototype, test, and revise. In A2.2, you learned to distinguish criteria from mandatory constraints. In A2.3, you compared alternatives using weighted scores. In A2.4, you interpreted technical drawings and calculated geometry. In A2.5, you planned controlled experiments and analyzed repeated measurements. This final lesson adds traceable records, responsible treatment of intellectual property, and evidence-based redesign.

Documentation: Organized record of a design and its evidence. Revision: Identified version of a design. Change log: Record of what changed and why. Baseline: Original version or value used for comparison. Patent: Potential legal protection for a qualifying invention. Trademark: Source-identifying brand protection. Copyright: Protection for original expression. Trade secret: Protected confidential business information. Feasibility screening: Checking every mandatory constraint before ranking designs. Redesign: Changing a design based on requirements and evidence.

For the Unit A2 assessment and the final exam, practice matching types of intellectual property to examples, selecting useful revision records, identifying unsupported conclusions, calculating before-and-after improvements, and explaining why an option that fails a mandatory requirement cannot be selected merely because it has a desirable performance number.
