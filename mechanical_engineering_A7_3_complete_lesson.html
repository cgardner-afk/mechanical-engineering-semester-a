Section 01

Why acceleration matters in machine design

Begin with something familiar: a car leaving a stoplight.

When a car first starts moving, its speed is small. A few seconds later it may be traveling much faster. We say the car accelerated. When the driver brakes, the car's speed decreases. Engineers study both changes because a machine cannot safely start or stop instantly.

Now imagine a motorized factory cart carrying a heavy box along a straight rail. A motor provides motion, a controller tells the motor what to do, and a sensor measures what actually happened. A motion profile is simply the plan for how the cart should speed up, travel, and slow down. You do not need to know how to build a motor to understand the measurements.

Our question is: How can we tell whether the cart is speeding up, slowing down, or moving at a steady pace? We will answer first with numbers and then with graphs.

An automated transfer cart carries heavy machined parts between stations in a factory. The engineer knows the cart's destination and desired cruising speed, but those two values do not tell the whole story. A loaded cart cannot safely jump from rest to full speed, and it cannot stop instantly at the inspection station. The drive motor, transmission, wheels, rail, controller, and braking system must work together to change velocity in a predictable way.

Acceleration describes how quickly velocity changes. Engineers use acceleration data to size drives, plan smooth motion profiles, interpret sensor measurements, and check whether a machine can reach its target position within an allotted time. In this lesson, we will study straight-line motion with constant acceleration, read position–time and velocity–time graphs, and investigate a programmed cart's motion using a virtual machine test stand.

Although the mathematics is shared with physics, the engineering goal is different: we use the results to make decisions about machine travel, motor performance, inspection timing, and safe operating limits.

Section 02

Define velocity, acceleration, and sign

What does “meters per second squared” mean?

Suppose the cart starts at 0 m/s and increases its velocity by 2 m/s every second. After one second it moves at 2 m/s; after two seconds, 4 m/s; after three seconds, 6 m/s. Its acceleration is 2 m/s². The unusual unit means “the number of meters per second that velocity changes during each second.”

The letter a stands for acceleration, v for velocity, and t for time. The symbol Δ (delta) means “change in.” Therefore, Δv = final velocity minus starting velocity, and Δt = elapsed time. The equation a = Δv/Δt asks: How much did the velocity change, divided by how long did that change take?

Direction warning: If the cart is moving right, a negative acceleration usually slows it down. If it is moving left, a negative acceleration may make it move left faster. Always ask which direction it is moving before deciding whether it is speeding up.

Choose east as the positive direction along the factory rail. Velocity is positive when the cart moves east and negative when it moves west. Acceleration is the change in signed velocity divided by elapsed time. Its unit is meters per second squared (m/s²), meaning that the velocity changes by that many meters per second during each second, if acceleration is constant.

Average acceleration: a = (vf − vi)/Δt

A positive acceleration means the velocity is becoming more positive; a negative acceleration means the velocity is becoming more negative. Neither sign by itself tells you whether the cart is speeding up. For example, a cart moving west at −8 m/s with acceleration +2 m/s² is slowing down because its velocity is moving toward zero. Engineers must compare the signs of velocity and acceleration rather than assuming negative acceleration always means braking.

Section 03

Calculate acceleration from changing velocity

Work through a calculation slowly.

A cart begins at 2 m/s and reaches 8 m/s after 3 seconds. Step 1: Write the starting velocity, vi = 2 m/s. Step 2: Write the ending velocity, vf = 8 m/s. Step 3: Find the change: 8 − 2 = 6 m/s. Step 4: Divide by time: 6 ÷ 3 = 2 m/s². Step 5: Interpret: the cart gained 2 m/s of velocity each second, on average.

Now try changing only the time. If the same 6 m/s change took 6 seconds instead of 3, acceleration would be 6 ÷ 6 = 1 m/s². Taking longer to make the same velocity change means a smaller acceleration.

Worked example 1: A powered cart accelerates east from 2 m/s to 14 m/s over 6 s. Subtract the initial velocity from the final velocity: Δv = 14 − 2 = +12 m/s. Divide by 6 s to obtain a = +2 m/s². If this acceleration remains constant, the cart gains 2 m/s of eastward velocity every second.

Worked example 2: A cart approaches a docking station traveling +12 m/s and slows uniformly to +3 m/s in 3 s. Its acceleration is (3 − 12)/3 = −3 m/s². The negative sign indicates that the velocity is changing westward relative to the positive axis. Because the cart's velocity remains positive, its speed is decreasing.

Worked example 3: A reverse-moving maintenance carriage changes velocity from −10 m/s to −4 m/s in 2 s. Its acceleration is [−4 − (−10)]/2 = +3 m/s². The carriage is still moving west, but slowing down. This distinction matters when engineers interpret braking and reversing sensor logs.

Section 04

Predict final velocity from a drive profile

Where does vf = vi + at come from?

Start with the idea that acceleration is the velocity change each second. If acceleration is 2 m/s² for 4 seconds, the total velocity change is 2 × 4 = 8 m/s. Add that change to whatever velocity the cart had at the start. If it started at 3 m/s, it finishes at 3 + 8 = 11 m/s.

The equation uses short labels: vi is initial (starting) velocity; vf is final (ending) velocity; a is acceleration; and t is elapsed time. The formula works as written when acceleration stays constant over the chosen time interval.

When acceleration is constant, the velocity changes by the same amount during each equal time interval. Rearranging the acceleration equation gives a formula used to predict velocity after a specified period of motor operation.

Final velocity: vf = vi + at

Worked example 4: A robot cart starts at +4 m/s and accelerates at +2.5 m/s² for 6 s. Its final velocity is 4 + (2.5)(6) = +19 m/s. The engineer can compare this prediction to the controller's commanded maximum speed.

Worked example 5: A cart begins at +15 m/s and brakes with constant acceleration −2 m/s² for 4 s. Its final velocity is 15 − 8 = +7 m/s. It is still moving east; the brakes have reduced its speed but have not yet brought it to rest. Do not assume that applying the brakes means the final velocity is zero.

Section 05

Position–time graphs show where the machine is

How to read a graph when you've never seen one.

A graph is a picture of how one measurement changes compared with another. The horizontal axis runs left to right; here it shows time in seconds. The vertical axis runs up and down; on a position–time graph it shows where the cart is in meters. Each point means “at this time, the cart was at this position.”

To find the slope, choose two points on a straight line. Find how much the vertical value changed (called the rise) and divide by how much the horizontal value changed (called the run). For example, from 0 m at 0 s to 12 m at 3 s, the rise is 12 m and the run is 3 s. Slope = 12 ÷ 3 = 4 m/s. That is the cart's velocity.

What does the line shape tell you? A line rising to the right means positive velocity. A line falling to the right means negative velocity. A flat line means the position did not change, so the cart was stopped during that interval.

A position–time graph plots the cart's position vertically and time horizontally. The slope of a straight-line segment is the change in position divided by elapsed time, which is the cart's velocity over that interval. An upward straight line means constant positive velocity; a downward straight line means constant negative velocity; a horizontal line means the position is not changing.

Worked example 6: A cart's position increases from 0 m at 0 s to +20 m at 5 s along a straight line. Its velocity is the slope: (20 − 0)/(5 − 0) = +4 m/s. If the line were horizontal instead, the cart would be stationary during that interval. A curved position–time graph indicates changing slope and therefore changing velocity.

Section 06

Velocity–time graphs reveal acceleration

Same graph-reading skill, different measurement.

On a velocity–time graph, the bottom axis still shows time. But now the vertical axis shows velocity, not position. That changes what the slope means. If velocity rises from 2 m/s to 10 m/s over 4 seconds, the vertical change is 8 m/s and the horizontal change is 4 s. The slope is 8 ÷ 4 = 2 m/s², which is acceleration.

Notice the flat part of the graph. The cart's velocity stays at 10 m/s, so its acceleration is zero. But the cart is still moving. A flat velocity–time line means steady velocity; a flat position–time line means no movement. Always read the vertical-axis label first.

A velocity–time graph plots signed velocity vertically and elapsed time horizontally. Its slope is acceleration. A rising straight line indicates constant positive acceleration; a falling straight line indicates constant negative acceleration. A horizontal segment indicates constant velocity and zero acceleration, even if the velocity is not zero.

Worked example 7: From 0 to 4 s, the velocity increases from +2 m/s to +10 m/s, so the slope is (10 − 2)/4 = +2 m/s². From 4 to 7 s, the line is horizontal at +10 m/s, so acceleration is 0 m/s² during that interval. The cart continues moving at constant velocity; it has not stopped.

Section 07

Area under velocity–time graphs measures displacement

What does “area under the graph” mean?

Imagine a rectangle whose width is 4 seconds and height is 3 meters per second. Multiplying width × height gives 4 s × 3 m/s = 12 m. The seconds cancel, leaving meters. That rectangle's area represents how far the cart's position changed during those four seconds.

When velocity steadily rises, the shape under the line may be a triangle or a trapezoid rather than a rectangle. For a trapezoid, average the starting and ending heights, then multiply by the width. For example, velocity rises evenly from 2 to 6 m/s over 4 s: [(2 + 6) ÷ 2] × 4 = 16 m.

Important: Area above the zero-velocity line counts as positive displacement. Area below it counts as negative displacement. To find total distance when the cart changes direction, add the positive sizes of both regions rather than allowing them to cancel.

The signed area between a velocity–time curve and the time axis gives displacement. When the velocity is positive throughout an interval, the area also equals the distance traveled. When velocity crosses below zero, the area below the axis counts as negative displacement, while total distance adds the magnitudes of all positive and negative areas.

For constant positive velocity, the region is a rectangle: displacement = velocity × time. For a velocity that increases uniformly from vi to vf, the region is a trapezoid: displacement = [(vi + vf)/2]t. The same result can be seen as an initial-velocity rectangle plus an acceleration triangle.

Constant acceleration: Δx = [(vi + vf)/2]t = vit + ½at²

Worked example 8: For the graph in the previous section, the rising segment from 0 to 4 s covers a trapezoid with area [(2 + 10)/2] × 4 = 24 m. The constant-velocity segment from 4 to 7 s covers 10 × 3 = 30 m. Total displacement is 54 m; because velocity is always positive, total distance is also 54 m.

Section 08

Predict distance with constant acceleration

Why does the distance formula include ½at²?

If a cart starts from rest and speeds up evenly, it does not move at its final speed for the entire trip. Its speed starts at zero and increases. With constant acceleration, its average velocity during that interval is halfway between its starting and ending velocities. Starting from rest at 0 m/s and ending at 8 m/s means average velocity is 4 m/s. Over 4 seconds, displacement is 4 × 4 = 16 m.

The equation Δx = vit + ½at² combines the distance due to starting velocity with the extra distance due to accelerating. The superscript ² means “multiply by itself,” so t² = t × t. If t = 4 s, then t² = 16 s². You can check the example with a = 2 m/s²: 0(4) + ½(2)(16) = 16 m.

A machine controller may specify initial velocity, constant acceleration, and the time allowed for a move. The engineering question is often whether the cart can cover the necessary travel before reaching the next station. Under constant acceleration, use the kinematic displacement equation.

Displacement: Δx = vit + ½at²

Worked example 9: A cart begins at +3 m/s and accelerates east at +2 m/s² for 5 s. Its displacement is (3)(5) + ½(2)(5²) = 15 + 25 = +40 m. The final velocity is 3 + (2)(5) = +13 m/s. Since velocity stays positive, distance traveled is 40 m.

Worked example 10: A cart starts from rest and accelerates at +1.5 m/s² for 8 s. Displacement is 0 + ½(1.5)(64) = +48 m. Final velocity is (1.5)(8) = +12 m/s. If the available rail were only 35 m long, this programmed move would exceed it. The engineer must change the profile or layout rather than rely on the desired endpoint alone.

Read sensor data and extrapolate a motion trend

A five-step problem-solving routine.

1. Sketch: Draw a horizontal arrow and label the positive direction. 2. List: Write what you know: starting velocity, acceleration, and time. 3. Choose: If the question asks final velocity, use vf = vi + at. If it asks displacement, use Δx = vit + ½at². 4. Substitute: Put the numbers in with their signs and units. 5. Interpret: Explain whether the cart ended farther right or left and whether the answer fits the available track.

Don't memorize an equation without knowing the question it answers. Engineers choose equations based on the information available and the decision they need to make.

Real machine controllers use sampled measurements rather than ideal textbook graphs. When the measured velocity increases by a consistent amount during equal time intervals, the engineer may infer approximately constant acceleration. A line fitted to the data can predict velocity at a later time, provided the acceleration remains unchanged and the prediction stays within a plausible operating range.

Section 10

Distinguish graph slope, graph area, and direction

How to use a data table.

A machine sensor might measure velocity once every two seconds. Read the table row by row. If velocity is 3 m/s at 0 s and 7 m/s at 2 s, it increased by 4 m/s in 2 seconds. That is 2 m/s². Check the next pair of rows: from 7 to 11 m/s in another 2 seconds is the same change. When the increases stay consistent, the data suggest constant acceleration.

Extrapolate means predict beyond the measurements using the observed pattern. If the same increase continues, the next reading two seconds later would be 4 m/s higher. In a real machine, however, the motor may reach a speed limit. An extrapolation is a prediction based on an assumption, not a guarantee.

Two graph-reading errors are especially common. First, the slope of a position–time graph gives velocity, not acceleration. Second, the slope of a velocity–time graph gives acceleration, while the area under that graph gives displacement. Engineers should label both axes and their units before interpreting any motion plot.

On a velocity–time graph, a horizontal line at +6 m/s represents motion at constant positive velocity. It does not represent a stationary machine. A line at zero velocity indicates rest. A downward-sloping line can represent slowing down when velocity is positive, or speeding up toward the west when velocity is negative. The signs must be interpreted together.

Graph-reading checklist:

Before answering any graph question, ask: (1) What does the bottom axis measure? (2) What does the side axis measure? (3) What units are shown? (4) Does the question ask for a slope, an area, or a value at one point? (5) Does the line cross below zero, meaning the machine changed direction?

Remember: Position–time slope → velocity. Velocity–time slope → acceleration. Velocity–time area → displacement. These are three different operations, even though all involve motion.

Section 12

Interpret virtual machine-test results

When you increase acceleration while holding the initial velocity and acceleration duration fixed, the end velocity increases according to vf = vi + at. The acceleration-stage displacement also increases because the cart spends more of the interval moving at higher velocities. The velocity–time graph becomes steeper, and the position–time graph curves upward more strongly.

When acceleration is zero, the velocity graph is horizontal and the position graph is a straight line. The cart can still travel a substantial distance: zero acceleration means unchanging velocity, not zero velocity. When acceleration is negative while the cart moves east, the velocity line slopes downward. If it crosses zero, the cart stops momentarily and then begins moving west; total distance must count travel in both directions.

Model limits: The simulation assumes ideal constant acceleration and immediate transitions between stages. Real systems have motor torque limits, wheel traction limits, jerk constraints, sensor noise, and controller response delays. Engineering validation requires actual performance measurements and appropriate safety margins.

Section 13

Engineering applications: drive sizing and stopping space

Acceleration analysis is part of selecting motors and drive systems. A cart that must reach a target speed within a short time needs sufficient drive force, and its moving mass influences the force required. A heavy payload can change the feasible acceleration even when the desired travel distance is unchanged. Later engineering work connects acceleration to net force using Newton's second law, but the motion profile can be analyzed first from measured velocities and times.

Braking introduces an equally important constraint. A machine traveling quickly may need considerable distance to reduce its velocity to zero. Engineers compare the required stopping distance with the available space before a wall, guard, or workstation. The simple constant-acceleration model can estimate a stopping profile, but a real safety system must also account for reaction time, braking variation, load, surface conditions, and applicable machine-safety requirements.

For the Unit A7 assessment, students should be able to interpret graphs and calculate motion quantities. For engineering design, they should also ask whether the calculated travel fits the actual machine and whether the assumptions are reasonable.

EXTRA GUIDED PRACTICE · BEGINNER SUPPORT

Motion-graph decision guide: which calculation should I use?

A motion graph is a picture of measurements over time. Before doing any arithmetic, read the vertical axis label and the horizontal axis label. The horizontal axis usually shows time in seconds (s). The vertical axis tells you what the graph is measuring.

If the vertical axis says…To find…Do this
Position (m)VelocityFind the slope: change in position ÷ change in time. A rising line means positive velocity; a falling line means negative velocity.
Velocity (m/s)AccelerationFind the slope: change in velocity ÷ change in time. A flat line means zero acceleration, even when the object is moving.
Velocity (m/s)DisplacementFind the signed area between the line and the time axis. Above zero is positive; below zero is negative.
Memory aid: Position graph → slope gives velocity. Velocity graph → slope gives acceleration, area gives displacement. Read the question first, then choose the operation.

Worked example A: position graph

A machine carriage is at 2 m at 1 s and 14 m at 5 s. Its position graph is a straight line. First find the vertical change: 14 − 2 = 12 m. Next find the horizontal change: 5 − 1 = 4 s. Slope = 12 ÷ 4 = +3 m/s. The positive sign means movement in the chosen positive direction.

Worked example B: velocity graph slope

A conveyor starts at +2 m/s at 0 s and reaches +10 m/s at 4 s along a straight line on its velocity graph. Acceleration = (10 − 2) ÷ (4 − 0) = +2 m/s². Notice that we subtracted velocities, not positions.

Worked example C: velocity graph area

During the same four seconds, velocity increases steadily from +2 to +10 m/s. The area is a trapezoid. Find its average height: (2 + 10) ÷ 2 = 6 m/s. Multiply by the width: 6 × 4 = +24 m displacement. If the velocity were constantly −3 m/s for 4 s instead, the signed area would be −3 × 4 = −12 m.

Worked example D: predicting a later velocity

If a cart has velocity +4 m/s at 2 s and accelerates steadily at +3 m/s², what is its velocity at 6 s? Only 4 seconds pass between the two times. Use final velocity = starting velocity + acceleration × elapsed time: 4 + 3 × 4 = +16 m/s. Do not multiply acceleration by 6 seconds unless the starting velocity was measured at 0 s.

Try independently before revealing the solutions

1. A position graph goes from −3 m at 2 s to +9 m at 6 s. Find its slope and interpret it.

Change in position = 9 − (−3) = 12 m. Elapsed time = 6 − 2 = 4 s. Slope = +3 m/s, so velocity is positive.

2. A velocity graph rises from −4 m/s at 0 s to +2 m/s at 3 s. Find acceleration.

Change in velocity = 2 − (−4) = +6 m/s. Time = 3 s. Acceleration = +2 m/s². The cart first moves in the negative direction, slows, then reverses.

3. A velocity graph stays at −5 m/s for 3 s. Find displacement and distance.

Signed area = −5 × 3 = −15 m displacement. The cart travels 15 m distance. The negative sign describes direction, not a negative distance.

4. A cart's velocity rises steadily from 0 to +8 m/s in 4 s. Find acceleration and displacement.

Acceleration = (8 − 0)/4 = +2 m/s². The velocity–time area is a triangle: ½ × 4 × 8 = +16 m.

5. A cart travels at +6 m/s at 3 s and accelerates steadily at −2 m/s². Predict its velocity at 5 s.

Elapsed time is 5 − 3 = 2 s. Final velocity = 6 + (−2 × 2) = +2 m/s. Negative acceleration here reduces its positive velocity.

Engineering check: Before accepting an answer, verify that the units match the question: velocity uses m/s, acceleration uses m/s², and displacement uses m. A correct number with the wrong unit is not a complete engineering result.

Section 14

Independent engineering practice

Work each problem on paper before opening the explanation. Identify the given quantities, select the appropriate equation, substitute with units, and interpret the sign.

1. A cart's velocity rises from 3 to 15 m/s in 4 s. Find acceleration.

a = (15 − 3)/4 = +3 m/s².

2. A cart slows from +14 to +2 m/s in 6 s. Find acceleration.

a = (2 − 14)/6 = −2 m/s².

3. A carriage moves at −8 m/s and accelerates at +2 m/s² for 3 s. Find final velocity.

v = −8 + (2)(3) = −2 m/s. It is still moving west, but slower.

4. A robot begins at +5 m/s and accelerates at +1.5 m/s² for 8 s. Find final velocity.

v = 5 + (1.5)(8) = +17 m/s.

5. A straight position–time graph rises from 4 m at 1 s to 28 m at 7 s. Find velocity.

Slope = (28 − 4)/(7 − 1) = +4 m/s.

6. A velocity–time graph rises from 1 m/s at 0 s to 13 m/s at 6 s. Find acceleration.

Slope = (13 − 1)/6 = +2 m/s².

7. A cart moves at +8 m/s for 5 s. Find displacement from the velocity–time graph.

Rectangle area = 8 × 5 = +40 m.

8. Velocity increases uniformly from 2 to 10 m/s in 4 s. Find displacement.

Trapezoid area = (2 + 10)/2 × 4 = 24 m.

9. A cart starts at 2 m/s and accelerates at 1.5 m/s² for 4 s. Find displacement.

Δx = (2)(4) + ½(1.5)(16) = 8 + 12 = 20 m.

10. A cart starts at 4 m/s and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 5 s. Find displacement.

Δx = (4)(5) + ½(2)(25) = 20 + 25 = 45 m.

11. A sensor reports 4, 7, 10, 13 m/s at 0, 2, 4, 6 s. Predict velocity at 8 s.

Each 2 s adds 3 m/s, so a = 1.5 m/s². At 8 s, v = 4 + (1.5)(8) = 16 m/s.

12. On a velocity–time graph, the line is horizontal at +6 m/s from 3 to 9 s. Find acceleration and displacement for that interval.

Acceleration = 0 m/s². Displacement = 6 × (9 − 3) = +36 m.

13. A velocity–time graph shows +4 m/s for 3 s, then −2 m/s for 2 s. Find displacement and distance.

Displacement = (4)(3) + (−2)(2) = +8 m. Distance = 12 + 4 = 16 m.

14. A cart starts at +6 m/s with a = −2 m/s². When does it stop?

0 = 6 − 2t, so t = 3 s.

15. For problem 14, how far does it travel before stopping?

Δx = 6(3) + ½(−2)(3²) = 18 − 9 = 9 m.

16. What does a steeper upward line on a velocity–time graph indicate?

A larger positive acceleration, because slope is Δv/Δt.

17. A position–time graph is horizontal for 5 s. What is the cart doing?

Its position is constant, so its velocity is 0 m/s during that interval.

18. Why must engineers distinguish signed displacement from total distance when a velocity graph crosses zero?

The signed areas may cancel in displacement, but the magnitudes of areas in both directions must be added for distance.

Section 15

Vocabulary glossary

Expand each term to review the language used by mechanical engineers and motion-control technicians.

Acceleration

Rate of change of signed velocity with time, measured in m/s².

Constant acceleration

Motion in which velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals.

Initial velocity

Velocity at the beginning of the chosen observation interval.

Final velocity

Velocity at the end of the chosen observation interval.

Position–time graph

Plot of signed position versus time; its slope gives velocity.

Velocity–time graph

Plot of signed velocity versus time; its slope gives acceleration.

Graph slope

Vertical change divided by horizontal change; its meaning depends on the axes.

Area under velocity–time graph

Signed displacement over the time interval.

Total distance

The total length of the path an object travels, regardless of direction.

Displacement

The change in an object's position from start to finish, including direction. Calculate it as final position minus initial position: Δx = xfinal − xinitial.

Constant velocity

Unchanging signed velocity, represented by a horizontal line on a velocity–time graph.

Cruising stage

Motion interval during which a machine maintains approximately constant velocity.

Motion profile

Planned sequence of velocity and acceleration changes for a machine.

Braking acceleration

Acceleration applied to reduce speed; its sign depends on travel direction.

Reversal

Change in direction when signed velocity passes through zero.

Extrapolation

Prediction beyond measured data based on an assumed continuing trend.

Sensor sampling

Recording motion measurements at successive times.

Travel envelope

The complete range of positions a machine occupies during its movement.

A7.3 Review Quiz

Acceleration measures the rate of change of velocity. For constant acceleration, vf = vi + at and Δx = vit + ½at². On a position–time graph, slope represents velocity. On a velocity–time graph, slope represents acceleration and signed area represents displacement. When velocity remains positive, the area also equals distance; when velocity changes sign, distance is the sum of absolute areas.

Mechanical engineers use these relationships to interpret machine sensors, evaluate motion profiles, plan travel space, and understand stopping requirements. In A7.4, we will extend motion analysis to momentum and collisions, investigating what happens when moving carts interact and how engineers analyze those events.
