Section 01

The engineering challenge: stop a moving cart safely

Imagine a 500 kg industrial cart approaching a loading platform at 6 m/s. A protective barrier must stop it without damaging the platform or allowing the cart to reach a worker. The barrier needs to reduce the impact force, but it also has to fit in the available space and stay within a purchasing budget. An engineer cannot choose a barrier using force alone.

In this lesson, you will act as a mechanical engineer. You will compare proposed protective systems, calculate their average stopping forces, check their design constraints, and defend a recommendation using numerical evidence. You will also learn how to conduct a fair test so that a comparison is meaningful.

The central engineering question

Which barrier stops the cart with the lowest average force while meeting both the available deformation space and the project budget?

Section 02

Review impulse and average stopping force

From A7.5, you know that impulse is a change in momentum. When an object stops, its final velocity is zero. For a cart of constant mass, the impulse needed to stop it is the negative of its initial momentum. If east is positive, a cart moving east receives a westward stopping impulse.

J = Δp = m(vf − vi)   and   Favg = J ÷ Δt

For a 500 kg cart moving east at +6 m/s, initial momentum is +3,000 kg·m/s. Stopping it requires an impulse of −3,000 N·s. If the stopping time is 0.30 s, the average net force is −10,000 N. The negative sign means the force points west; the force magnitude is 10,000 N.

These calculations assume a one-dimensional stop and a known stopping time. They do not tell us the peak force, the exact material behavior, or whether a barrier can survive repeated impacts.

Section 03

Why cushioning and stopping time matter

A rigid stop brings a moving cart to rest quickly. A compressible barrier can extend the stopping process by bending, compressing, or otherwise changing shape. For the same initial momentum and final velocity, a longer stopping time means a smaller magnitude of average force.

Worked example 1: The same 500 kg cart at +6 m/s stops in 0.15 s against a stiff barrier. The average force is −3,000 ÷ 0.15 = −20,000 N. A second barrier stops it in 0.60 s, giving −3,000 ÷ 0.60 = −5,000 N. The second design reduces average force magnitude to one quarter of the first.

However, a softer barrier might compress too far. Engineers must compare the force benefit with the physical space available for the barrier to deform.

Section 04

Three kinds of engineering constraints

A design criterion is a feature used to judge how well a design performs. For this project, lower average stopping force is the primary performance criterion. A constraint is a limit that a design must not violate. Our first constraint is maximum deformation: the barrier may compress no more than 0.60 m. The second is maximum cost: the barrier may cost no more than $170.

A feasible design must satisfy: deformation ≤ 0.60 m AND cost ≤ $170

Notice the word AND. A design is feasible only if it satisfies both limits. A barrier that costs $160 but deforms 0.75 m is not feasible. A barrier that deforms only 0.40 m but costs $210 is also not feasible. A design that meets both limits can then be compared with other feasible designs for force performance.

Important distinction: The lowest-force design is not automatically the best engineering choice. It must first satisfy all required constraints.

Section 06

Worked design decision: identify the feasible options

Worked example 2: You are given the four barriers in the table above. Your supervisor asks you to select the best option under a deformation limit of 0.60 m and a budget of $170.

Step-by-step worked method · Select a barrier

  1. Step 1 — State the limits: deformation must be ≤ 0.60 m and cost must be ≤ $170.
  2. Step 2 — Check A: 0.22 m ≤ 0.60 m and $100 ≤ $170. A passes both.
  3. Step 3 — Check B: 0.55 m ≤ 0.60 m and $160 ≤ $170. B passes both.
  4. Step 4 — Check C: 0.78 m exceeds 0.60 m. C fails, even though it is inexpensive.
  5. Step 5 — Check D: $220 exceeds $170. D fails, even though its deformation is acceptable.
  6. Step 6 — Compare feasible options: B's 5,000 N is less than A's 15,000 N.
  7. Step 7 — Recommend: Choose B and explain that it meets both constraints while giving the lowest force among feasible choices.

In an engineering report, a recommendation should include the numerical evidence, not merely the name of the chosen design.

Section 07

Worked calculations for different barrier times

Worked example 3: A 600 kg test cart moves east at 5 m/s and stops in 0.40 s. First calculate the initial momentum: 600 × (+5) = +3,000 kg·m/s. Then find the impulse: 0 − 3,000 = −3,000 N·s. Divide by the stopping time: −3,000 ÷ 0.40 = −7,500 N. The average stopping force magnitude is 7,500 N.

Worked example 4: The same cart stops in 0.75 s using a different barrier. The impulse remains −3,000 N·s, but average force becomes −3,000 ÷ 0.75 = −4,000 N. This is a 3,500 N reduction in average force magnitude. If the longer-stop barrier exceeds a required deformation limit, though, it still cannot be selected.

Worked example 5: A 400 kg cart travels west at −4 m/s and stops in 0.50 s. Its initial momentum is −1,600 kg·m/s, so the impulse is +1,600 N·s. Average force is +1,600 ÷ 0.50 = +3,200 N. The positive sign indicates an eastward stopping force. Always distinguish a signed force from its nonnegative magnitude.

Section 08

Why fair testing matters

An engineering team may claim that one barrier is safer because its measured stopping force was lower. But what if the first test used a 500 kg cart at 6 m/s while the second used a 300 kg cart at 4 m/s? Those results cannot isolate the barrier's effect. Both the mass and the incoming velocity changed, so the initial momentum was different.

A fair test changes only the independent variable being investigated while keeping relevant controlled variables the same. To compare barrier designs, use the same cart mass, approach speed, direction, measurement method, and starting conditions. The barrier type is the independent variable. Stopping time, deformation, and measured force are dependent variables, or outcomes that may change in response.

Fair comparison

Test Barrier A and Barrier B with the same 500 kg cart traveling at +6 m/s, on the same track, using the same sensors. Then compare their stopping time, deformation, and average force.

Unfair comparison: Test A at 6 m/s and B at 3 m/s, then conclude that B is better because it produced less force. The lower incoming speed could explain the difference.
Section 09

Controlled variables, repeated trials, and evidence

Even with controlled conditions, real measurements vary. Small differences in cart alignment, material temperature, sensor timing, or barrier installation can change the result. Engineers conduct repeated trials to see whether a design performs consistently. They record the results rather than selecting only the most favorable run.

For example, suppose Barrier B produces stopping times of 0.58 s, 0.61 s, and 0.60 s in three repeated tests with the same cart. Its mean stopping time is (0.58 + 0.61 + 0.60) ÷ 3 = 0.597 s, approximately 0.60 s. An engineer would also inspect whether deformation remains below 0.60 m in every trial and whether the system behaves safely when tested repeatedly.

Measurement uncertainty means a measured value may differ slightly from the true value. A design that deforms exactly 0.60 m in one test could exceed the limit in another. This is one reason real projects often include a safety margin rather than designing precisely at a hard limit.

Section 11

How to write a defensible engineering recommendation

An engineering recommendation is a short argument supported by calculations and test results. Begin by describing the problem and the constraints. Then explain which options pass and fail. Finally, compare the performance of the remaining feasible options and identify the recommended design.

Model recommendation:

For the 500 kg cart traveling at 6 m/s, I recommend Barrier B. It costs $160, which is below the $170 budget, and deforms 0.55 m, which is below the 0.60 m limit. Its average stopping force magnitude is 5,000 N, compared with 15,000 N for the only other feasible barrier, A. Although Barrier C has a lower calculated force, it exceeds the deformation limit. Barrier D exceeds the budget. Therefore, B is the best feasible choice under the stated criteria.

Notice that the recommendation does not say B is universally the safest barrier. It is the best option under the stated conditions and simplified model. A real approval would require additional testing of peak force, material durability, repeated impacts, installation, and safety margins.

Section 12

Independent practice with guided solutions

Try each problem before opening the solution. Show the constraints, the impulse–momentum calculation when required, and a sentence explaining your design decision.

1. A 300 kg cart moves at +4 m/s and stops in 0.30 s. Find the average net stopping force.

Guided solution: Initial momentum = +1,200 kg·m/s. Impulse = −1,200 N·s. Average force = −1,200/0.30 = −4,000 N; magnitude 4,000 N.

2. The same cart stops in 0.60 s. What happens to force magnitude?

Guided solution: Impulse remains −1,200 N·s. Average force = −1,200/0.60 = −2,000 N. Magnitude is halved.

3. Maximum deformation is 0.50 m. A barrier deforms 0.52 m. Is it feasible?

Guided solution: No. It exceeds the deformation constraint by 0.02 m.

4. Budget is $150. A barrier costs $150 and meets all other limits. Does it pass the budget test?

Guided solution: Yes. A maximum of $150 allows a cost equal to $150.

5. Design X: 7,000 N, 0.40 m, $120. Design Y: 4,000 N, 0.65 m, $110. Limits: 0.60 m and $150. Which is feasible?

Guided solution: X is feasible. Y fails the deformation limit even though its force is lower.

6. Design P: 6,000 N, 0.45 m, $130. Design Q: 5,000 N, 0.50 m, $145. Limits: 0.60 m and $150. Which is preferred?

Guided solution: Both are feasible. Q has the lower average force magnitude, so choose Q.

7. What two variables must stay constant when comparing stopping times for two barriers using the same cart?

Guided solution: Cart mass and incoming velocity should stay constant; also keep relevant test conditions and measurement methods consistent.

8. Why is comparing barriers at different approach speeds an unfair test?

Guided solution: Changing speed changes incoming momentum and stopping impulse. The difference in force may be caused by speed rather than barrier design.

9. A 500 kg cart at +6 m/s stops in 0.75 s. Find average force magnitude.

Guided solution: Impulse = −3,000 N·s; average force = −4,000 N; magnitude 4,000 N.

10. A 400 kg cart moving west at −5 m/s stops in 0.50 s. Find signed force.

Guided solution: Initial momentum −2,000 kg·m/s; impulse +2,000 N·s; average force +4,000 N (eastward).

11. If a design has the lowest force but exceeds budget, should it be selected?

Guided solution: No. It is infeasible under the stated budget constraint.

12. Why should an engineer repeat an impact test?

Guided solution: To check consistency and identify variation from measurement error or changing conditions.

13. What does a 0.60 m deformation limit mean?

Guided solution: The barrier's measured deformation must be no more than 0.60 m.

14. Name one limitation of predicting impact safety from average force alone.

Guided solution: Peak force, local stress, material failure, repeated-impact durability, and deformation are not determined by average force alone.

15. At the baseline settings, which barriers pass both constraints?

Guided solution: A and B. C exceeds 0.60 m deformation and D exceeds the $170 budget.

16. At baseline, why is B preferred to A?

Guided solution: Both meet the constraints, but B has a smaller calculated average stopping-force magnitude: 5,000 N versus 15,000 N.

Section 13

Glossary: impact-design vocabulary

Select each bolded term to review its meaning. These are the same terms emphasized throughout the lesson.

Impulse

The change in momentum of an object, measured in newton-seconds (N·s).

Momentum

Mass multiplied by signed velocity, measured in kg·m/s.

Average stopping force

Net stopping impulse divided by stopping time; its sign shows direction.

Force magnitude

The nonnegative size of a force, without its direction sign.

Stopping time

The time taken to bring an object to rest.

Peak force

The greatest instantaneous force during a collision.

Deformation

Change in the shape or dimensions of a material or component under load.

Design criterion

A performance characteristic used to compare engineering options.

Design constraint

A requirement or limit that a design must satisfy.

Maximum deformation

The largest permitted compression or shape change.

Maximum cost

The largest permitted project expenditure.

Feasible design

A design that satisfies all required constraints.

Independent variable

The factor deliberately changed in an experiment.

Dependent variable

An outcome measured to see the effect of the independent variable.

Controlled variable

A factor kept the same to make comparisons fair.

Fair test

A comparison that changes the intended variable while controlling other relevant factors.

Repeated trial

A repeat of a test under the same conditions to assess consistency.

Measurement uncertainty

The possible difference between a measurement and the true value.

Safety margin

Extra allowance between expected performance and a failure or limit threshold.

Engineering recommendation

A design choice justified by criteria, constraints, and evidence.

Section 15

Lesson summary and next steps

Engineering design is more than calculating force. A protective barrier must stop the cart, fit the available space, and meet the budget. The best feasible design satisfies every constraint and performs best against the stated criterion. To calculate average stopping force, find the impulse from the change in momentum and divide by stopping time. For the same cart and momentum change, a longer stopping time reduces the magnitude of average force.

Fair testing requires consistent cart mass, speed, starting conditions, and measurement methods when comparing barriers. Repeated trials and safety margins help engineers understand whether a result is reliable. You are now ready to apply the motion, momentum, impulse, and design-decision ideas from Unit A7 to its assessment and semester review.
