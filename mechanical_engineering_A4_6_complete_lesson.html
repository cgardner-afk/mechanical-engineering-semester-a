CURRICULUM ALIGNMENT

Semester objective A-O4: Analyze forces and motion using Newton’s laws, vectors, and free-body diagrams.

Unit essential question: How do forces determine the motion or equilibrium of mechanical systems?

Learning target: Plan a controlled virtual investigation, predict motion using Fnet = ma, graph results, and support an engineering conclusion with evidence.

Objectives: Identify experimental variables and controls; calculate net force and acceleration; investigate direct and inverse relationships; interpret force-and-motion graphs; and evaluate a design requirement.

SECTION 01

From force calculations to an investigation

In earlier lessons, you learned to identify forces, use Newton’s three laws, draw free-body diagrams, and calculate friction. Engineers use these ideas to make predictions, but a prediction is only useful when it can be checked. In this final Unit A4 lesson, you will design and carry out a virtual force-and-motion investigation using a cart on a level track.

Imagine a small automated warehouse cart. Its motor must move packages without accelerating too slowly or too quickly. Engineers need to know how cart mass, motor force, and resistance affect acceleration. Rather than changing everything at once, they test one factor at a time, record measurements, graph results, and decide whether the evidence supports their model.

The central model is Newton’s second law: net force = mass × acceleration. The important word is net. A motor may push forward while friction pushes backward. The cart responds to the combined force, not just the motor’s push.

SECTION 02

Build a force model before testing

Our virtual cart moves horizontally. Its weight acts down and the track’s normal force acts up. Since the cart does not accelerate vertically, these two forces balance. A motor applies a horizontal driving force, while a simplified resistance force opposes motion. For the forward-moving trials in this lesson, we take rightward as positive.

ΣFx = Fdrive − Fresist = ma
a = (Fdrive − Fresist) / m

Acceleration is measured in meters per second squared (m/s²), force in newtons (N), and mass in kilograms (kg). In the model, resistance is a constant opposing force for the forward-moving trials; this is an intentional simplification. Actual wheel and bearing resistance can vary with speed and conditions.

SECTION 03

Make a testable prediction

An engineering hypothesis is a specific prediction that can be tested. Newton’s second law gives two useful predictions. First, if mass stays the same, increasing net force increases acceleration in direct proportion. Second, if net force stays the same, increasing mass decreases acceleration.

For example, doubling net force from 20 N to 40 N on the same 10 kg cart doubles acceleration from 2 m/s² to 4 m/s². But doubling the mass from 10 kg to 20 kg while keeping net force at 20 N cuts acceleration from 2 m/s² to 1 m/s².

A strong prediction states what will change, what will stay constant, and the expected relationship. For the first experiment, you might predict: If net force increases while cart mass remains constant, acceleration will increase proportionally. For the second: If cart mass increases while net force remains constant, acceleration will decrease.

SECTION 04

Choose variables and controls

The independent variable is what you deliberately change. The dependent variable is what you measure or calculate in response. Controlled variables are conditions you keep the same so the test is fair.

Experiment A · Force and acceleration

Change the driving force. Measure acceleration. Keep cart mass and resistance constant. Since resistance stays fixed, every increase in driving force creates the same increase in net force.

Experiment B · Mass and acceleration

Change cart mass. Measure acceleration. Keep driving force and resistance constant so net force remains unchanged.

Changing force and mass at the same time would make the results harder to interpret. If acceleration changed, you would not know which variable caused it. The virtual model lets you isolate each factor, which is why it is useful for learning experimental design.

SECTION 05

Worked example: predict a cart’s motion

A 12 kg cart receives 66 N of driving force while 6 N of resistance acts backward. The cart begins at rest. Find its acceleration and the speed it reaches after 3 seconds, assuming the forces remain constant.

Step-by-step solution

  1. Draw a force diagram. The drive force points right, resistance points left, normal force points up, and weight points down.
  2. Find net horizontal force: Fnet = 66 − 6 = 60 N right.
  3. Apply Newton’s second law: a = 60 ÷ 12 = 5.0 m/s² right.
  4. Because the cart starts at rest, v = v0 + at = 0 + 5.0 × 3 = 15 m/s right.
  5. Check units: N/kg = m/s², and (m/s²) × s = m/s.

This example assumes constant acceleration. In a real cart, resistance and motor force might change with speed. The simplified model is still useful for testing the basic relationship between force, mass, and acceleration.

SECTION 06

Plan a repeatable investigation

Before collecting data, write down your hypothesis, the independent variable, the dependent variable, and the controls. Then select several settings spread across a useful range. Using only two settings may show a difference, but five or more settings make a pattern easier to recognize.

For Experiment A, try a cart mass of 10 kg and a resistance force of 10 N. Use driving forces of 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 N. The corresponding net forces are 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 N. Record the acceleration for each trial. For Experiment B, keep driving force at 60 N and resistance at 10 N. Change mass through 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 kg.

Repeat each trial when working with measured data. The simulator offers an optional measurement-variation setting to imitate small differences between repeated readings. When variation is off, the mathematical model is deterministic: identical inputs always produce identical results. This distinction matters because repeating a calculation is not the same as collecting independent physical measurements.

SECTION 08

Interpret your results

In Experiment A, plot net force on the horizontal axis and acceleration on the vertical axis. With mass held constant, the graph should form a straight line through or near the origin. Its slope is acceleration divided by net force, which equals 1/mass. For a 10 kg cart, the ideal slope is 0.10 (m/s²)/N.

In Experiment B, plot mass on the horizontal axis and acceleration on the vertical axis. With net force held constant, the graph curves downward. Doubling mass halves acceleration. This is an inverse relationship, so it is not expected to form a straight line when acceleration is graphed directly against mass.

If repeated measured readings differ slightly, calculate the mean acceleration at each setting. Compare that average with the model prediction. Differences can come from measurement variation, imperfect control of conditions, or an oversimplified model. Do not call a result “wrong” simply because it differs slightly from a prediction; explain the size and possible causes of the difference.

FINAL EXAM CONNECTION · GRAPH AND DATA ANALYSIS

Interpret resistance thresholds and changing mass

A graph of acceleration against applied force can show an apparent starting threshold when resistance prevents a cart from accelerating at smaller applied forces. In a simplified model with 15 N of constant opposing resistance during forward motion, the forward acceleration above that threshold is a = (Fapplied − 15)/m. For a 10 kg cart, applied forces of 25, 35, and 45 N produce accelerations of 1, 2, and 3 m/s². Extending that straight-line relationship backward reaches zero acceleration at an applied force of 15 N: the horizontal intercept identifies the modeled resistance threshold. This is a model for forward motion; actual static friction may behave differently before sliding begins.

Do not confuse a force–acceleration graph with an acceleration–net-force graph. If net force is on the horizontal axis, zero net force corresponds to zero acceleration. If applied force is on the horizontal axis and resistance must be overcome, a positive intercept may appear. Always read the axis labels before interpreting the graph.

Worked example · Infer mass, then add a payload

A cart accelerates at 0.5 m/s² under 20 N of net force, 1.0 m/s² under 40 N, and 1.5 m/s² under 60 N. The ratio Fnet/a is 40 kg in every trial, so the cart's mass is 40 kg. Engineers then add a 20 kg payload, making the new mass 60 kg. At the same 60 N net force, the new acceleration is a = 60/60 = 1.0 m/s². A greater mass reduces acceleration when net force stays the same.

Practice · Interpret a new set of trials

A second cart accelerates at 2 m/s² under a 100 N net force. A 25 kg payload is added while the same net force is maintained. Find the new acceleration to two decimal places.

Show worked solution

Original mass = 100/2 = 50 kg. New mass = 50 + 25 = 75 kg. New acceleration = 100/75 = 1.33 m/s² (to two decimal places).

SECTION 09

Turn evidence into an engineering decision

Suppose a warehouse cart must accelerate at least 2.0 m/s² while carrying a 25 kg load, and its resistance force is 15 N. What minimum driving force does the motor need? Begin with Newton’s second law: Fnet = ma = 25 × 2.0 = 50 N. The motor must overcome resistance as well, so Fdrive = 50 + 15 = 65 N.

Now consider a larger load. If mass rises to 35 kg while the same 65 N drive and 15 N resistance remain, net force stays 50 N but acceleration becomes 50/35 ≈ 1.43 m/s². The cart no longer meets the 2.0 m/s² target. The engineer could choose a stronger motor, reduce resistance, or limit the carried mass. Each choice has trade-offs in cost, energy use, and performance.

An engineering conclusion should state whether the design meets the requirement and support that judgment with calculations or test results. It should also explain what further testing would be needed before using the cart in a real warehouse.

INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Independent practice and worked solutions

Use the model Fnet = Fdrive − Fresist and g = 9.8 m/s² when needed. Work each problem before opening the solution.

A · Net force and acceleration

A 15 kg cart has a 75 N drive and 15 N resistance. Find net force and acceleration.

Show solution

Net force = 75 − 15 = 60 N forward. Acceleration = 60/15 = 4.0 m/s² forward.

B · Change mass

The net force stays 60 N, but cart mass doubles from 15 kg to 30 kg. What is the new acceleration?

Show solution

a = 60/30 = 2.0 m/s², half the original acceleration.

C · Required driving force

A 20 kg cart must accelerate at 3.0 m/s² against 12 N of resistance. What drive force is required?

Show solution

Required net force = 20 × 3 = 60 N. Drive force = 60 + 12 = 72 N.

D · Motion after time

A cart starts at rest and accelerates at 2.5 m/s² for 4 seconds. Find final speed and distance traveled.

Show solution

v = at = 2.5 × 4 = 10 m/s. Distance = ½at² = ½ × 2.5 × 16 = 20 m.

E · Identify variables

You want to test whether doubling net force doubles acceleration. Which quantity should you change, which should you measure, and what should remain constant?

Show solution

Independent variable: net force. Dependent variable: acceleration. Keep mass constant; also control track conditions and resistance.

F · Interpret a graph

For a cart with constant mass, net force rises from 20 N to 40 N and acceleration rises from 2 m/s² to 4 m/s². What relationship do the data support? What is the mass?

Show solution

Acceleration is directly proportional to net force. Mass = F/a = 20/2 = 10 kg.

VOCABULARY & GLOSSARY

Vocabulary and key concepts

Hypothesis

A testable prediction about how variables are related.

Independent variable

The factor intentionally changed during an experiment.

Dependent variable

The measured or calculated response.

Controlled variable

A condition kept the same to make comparisons fair.

Net force

The vector sum of all forces acting on an object.

Acceleration

The rate at which velocity changes; measured in m/s².

Direct relationship

A relationship in which one quantity increases proportionally with another when other conditions stay constant.

Inverse relationship

A relationship in which one quantity decreases as another increases, such as a = F/m at constant net force.

Measurement variation

Differences between repeated observations that may result from measurement uncertainty or changing conditions.

QUESTION 1 OF 12

Lesson summary

Engineers investigate motion by defining a testable question, controlling variables, recording data, and comparing results with predictions. A cart’s horizontal acceleration depends on net force and mass: a = Fnet/m. At constant mass, acceleration rises directly with net force; at constant net force, acceleration falls as mass increases. Free-body diagrams, careful units, repeatable procedures, and well-labeled graphs help turn calculations into reliable evidence. An engineering conclusion explains whether a design meets its requirements and identifies the limits of the model or experiment.
