Section 01

Why mechanical engineers measure motion over time

First, picture the equipment.

A warehouse stores parts and products. An automated guided vehicle (AGV) is a motorized cart that carries those parts along a planned route, often without a person driving it. A conveyor is a moving belt or roller system that carries objects from one place to another. A linear actuator is a machine part that pushes or pulls in a straight line.

All of these machines move, but an engineer needs to know more than where they end. The engineer must also know how long the movement takes. A cart that travels 20 meters in 5 seconds performs differently from one that needs 20 seconds for the same trip.

In A7.1, you learned that distance is all the travel added together, while displacement is the change from start to finish. Keep those two ideas in mind. They lead to two different measurements of how fast something moves.

Imagine a warehouse where an automated guided vehicle (AGV) carries machine parts between storage racks and assembly stations. The AGV must complete deliveries quickly, remain inside its assigned aisle, and approach workers and equipment safely. A position sensor tells the controller where the cart is, but position alone cannot tell the engineer how quickly the cart moves. To evaluate transport performance, the engineer must combine a change in location with a measurement of elapsed time.

This lesson extends A7.1 by distinguishing average speed, which describes the rate of total travel, from average velocity, which describes the rate of signed displacement. The difference becomes especially important when a robot reverses direction, completes a round trip, or starts away from the origin. Engineers use these quantities when comparing conveyor throughput, selecting actuators, evaluating machine travel, and interpreting sensor logs.

Section 02

Start with a reference direction and a clock

What does elapsed time mean?

Elapsed time is simply how much time passes. If a cart starts moving when the stopwatch reads 3 seconds and finishes when it reads 11 seconds, the trip lasted 11 − 3 = 8 seconds. We subtract the earlier reading from the later reading, just as we subtract a starting position from an ending position.

The symbol Δt is pronounced “delta t” and means change in time. The letter t stands for time. Engineers use the abbreviation s for seconds and m for meters. So m/s is read “meters per second,” meaning how many meters are covered during one second on average.

For the warehouse cart, define east as the positive x direction. Positions to the east of the loading station are positive; positions to the west are negative. Time measurements begin when the observation interval starts. The elapsed time is the difference between the ending and starting timestamps: Δt = tf − ti.

Always check that elapsed time is positive and that your distance and displacement units match the time unit. A cart traveling 20 meters in 10 seconds can be analyzed in m/s. If the controller records millimeters and milliseconds, the engineer must convert units consistently before comparing results to a specification written in m/s.

Elapsed time: Δt = tfinal − tinitial. For the calculations in this lesson, use seconds (s).
Section 03

Average speed measures total travel per time

Why do we divide distance by time?

Suppose a cart travels 12 meters in 4 seconds. Imagine dividing the trip into four equal one-second portions. On average, it covers 3 meters during each second because 12 ÷ 4 = 3. We say its average speed is 3 m/s. The word average matters: the cart might have started slowly and sped up later. We know only the total distance and total time.

How to solve: Step 1: Find total distance. Step 2: Find total elapsed time. Step 3: Divide distance by time. Step 4: write m/s. For 30 m in 10 s, average speed = 30 ÷ 10 = 3 m/s.

Important: Speed does not include a direction. A cart moving 3 m/s to the right and one moving 3 m/s to the left have the same speed.

Average speed is the total distance traveled divided by total elapsed time. Because distance is never negative, average speed cannot be negative. It does not identify direction. A cart moving east at 2 m/s and a cart moving west at 2 m/s have the same speed even though their velocities differ.

Average speed = total distance ÷ elapsed time

Average speed = dtotal/Δt

Worked example 1: A conveyor inspection cart travels 90 m over 30 s. Its average speed is 90/30 = 3.0 m/s. The engineer can compare this rate to the machine's travel-time requirement, but the calculation does not reveal which direction the cart moved.

Worked example 2: A service robot travels 18 m east and then 6 m west in a total of 12 s. The total distance is 18 + 6 = 24 m. Its average speed is 24/12 = 2.0 m/s. The return movement contributes to the distance and must not be subtracted.

Section 04

Average velocity measures displacement per time

Why do we also need velocity?

Suppose two carts each travel 10 meters in 5 seconds. Cart A moves 10 meters right. Cart B moves 10 meters left. Both have average speed 10 ÷ 5 = 2 m/s. But they are not going the same way. To describe motion with direction, engineers use velocity.

If right is positive, Cart A has average velocity +2 m/s and Cart B has average velocity −2 m/s. The sign tells the direction; it does not mean Cart B is slower.

How to solve: Step 1: Find the starting and ending positions. Step 2: Subtract starting position from ending position to get signed displacement. Step 3: Divide that displacement by elapsed time. Step 4: explain what the positive or negative sign means.

Average velocity is the signed displacement divided by elapsed time. The sign indicates the direction of net movement relative to the chosen axis. Unlike average speed, average velocity can be positive, negative, or zero. In one-dimensional motion, its magnitude is no greater than the average speed over the same time interval.

Average velocity: v̄ = Δx/Δt = (xf − xi)/(tf − ti)

Worked example 3: The service robot from the previous example moves +18 m, then −6 m in 12 s. Its displacement is +12 m, so its average velocity is 12/12 = +1.0 m/s (east). Its average speed is 2.0 m/s. Both values are correct because they answer different questions.

Worked example 4: A maintenance cart moves from +14 m to −10 m in 8 s. Its displacement is −10 − 14 = −24 m. Average velocity is −24/8 = −3.0 m/s (west). A negative velocity does not mean a negative speed or an error in the machine's operation; it simply indicates direction.

Section 06

Round trips and zero average velocity

Try a round trip yourself.

Walk 6 meters away from a doorway, then 6 meters back to it. You traveled 12 meters in total, so if the walk took 6 seconds your average speed was 12 ÷ 6 = 2 m/s. But you ended exactly where you started. Your displacement was 0 meters, so your average velocity was 0 ÷ 6 = 0 m/s.

This does not mean you stood still. It means the starting and ending positions were the same. An engineer choosing how quickly a delivery cart completes a round trip would look at speed and total trip time, not average velocity alone.

A cart can have a positive average speed while its average velocity is zero. Suppose an inspection robot leaves its home station, travels 25 m east to inspect a valve, and returns 25 m west. If the trip takes 20 s, the total distance is 50 m, so its average speed is 2.5 m/s. The final position equals the initial position, so the displacement is zero and the average velocity is 0 m/s.

This result does not mean the cart stood still. It completed a full service cycle. Engineers may use the average speed to evaluate how quickly the route was completed, but average velocity alone would be a poor measure of the cart's productivity on a round trip.

Section 07

Time includes stops, waits, and reversals

When calculating average speed or average velocity over an entire observation interval, include all elapsed time, including pauses. A machine's travel-time specification may account for loading, unloading, dwell times, and direction changes. Ignoring stopped time would overstate its overall average speed.

Worked example 6: A robot travels 20 m east in 5 s, waits at a scanner for 5 s, then travels 10 m west in 5 s. Total elapsed time is 15 s. Total distance is 30 m, so average speed is 30/15 = 2.0 m/s. Net displacement is +10 m, so average velocity is 10/15 ≈ +0.67 m/s east.

The robot's speed during the first moving segment is 4 m/s, but its overall average speed is 2 m/s. Segment speed and whole-trip average speed must not be confused.

Section 08

Average versus instantaneous motion

What is the difference between average and instantaneous?

Think about the speed display in a car. It tells you roughly how fast you are moving right now. That is an instantaneous speed. Your average speed for the whole drive might be lower because you waited at stoplights. A machine works the same way: a cart might briefly move quickly even though its average over a long trip is slow.

Why does this matter? A machine's brakes must be able to handle how fast the cart is actually moving when it needs to stop. Knowing only its whole-trip average is not enough to design a safe stopping system.

An average describes an interval; instantaneous speed and instantaneous velocity describe motion at a particular moment. A motion sensor may report the cart's current velocity every fraction of a second, while a shift report may calculate average speed from cumulative travel and elapsed time.

These measurements support different engineering decisions. Average speed can estimate delivery throughput, but an emergency stopping controller needs information about the cart's current speed, braking capability, load, and available stopping distance. A robot with an average speed of 2 m/s might have reached 5 m/s briefly, so the average alone is not sufficient for a safety decision.

In A7.3, students will investigate how velocity changes with time and how to read motion graphs. For now, remember that an interval average does not prove that the machine moved at a constant speed throughout that interval.

Section 09

Units: meters per second and kilometers per hour

Understanding the conversion instead of memorizing it.

A kilometer is 1,000 meters. An hour is 3,600 seconds. Therefore, 1 km/h means 1,000 meters traveled in 3,600 seconds, or about 0.278 m/s. That is why converting km/h to m/s means dividing by 3.6.

Example: 36 km/h ÷ 3.6 = 10 m/s. To go the other direction, multiply: 10 m/s × 3.6 = 36 km/h. Always write the unit after the number so a reader knows what was measured.

In engineering calculations, speed and velocity are often expressed in meters per second (m/s). Transportation specifications may use kilometers per hour (km/h). To convert km/h to m/s, divide by 3.6. To convert m/s to km/h, multiply by 3.6.

1 m/s = 3.6 km/h

m/s = km/h ÷ 3.6; km/h = m/s × 3.6

Worked example 7: A transport cart is specified at 18 km/h. Convert to m/s: 18 ÷ 3.6 = 5.0 m/s. If it traveled at that constant speed for 4 s, it would cover 20 m.

Worked example 8: A conveyor cart's average speed is 1.5 m/s. Convert to km/h: 1.5 × 3.6 = 5.4 km/h. Engineers should keep units visible in every calculation and check that the resulting magnitude is reasonable for the equipment.

Section 10

Use a machine travel log to verify performance

How to read a motion log:

A motion log is simply a table showing where a machine was at different times. Read one row at a time. If a cart is at 0 m at 0 s and at +12 m at 6 s, it moved 12 m right in 6 s. Its average velocity for that part is +12 ÷ 6 = +2 m/s. If the next row says +4 m at 10 s, it moved from +12 to +4: that is −8 m in 4 s, or −2 m/s.

To calculate the whole trip, don't average those two velocity numbers directly. Instead, find the total displacement from the first to the last coordinate, then divide by the total elapsed time. This prevents a common mistake when segments last different amounts of time.

Automated machines frequently log positions and timestamps. An engineer can calculate each segment's displacement from consecutive position readings, its travel distance from the absolute displacement, and its average velocity from displacement divided by segment duration. The total distance across a route is the sum of segment distances, while the total displacement is the final coordinate minus the initial coordinate.

Suppose a cart is at x = 0 m at t = 0 s, x = +12 m at t = 6 s, and x = +4 m at t = 10 s. Segment one covers 12 m in 6 s, with average velocity +2.0 m/s. Segment two covers 8 m west in 4 s, with average velocity −2.0 m/s. Over the entire 10 s, total distance is 20 m, average speed is 2.0 m/s, displacement is +4 m, and average velocity is +0.4 m/s.

This example shows that equal-magnitude segment velocities in opposite directions do not imply zero whole-trip average velocity unless the net displacement is zero.

Section 12

Interpret the results as a mechanical engineer

Suppose two warehouse carts both travel 24 m in 12 s. Both have an average speed of 2 m/s. Cart A travels 24 m east without reversing, so its average velocity is +2 m/s. Cart B travels 12 m east and 12 m west, so its average velocity is 0 m/s. The equal speeds describe equal accumulated travel per time; the different velocities describe different net changes in position.

If an engineer is measuring how far the cart's drive wheels have rolled, average speed is useful. If the engineer is measuring progress toward a destination, average velocity and final position are useful. Neither quantity alone establishes throughput, safety, or actuator suitability. The design specification determines what must be measured.

The virtual lab uses idealized commanded movements and time intervals. Real machines may experience wheel slip, encoder quantization, delays, acceleration, and changing loads. The lesson's calculations are correct for the specified input model, but engineers verify actual performance with appropriate sensors.

Section 13

Common mistakes in motion calculations

Using displacement in the speed formula: Average speed requires total distance, not net displacement. Using distance in the velocity formula: Average velocity requires signed displacement. Dropping the sign: A negative average velocity is a meaningful direction, not an impossible speed. Ignoring waiting time: Dwell time is part of the elapsed interval for a whole-trip average.

Confusing average and instantaneous speed: A cart can accelerate and stop while still having a moderate average speed. Forgetting the origin: The final coordinate is not always the displacement, particularly when the cart starts somewhere other than zero. Mixing units: A numerical result in m/s cannot be compared directly with km/h without conversion.

Section 14

Independent engineering practice

Complete these problems before expanding the explanations. Show the motion quantities, formula, substitution, units, and direction when applicable.

1. A machine cart travels 120 m in 30 s. Find its average speed.

Average speed = 120/30 = 4.0 m/s.

2. A cart moves from x = 5 m to x = 65 m in 20 s. Find average velocity.

Δx = 65 − 5 = +60 m; v̄ = 60/20 = +3.0 m/s.

3. A robot moves 40 m east, then 10 m west, in 10 s. Find speed and velocity.

Distance = 50 m; displacement = +30 m. Average speed = 5.0 m/s; average velocity = +3.0 m/s.

4. A shuttle travels 25 m east then 25 m west in 20 s. Find both averages.

Distance = 50 m, so average speed = 2.5 m/s. Displacement = 0 m, so average velocity = 0 m/s.

5. East is positive. A robot moves from +18 m to −6 m in 8 s. Find average velocity.

Δx = −6 − 18 = −24 m. Average velocity = −3.0 m/s (west).

6. A conveyor moves packages 90 m in 45 s without reversing. Find average speed.

90/45 = 2.0 m/s.

7. Convert 72 km/h to m/s.

72 ÷ 3.6 = 20 m/s.

8. Convert 5 m/s to km/h.

5 × 3.6 = 18 km/h.

9. A linear actuator moves 0.6 m in 3 s at a constant speed. Find its speed.

0.6/3 = 0.20 m/s.

10. A cart averages −2.0 m/s over 12 s. Find its displacement.

Δx = v̄Δt = −2.0 × 12 = −24 m.

11. A robot moves 15 m east in 5 s and 15 m west in 10 s. Find average speed.

Total distance = 30 m; total time = 15 s; average speed = 2.0 m/s.

12. For problem 11, find average velocity.

Displacement = 0 m, so average velocity = 0 m/s.

13. Two carts have average speed 3 m/s. Must their average velocities match?

No. Average velocity depends on signed displacement; the carts may move in opposite directions or take different routes.

14. Why does an engineer need more than average speed to specify an emergency stopping system?

Stopping design also requires instantaneous speed, deceleration capability, reaction/control delays, available stopping distance, load, and operating conditions.

15. A cart moves 24 m east in 6 s, pauses 4 s, then moves 12 m west in 6 s. Find average speed and velocity.

Distance = 36 m; displacement = +12 m; elapsed time = 16 s. Average speed = 2.25 m/s; average velocity = +0.75 m/s.

Section 15

Vocabulary glossary

Select each term to review its meaning in mechanical-engineering motion analysis.

Average speed

Total distance traveled divided by the total elapsed time; a nonnegative scalar.

Average velocity

Signed displacement divided by elapsed time in one-dimensional motion.

Instantaneous speed

Magnitude of motion rate at a particular moment.

Instantaneous velocity

Rate of change of position at a particular moment, including direction.

Displacement

Signed change in position: Δx = xfinal − xinitial.

Total distance

Sum of the absolute lengths of all traveled motion segments.

Elapsed time

Difference between final and initial time readings.

Reference frame

Specified coordinate origin and positive direction.

Dwell time

Time a machine remains stationary during a process or route.

Motion log

Recorded sequence of positions and times from a machine or sensor.

Conveyor

Mechanism used to move materials along a prescribed path.

Automated guided vehicle

Mobile industrial transport machine following a planned route.

Linear actuator

Machine component producing motion along a straight line.

Throughput

Amount of work or material processed per unit time; not necessarily equal to cart speed.

Motion sensor

Device used to measure or estimate position, velocity, or related motion quantities.

Section 17

Lesson summary and next engineering question

Average speed describes total travel distance per elapsed time. Average velocity describes signed displacement per elapsed time. Both use time, but they answer different engineering questions. The two averages have equal magnitudes only when the path does not reverse along the one-dimensional axis. A round trip can produce a positive average speed and zero average velocity. Stopped time must be included when calculating whole-trip averages.

Mechanical engineers apply these distinctions to conveyor systems, robot travel, actuator performance, and sensor data.
