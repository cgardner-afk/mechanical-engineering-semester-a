Section 01

The engineering design decision

An industrial lifting fixture uses a steel pin to connect a lifting link to a bracket. The fixture must carry a specified working load, fit within a limited space, and stay within a material budget. A pin that appears strong enough in a simple static calculation may still be unsuitable if it has inadequate design margin, experiences repeated loads, or is installed in a joint with unfavorable geometry.

Mechanical engineers address this problem by defining a design load, identifying the relevant failure mode, calculating the resulting stress, and comparing that stress with an allowable stress. They then evaluate practical alternatives against cost, mass, geometry, and service requirements. This lesson introduces factor-of-safety calculations and direct shear in mechanical connections. The purpose is not to memorize a single formula but to justify a defensible component choice.

Section 02

What a factor of safety means

A factor of safety (FoS, sometimes called design factor) compares a specified strength limit to the calculated working stress for a particular failure mode. In a simple yield-based axial check, FoS = yield strength / working stress. A larger value indicates a greater margin relative to that particular strength criterion. It does not guarantee safety against fatigue, buckling, corrosion, manufacturing defects, or incorrect load assumptions.

For example, if a component's specified yield strength is 300 MPa and its calculated working stress is 100 MPa, the yield-based FoS is 3.0. If the working stress rises to 150 MPa, the factor falls to 2.0. Engineers select required factors according to relevant standards, uncertainties, consequences of failure, and loading conditions. No single factor is appropriate for every machine.

Engineering distinction: A calculated factor of safety is the margin a proposed design currently has. A required factor of safety is the minimum margin specified by the design requirements. A design passes the simplified check only when the calculated factor meets or exceeds the required factor.
Section 03

Allowable stress and the design requirement

Engineers often rearrange the factor-of-safety relationship to calculate an allowable stress. For a simple yield-based normal-stress criterion, σallow = Sy/n, where Sy is specified yield strength and n is the required factor of safety. The working stress must satisfy |σ| ≤ σallow.

Worked example 1: A steel tie rod has a specified yield strength of 360 MPa. The project requires n = 3.0 against yielding. The allowable normal stress is 360/3 = 120 MPa. A working stress of 95 MPa passes this simplified check, while 135 MPa fails. Both results still require other relevant failure-mode checks.

When a problem provides an allowable stress directly, use that value. Do not apply another safety factor unless the problem explicitly instructs you to do so; doing so would count the design margin twice.

Section 04

Design a tension member for the required margin

From A6.2, average axial normal stress is σ = F/A. Combining this with the allowable-stress criterion gives Amin = Fn/Sy for a simplified yield-based axial design. This equation sizes a theoretical minimum area for the specified working force and factor of safety.

Worked example 2: A machine lifting link carries 18,000 N. The material has specified yield strength Sy = 300 MPa, and the required factor is 2.5. The allowable stress is 300/2.5 = 120 MPa. Therefore Amin = 18,000/120 = 150 mm². A proposed 12 mm × 12 mm section provides 144 mm² and fails this check; a 15 mm × 12 mm section provides 180 mm² and passes.

The larger section is not automatically a finished design. Holes, bending, fatigue, tolerances, and connection geometry can change the governing condition.

Step-by-step method · Size a safe tension member

  1. Step 1: Given F = 18,000 N, yield strength 300 MPa, required FoS 2.5.
  2. Step 2: Allowable stress = 300/2.5 = 120 MPa.
  3. Step 3: Required area = 18,000/120 = 150 mm².
  4. Step 4: 12 × 12 = 144 mm² fails; 15 × 12 = 180 mm² passes.
  5. Step 5: Check holes, bending, fatigue, and connections separately.
Section 05

Shear stress in pins and connections

Not every machine part carries pure axial tension. A pin connecting two plates can resist forces that tend to slide one portion of the pin past another. This is direct shear. The average shear stress is τ = V/A, where V is the shear force on a specified shear plane and A is the area of that plane. Shear stress is commonly reported in MPa when force is in N and area is in mm².

For a round pin with diameter d, the area of one shear plane is A = πd²/4. A connection in single shear has one primary shear plane; a symmetrical clevis-style connection in double shear has two. In an ideal equal-load-sharing double-shear model, each plane carries half the applied force, so τ = F/(2A). Actual connections require additional checks for bearing, bending, unequal load distribution, plate tear-out, and other failure modes.

Section 06

Worked example 3 · Single-shear pin

A bracket pin carries a transverse load of 8,000 N through one idealized shear plane. The pin diameter is 12 mm. First calculate the area: A = π(12²)/4 ≈ 113.10 mm². Average shear stress is τ = 8,000/113.10 ≈ 70.7 MPa.

If the project specifies an allowable average shear stress of 65 MPa, the proposed pin fails this screening check. Increasing the diameter increases the shear area with the square of diameter. For example, a 14 mm pin has area ≈ 153.94 mm² and stress ≈ 52.0 MPa, which passes the same simplified shear criterion.

Step-by-step method · Single-shear pin

  1. Step 1: Given F = 8,000 N, diameter 12 mm, one shear plane.
  2. Step 2: Area A = π(12²)/4 ≈ 113.10 mm².
  3. Step 3: Shear stress τ = 8,000/113.10 ≈ 70.7 MPa.
  4. Step 4: 70.7 > 65 MPa allowable: 12 mm fails.
  5. Step 5: At 14 mm, τ ≈ 52.0 MPa, which passes the stated average-shear check.
Section 07

Worked example 4 · Double-shear clevis pin

Now suppose a symmetrical clevis joint carries the same 8,000 N through two equal shear planes using the same 12 mm pin. The total idealized resisting area is 2 × 113.10 = 226.20 mm². Average shear stress is 8,000/226.20 ≈ 35.4 MPa.

Under the ideal equal-sharing assumption, double shear halves the average stress compared with single shear for the same load and pin diameter. It does not necessarily halve every other stress in the connection. Engineers must still evaluate bearing between the pin and plates, pin bending, edge distance, and how accurately the load is shared.

Step-by-step method · Double-shear pin

  1. Step 1: Count two shear planes with ideal equal load sharing.
  2. Step 2: Area per plane for 12 mm pin ≈ 113.10 mm².
  3. Step 3: Total area 2A ≈ 226.20 mm².
  4. Step 4: Shear stress = 8,000/226.20 ≈ 35.4 MPa.
  5. Step 5: Compare with single shear 70.7 MPa; check other joint failure modes.
Section 08

Minimum pin diameter from allowable shear

For a solid circular pin in single shear, the allowable-stress requirement τ = F/A ≤ τallow gives dmin = √(4F/(πτallow)). For an idealized double-shear connection, replace the resisting area A with 2A, giving dmin = √(2F/(πτallow)).

Worked example 5: A single-shear pin must carry 12,000 N with allowable shear stress 75 MPa. The required area is 12,000/75 = 160 mm². The theoretical minimum diameter is √(4 × 160/π) ≈ 14.27 mm. A 14 mm pin would not pass, because its area is less than 160 mm². A 16 mm standard-size candidate provides approximately 201.06 mm² and passes this simplified check.

Never round a calculated minimum diameter down when selecting a standard size. Select a diameter at or above the minimum, then perform the remaining design checks.

Step-by-step method · Minimum pin diameter

  1. Step 1: Given F = 12,000 N, allowable shear 75 MPa, single shear.
  2. Step 2: Required area A_min = 12,000/75 = 160 mm².
  3. Step 3: Rearrange d_min = √(4A_min/π).
  4. Step 4: Calculate d_min ≈ 14.27 mm.
  5. Step 5: Do not round down; choose 16 mm and verify its stress is below 75 MPa.
Section 09

Material selection: strength is not the only requirement

Two materials may both satisfy the same allowable-stress requirement but differ in density, stiffness, corrosion resistance, manufacturability, availability, and cost. A high-strength material may allow a smaller cross section, but that section might deform too much, wear rapidly, or be difficult to machine. A low-density material may reduce moving mass but require a larger diameter to satisfy stiffness or fatigue requirements.

For a lifting pin, the engineer should identify the governing failure modes before choosing material. Shear strength, bearing behavior, fatigue performance, surface hardness, corrosion exposure, and fit with mating parts can all matter. For a machine support rod, axial yielding and elastic extension may dominate instead. This is why material selection is a structured engineering decision rather than a ranking of “strongest” materials.

Section 10

Worked example 6 · Compare feasible designs

A factory needs a pin for an idealized single-shear joint carrying 10,000 N. The project specifies an allowable average shear stress of 60 MPa and a material budget of $28 per pin. Three candidates are offered: A is a 12 mm pin costing $12; B is a 16 mm pin costing $21; C is a 20 mm pin costing $34. For this classroom comparison, all candidates are assumed to have the same specified allowable shear stress.

Candidate A has area ≈ 113.10 mm² and average shear stress ≈ 88.4 MPa: it fails. Candidate B has area ≈ 201.06 mm² and stress ≈ 49.7 MPa: it passes stress and budget. Candidate C has area ≈ 314.16 mm² and stress ≈ 31.8 MPa: it passes stress but exceeds budget. Therefore B is the lowest-cost feasible choice among these three candidates under the stated constraints.

A complete engineering selection would also check bearing, bending, fatigue, installation tolerances, and any applicable standard requirements before approving the actual joint.

Section 12

Independent engineering practice

Try each problem before expanding the explanation. Show units and state what the result means for the design.

1. A link has yield strength 300 MPa and working stress 100 MPa. Find its yield-based factor of safety.

FoS = 300/100 = 3.0 for this yield criterion.

2. A design requires n = 2.5 against yielding. The specified yield strength is 350 MPa. Find allowable stress.

σallow = 350/2.5 = 140 MPa.

3. A tension link carries 21,000 N with allowable stress 105 MPa. Find minimum area.

Amin = 21,000/105 = 200 mm².

4. A 10 mm diameter pin carries 6,000 N in single shear. Find average shear stress.

A = π(10²)/4 ≈ 78.54 mm²; τ ≈ 6,000/78.54 = 76.4 MPa.

5. What is the idealized double-shear stress for the same pin and load?

τ = 6,000/(2 × 78.54) ≈ 38.2 MPa.

6. A pin must carry 9,000 N in single shear at an allowable shear stress of 50 MPa. Find minimum area.

Amin = 9,000/50 = 180 mm².

7. A pin has a calculated minimum diameter of 14.27 mm. Available diameters are 12, 14, and 16 mm. Which is the smallest passing option?

16 mm. Do not round the minimum diameter down.

8. A candidate passes shear stress but exceeds budget. Does it satisfy all constraints?

No. A design is feasible only if it meets every stated requirement.

9. Why might a real double-shear connection not divide load exactly equally?

Geometry, clearance, manufacturing tolerances, stiffness differences, and eccentric loading may affect load sharing.

10. Why does a passing average shear-stress check not certify a pin joint?

Pin bending, bearing, fatigue, plate tear-out, wear, and other relevant modes have not been checked.

Section 13

Vocabulary glossary

Expand each engineering term to review its definition.

Factor of safety

Ratio of a specified strength criterion to calculated working stress for a particular failure mode.

Required safety factor

Minimum factor specified by design requirements or applicable standards.

Allowable stress

Maximum stress permitted by a stated design criterion.

Yield strength

Stress associated with a specified onset of plastic deformation.

Normal stress

Force per area acting perpendicular to a cross section.

Shear stress

Force per area acting tangentially along a section.

Single shear

Connection configuration with one principal idealized shear plane.

Double shear

Connection configuration with two principal idealized shear planes.

Shear plane

Section across which parts of a connection tend to slide relative to one another.

Pin bearing

Contact pressure between a pin and the hole surfaces of mating parts.

Design constraint

Requirement a candidate must satisfy, such as stress, cost, or size.

Feasible design

Candidate meeting all stated design constraints.

Governing failure mode

Failure mechanism that sets the most restrictive requirement.

Design margin

Quantified separation between working demand and a specified limit.

Section 15

Lesson summary

Mechanical engineers calculate factors of safety and allowable stresses to compare working loads against stated material limits. For an axial member, a simplified yield-based requirement can be written as σallow = Sy/n. For a direct-shear pin, τ = F/(mA), where m is the number of equally loaded shear planes. These relationships help engineers estimate minimum cross sections and screen candidate connections.

A design must satisfy all stated constraints, not merely the stress check. A passing classroom calculation is a preliminary result, not a safety certification. In the next lesson, you will bring material selection, stress, deformation, and failure considerations together in a more comprehensive engineering investigation.
