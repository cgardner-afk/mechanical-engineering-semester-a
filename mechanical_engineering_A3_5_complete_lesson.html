SECTION 01

Why Engineers Graph Data

A calculated prediction is only the beginning of engineering. Real machines must be tested. Measurements show whether a design meets requirements, and graphs reveal relationships that a long list of numbers can hide. If a spring extends more than predicted or a motor gets too hot, the engineer must investigate rather than assume the model is correct.

A company asks you to test whether a machine mount bends too far under load. You measure deflection at several loads, graph the results, and compare them with the design limit.

SECTION 02

Variables and Fair Experiments

The independent variable is the quantity you deliberately change. The dependent variable is the response you measure. Controlled variables are conditions you keep consistent. In a spring experiment, applied force is independent and extension is dependent. Keep the spring, measurement method, and temperature consistent. Record each trial in a table with units rather than relying on memory.

You are testing a spring for a machine latch. At 2 N you record extensions of 3.8, 4.0, and 4.2 mm. At 4 N you record 7.9, 8.0, and 8.1 mm. Repeated trials help you evaluate consistency.

SECTION 03

Reading and Constructing Graphs

A scatter plot displays paired measurements. Usually the independent variable belongs on the horizontal x-axis and the dependent variable on the vertical y-axis. Label each axis with its quantity and unit. A point (4,8) on a force-extension graph means that 4 N produced 8 mm of extension. Use readable scales and a descriptive title. A trend line summarizes the relationship but does not prove the model is perfect. Interpolation estimates between tested points; extrapolation predicts outside the tested range and is less reliable.

You graph motor temperature against operating speed to identify a region where heating rises rapidly. That pattern helps you choose the next operating conditions to test.

Which graph should an engineer use?

Different graphs answer different questions. Line graphs emphasize change across an ordered sequence such as time. Scatter plots show individual paired measurements and help reveal a relationship or measurement variation. Bar graphs compare separate categories. A position–time graph is a line or scatter graph with physical meaning: its slope describes velocity. A nonlinear graph is not a separate chart type; it describes a curved relationship in which the slope changes.

Line graph — motor temperature during operation

An engineer records motor temperature at regular times. The upward line indicates continued heating. The actual temperature values and allowable operating limit must be checked before making a safety decision.

Scatter plot — spring force versus extension

Each gold point is an individual measurement. Points cluster around an upward trend, but the small deviations show that actual measurements need not fall exactly on a theoretical line.

Bar graph — comparative material load tests

Each bar represents a different material specimen category. Bar height allows comparison of maximum test loads. A real report must identify the materials and test conditions to make the comparison meaningful.

Position–time graph — conveyor motion

The position increases by 2 m each second. The slope is 2 m/s, describing constant velocity over the illustrated interval.

Nonlinear relationship — braking distance and speed

The curve steepens as speed rises. In an idealized constant-deceleration model, braking distance increases approximately with speed squared. The values here illustrate shape, not actual stopping distances for a vehicle.

SECTION 04

Slope and Engineering Meaning

Slope equals the change in the vertical quantity divided by the change in the horizontal quantity: (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁). Its units matter. The slope of position versus time is measured in m/s and represents speed over the interval. The slope of extension versus force is mm/N. Reversing the axes reverses the ratio. A curved graph has a changing slope; a straight line has constant slope.

YOU ARE THE ENGINEER

A conveyor marker moves from 1.5 m at 2 s to 6.5 m at 7 s. Its average speed is the slope: (6.5−1.5)/(7−2)=1.0 m/s.

Worked example: slope

Spring extension is 4 mm at 2 N and 16 mm at 8 N.

  1. Step 1 — Identify: (2 N,4 mm) and (8 N,16 mm).
  2. Step 2 — Choose formula: slope=(y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁).
  3. Step 3 — Calculate: (16−4)/(8−2)=2 mm/N. The spring extends approximately 2 mm per additional newton.
SECTION 05

Accuracy, Precision, and Resolution

Accuracy describes closeness to a reference value. Precision describes how closely repeated readings agree. Resolution is the smallest difference an instrument can display. A caliper that reads 20.39, 20.40, and 20.41 mm for a reference diameter of 20.00 mm is precise but inaccurate. Displaying more digits does not automatically improve accuracy. Calibration, instrument condition, and technique matter.

A shaft must fit a bearing. Your caliper gives consistent readings but is offset by 0.40 mm. You investigate calibration before approving production.

SECTION 06

Random and Systematic Error

Random variation creates unpredictable differences between readings, such as sensor noise or small differences in placement. Systematic error creates a repeatable bias, such as a scale that reads 0.3 kg high even when unloaded. Averaging repeated readings can summarize random variation, but it does not eliminate a consistent bias. Engineers use calibration, independent checks, and clear procedures to identify errors.

Two load cells disagree by about 1.0 N, while readings from each vary by 0.2 N. You investigate the systematic disagreement separately from the random scatter.

SECTION 07

Mean, Range, and Uncertainty

The mean is the sum of readings divided by their count. The range is the maximum minus the minimum. Five thickness readings of 4.98, 5.01, 5.00, 4.99, and 5.02 mm have a mean of 5.00 mm and range of 0.04 mm. Measurement uncertainty describes doubt associated with a result. The observed range alone is not a complete formal uncertainty analysis; instrument calibration, resolution, and environment may also matter.

YOU ARE THE ENGINEER

A bracket must measure between 4.95 and 5.05 mm. You take several readings, calculate their mean and spread, and evaluate whether further inspection is needed.

Worked example: mean and range

  1. Step 1 — Identify: 4.98, 5.01, 5.00, 4.99, 5.02 mm.
  2. Step 2 — Choose formulas: mean=sum/count; range=max−min.
  3. Step 3 — Calculate: mean=25.00/5=5.00 mm; range=5.02−4.98=0.04 mm.
SECTION 08

Percent Error and Model Validation

Absolute error is |measured−reference|. Percent error is |measured−reference| divided by |reference|, multiplied by 100%. The reference must not be zero. If a model predicts 2.50 mm of deflection and the test shows 2.65 mm, the absolute difference is 0.15 mm and the percent error is 6%. The percentage alone cannot tell you whether the result meets a design requirement.

Your thermal-expansion model predicts 0.80 mm of rod growth, but a test measures 0.88 mm. The 10% difference prompts you to inspect test conditions and model assumptions.

Worked example: percent error

  1. Step 1 — Identify: Prediction 2.50 mm; measurement 2.65 mm.
  2. Step 2 — Choose formula: percent error=|measured−reference|/|reference|×100%.
  3. Step 3 — Calculate: 0.15/2.50×100=6%.
SECTION 09

Outliers and Evidence-Based Conclusions

An outlier is an observation unusually different from other results. Do not delete it simply because it makes a graph look untidy. Check the instrument, units, test notes, and conditions. Correlation between variables does not automatically prove causation. A strong engineering conclusion reports the trend, supporting measurements, tested range, and limitations.

One vibration reading is much higher than four earlier readings. You check whether a bolt loosened or a sensor shifted before deciding whether the machine has a real problem.

Independent Practice

Work through each problem before opening the solution. Identify known quantities, select a formula, substitute with units, and interpret the result in the context of a mechanical system.

You must explain your results to another engineer who needs to reproduce your reasoning.

Slope

Problem: Position changes from 3 m at 2 s to 15 m at 6 s.

Solution: (15−3)/(6−2)=3 m/s.

Problem: Readings: 10.1, 9.9, 10.0 mm.

Solution: Mean=(10.1+9.9+10.0)/3=10.0 mm.

Problem: Readings: 2.4, 2.6, 2.5, 2.7 N.

Solution: Range=2.7−2.4=0.3 N.

Percent error

Problem: Reference 50 N; measured 53 N.

Solution: |53−50|/50×100=6%.

Interpolation

Problem: At 4 N extension is 8 mm; at 6 N it is 12 mm. Estimate at 5 N.

Solution: Assuming a linear relationship, 10 mm.

Outlier

Problem: One vibration reading is unusually high. Should you delete it?

Solution: No. Investigate and document the unusual result.

Precision

Problem: A sensor reads 5.40 N repeatedly when reference is 5.00 N.

Solution: Precise but inaccurate; investigate systematic offset.

Extrapolation

Problem: Predict behavior at 20 N after testing only to 8 N.

Solution: Extrapolation; model validity beyond the tested range is uncertain.

Understanding absolute value before calculating error

Absolute value tells us a number's distance from zero on a number line. Distance cannot be negative. The vertical bars around a number mean “take its absolute value”: |3| = 3 and |−3| = 3. Both 3 and −3 are three units away from zero. Absolute value is not the same as simply deleting a minus sign in every part of an equation: you first calculate what is inside the bars, then take the nonnegative magnitude.

Why do engineers use absolute value when calculating error? Because a measurement can be above or below the reference. If a shaft should measure 20.00 mm, a reading of 20.30 mm has a signed difference of +0.30 mm, while a reading of 19.70 mm has a signed difference of −0.30 mm. Both measurements are 0.30 mm away from the required size. Absolute error reports the size of the difference, regardless of direction.

  1. Step 1 — Subtract: Measured − reference = 19.70 − 20.00 = −0.30 mm.
  2. Step 2 — Apply absolute value: |−0.30 mm| = 0.30 mm.
  3. Step 3 — Interpret: The shaft is 0.30 mm below the reference, but its absolute error is 0.30 mm. The signed difference tells direction; the absolute error tells magnitude.

For percent error, we divide this nonnegative absolute difference by the magnitude of the nonzero reference value and multiply by 100%. If the reference is 20.00 mm, percent error = 0.30/20.00 × 100% = 1.5%. When a reference is zero, this percentage formula is undefined because division by zero is not possible. In that situation, report an absolute difference or use a different comparison appropriate to the task.
