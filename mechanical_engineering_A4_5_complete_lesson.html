SECTION 01

Why friction matters

Imagine a technician pushing a heavy toolbox across a workshop floor. At first, the box does not move. The technician pushes harder, and suddenly it begins sliding. Once it is moving, keeping it sliding may take less force than starting it. This happens because of friction, a contact force that resists sliding or the tendency to slide between two surfaces.

Friction is not always a problem. Tires need friction to grip the road, and brakes depend on friction to slow a vehicle. At the same time, friction in bearings and gears can waste energy as heat and cause wear. Mechanical engineers must understand when friction is useful and when it needs to be reduced.

In this lesson, you will distinguish static friction from kinetic friction, calculate normal force and friction, and combine forces using Newton’s second law. You will also explore how surface conditions affect the force needed to move a machine component.

SECTION 02

Contact forces and the normal force

Contact forces occur when objects interact through physical contact. A table pushes upward on a resting book, and a floor pushes on a standing person. This supporting push is called the normal force, written N. “Normal” means perpendicular to the contact surface, not “ordinary.”

On a level floor, a box that has no vertical acceleration and no other vertical forces has N = mg. For a 30 kg box, N = 30 × 9.8 = 294 N. But if someone pulls upward on the box with 50 N while it remains on the floor, the floor only needs to push upward with 244 N.

ΣFy = N + Fup − mg = 0   →   N = mg − Fup

Do not automatically set the normal force equal to weight. First identify all the vertical forces on the object. On a ramp, the normal force points perpendicular to the slope, and its magnitude is often N = mg cos θ when no other perpendicular forces act.

SECTION 03

Static friction: before sliding begins

Static friction acts when two surfaces are not sliding relative to each other. It adjusts to match the applied force, up to a maximum value. If you push a stationary crate with 40 N and it stays still, static friction is 40 N in the opposite direction—not automatically the maximum possible friction.

0 ≤ fs ≤ μsN    and    fs,max = μsN

The symbol μs (pronounced “mew sub s”) is the coefficient of static friction. It has no units and depends on the two contacting surfaces and their condition. A larger coefficient usually means greater resistance to the start of sliding.

Worked example · Will the crate move?

A 20 kg crate sits on a level floor. The coefficient of static friction is 0.40. A worker pushes horizontally with 60 N.

  1. Normal force: N = mg = 20 × 9.8 = 196 N.
  2. Maximum static friction: fs,max = 0.40 × 196 = 78.4 N.
  3. The push (60 N) is less than 78.4 N, so the crate does not slide.
  4. The actual static friction is 60 N opposite the push, making the net horizontal force zero.
SECTION 04

Kinetic friction: while surfaces slide

Kinetic friction acts when surfaces slide past each other. In the simplified model used in this course, its magnitude is approximately constant for a given pair of surfaces and normal force. It is calculated using the coefficient of kinetic friction, μk.

fk = μkN

For many dry surface pairs, μk is smaller than μs. That helps explain why an object can be harder to start moving than to keep sliding. Friction acts opposite the relative sliding direction, which is not always opposite the object's overall motion through a room.

Worked example · Sliding crate

A 20 kg crate slides right on a level floor with μk = 0.25. A worker continues pushing right with 90 N.

  1. Normal force: N = 20 × 9.8 = 196 N.
  2. Kinetic friction: fk = 0.25 × 196 = 49 N left.
  3. Net force: ΣFx = 90 − 49 = 41 N right.
  4. Acceleration: a = 41 ÷ 20 = 2.05 m/s² right.
FINAL EXAM CONNECTION · DATA INTERPRETATION

Read a friction experiment from measurements

Static friction is not a single fixed force: while a crate remains at rest, it increases to match the applied horizontal pull, up to a maximum. Once sliding begins, the kinetic friction force can be smaller. Engineers can recognize this change from a sequence of measured forces rather than only from a formula.

The evidence suggests a maximum static friction of about 35 N and kinetic friction of about 28 N under these conditions. It would be wrong to say friction was always 35 N before motion: at a 10 N pull, the measured static friction was only 10 N.

Check your reasoning

If the crate is still at rest under a 25 N horizontal pull, what friction force acts? What changes once it slides?

Show worked solution

While stationary, static friction balances the 25 N pull with 25 N in the opposite direction. Once sliding, the measured kinetic friction is about 28 N for this experiment. Whether the crate accelerates then depends on the applied force minus the sliding friction.

SECTION 05

Read the forces in a free-body diagram

Consider a crate being pulled right across a level floor. Four forces act on it: weight downward, normal force upward, applied pull right, and friction left. The force arrows belong on the crate's free-body diagram because each represents an interaction acting on the crate.

First solve the vertical balance to find N. Next use the correct friction equation. Finally subtract friction from the horizontal pull to calculate net force. If the forces balance, acceleration is zero. If they do not, the crate accelerates in the direction of the net force.

SECTION 06

Friction on a ramp

A ramp changes the direction of the normal force. For an object on a slope angled θ above horizontal, gravity can be separated into two components: mg sin θ down the ramp and mg cos θ into the ramp. The normal force is perpendicular to the ramp, not straight upward.

Worked example · Will a block slide?

A 10 kg block rests on a 20° ramp. The coefficient of static friction is 0.50. Assume no other applied forces.

  1. Weight: mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N.
  2. Normal force: N = 98 cos(20°) ≈ 92.1 N.
  3. Downhill gravity component: 98 sin(20°) ≈ 33.5 N.
  4. Maximum static friction: 0.50 × 92.1 ≈ 46.1 N.
  5. Because 33.5 N is less than 46.1 N, static friction can hold the block. It remains at rest, with actual friction about 33.5 N up the ramp.
SECTION 07

Engineering choices: grip, wear, and heat

Designers sometimes want more friction. A tire must grip pavement to accelerate, turn, and brake. Shoe soles and conveyor belts also rely on friction. Engineers choose materials, tread patterns, and surface treatments to provide the required grip under expected conditions.

In other machines, friction is unwanted. Bearings help rotating shafts move with less resistance, while lubricants reduce direct contact between surfaces. Less friction can mean lower operating temperature, less wear, and improved energy efficiency. Engineers must also consider whether a lubricant could reduce traction where grip is needed.

The coefficient-of-friction model is a useful starting point, but real friction can change with temperature, moisture, surface contamination, speed, and material condition. Engineering decisions should therefore be checked through testing rather than relying on a single coefficient for every situation.

SECTION 08

Avoid these common mistakes

One common error is using fs = μsN for every stationary object. That formula gives the maximum static friction, not necessarily the actual value. If a 30 N push is balanced by static friction, the actual friction is 30 N even when the maximum is 100 N.

Another mistake is assuming friction always acts left or always opposes velocity. Its direction depends on how the two surfaces slide—or would tend to slide—relative to each other. Also, never calculate friction before finding the correct normal force. On an incline or with an angled pull, N may be different from mg.

Finally, remember that the coefficients μs and μk are dimensionless. Friction and normal force are measured in newtons, while acceleration is measured in m/s².

INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Independent engineering practice

Use g = 9.8 m/s². For each problem, identify the forces and choose the static or kinetic friction model before calculating.

A · Maximum static friction

A 15 kg crate rests on a level floor with μs = 0.30. Find the maximum static friction.

Show solution

N = 15 × 9.8 = 147 N. Maximum static friction = 0.30 × 147 = 44.1 N.

B · Actual static friction

The same crate is pushed with 25 N and remains at rest. How much friction acts on it?

Show solution

Because 25 N is below the 44.1 N maximum, actual static friction is 25 N opposite the push.

C · Sliding acceleration

A 10 kg crate slides right on a level floor with μk = 0.20. A 50 N horizontal push acts right. Find acceleration.

Show solution

N = 98 N. Kinetic friction = 0.20 × 98 = 19.6 N left. Net force = 50 − 19.6 = 30.4 N right. Acceleration = 30.4/10 = 3.04 m/s² right.

D · Angled upward pull

A 20 kg crate stays on a level floor while a rope pulls upward with a vertical component of 36 N. Find the normal force.

Show solution

N + 36 − 196 = 0, so N = 160 N.

E · Sliding down a ramp

A 5 kg block slides down a 30° ramp with μk = 0.20. Find its acceleration along the ramp.

Show solution

Down-ramp gravity = 49 sin(30°) = 24.5 N. Normal = 49 cos(30°) ≈ 42.4 N. Kinetic friction ≈ 8.49 N up-ramp. Net force ≈ 16.01 N down-ramp. Acceleration ≈ 3.20 m/s² down the ramp.

F · Explain a mistake

A student calculates static friction as 80 N because μsN = 80 N, even though a 20 N push leaves the crate motionless. What is wrong?

Show explanation

80 N is the maximum possible static friction. The actual static friction is only 20 N to balance the push.

VOCABULARY & GLOSSARY

Vocabulary and glossary

Friction

A contact force that resists relative sliding or the tendency to slide between surfaces.

Static friction

Friction between surfaces that are not sliding relative to each other; its magnitude adjusts up to a maximum.

Kinetic friction

Friction between surfaces that are sliding relative to each other.

Normal force

The contact force perpendicular to a surface.

Coefficient of friction, μ

A dimensionless value used in the simplified model of friction between surfaces.

Equilibrium

A condition of zero net force and zero acceleration.

Contact force

A force arising from the interaction of objects at their contact surface.
